# False Stone

*Chapter Twenty-Seven — What the Hand Remembers*

---

The axe had not said a word in two hours, and Korroc was beginning to take it personally.

It was in his pack. It had been in his pack since the tower, jammed in head-first with the haft sticking up past his ear and the flap buckled down over it as far as the buckle would go, and it had screamed at him for a long breathless while in more voices than he could count — *there, there, she is THERE, what are you DOING* — and then it had stopped. Not tailed off. Stopped. The way a man stops talking to you across a table, and goes on sitting there, so you know.

Now he stood in the command room of Citadel Drezen with his torn arming coat on his back and his armor in a heap at his feet and a sulking dwarven battleaxe leaning against his spine, and on the other side of the long table a demon was answering questions.

He had his arms folded. He found he had had them folded for some time.

"What can you tell me about Xanthir Vang?"

Caleth. He had not sat down. None of them had, except Aravashnial, who was in his chair by the wall with his bitten leg up on its stool where somebody had put it. The room had been cleared down to the leadership and then — a little after — had grown by one, when the door opened on Horgus Gwerm, who had plainly been fetched out of somewhere warm by someone who would hear about it later. He had come three steps in, seen what was standing by the lamp, and stopped with his mouth a little open. He had not yet closed it. Korroc did not blame him.

"He is the current leader of the Ivory Sanctum," the succubus said.

Her voice was low and hoarse still, and she said it to Caleth and to nobody else. She had not sat either. She stood a little way from the table with her torn wing drawn in close and her bow held low at her side, empty, the string slack, and Korroc noticed — because it was the kind of thing he noticed — that nobody had asked her for it. Not Irabeth. Not Aron. Not anybody. Three hundred paladins in this citadel, and the demon had walked into the heart of it armed.

He decided he did not mind. He was not sure what that said about him.

"So does he lead the cultists?" said Caleth.

"No. That would be Hepzamirah."

"Tell me about Hepzamirah."

"She is the daughter of Baphomet."

Anevia, perched on the edge of the table, let out a breath through her teeth. Caleth's face did not change.

"So we have another demigod running around."

"Yes." The succubus looked at the lamp. "But she is in the Abyss now. Harvesting Nahyndrian crystals. To make elixirs." A small pause. "Her absence is why Vang has the Sanctum."

*Elixirs.*

Korroc had not thought about the word in weeks, and it came back to him whole: a council room in this citadel, and a dwarf with washed hands sitting very straight in a chair, telling them about his brother on a cot for three days with his muscles locked so hard the bones creaked. *He woke. He was stronger after.* Joran had said the vials came from the Ivory Sanctum. Korroc had not known, then, what an Ivory Sanctum was. He was not sure he knew now.

"How many elixirs have they made?" said Caleth.

"I don't know. Many."

"Is there somewhere we can stop them being made? Or delivered?"

"If you kill Vang," said the succubus, "and destroy the Ivory Sanctum, you will stop one of the places they go out from."

*One.* Korroc turned the word over and did not like the weight of it.

"Where is the Ivory Sanctum?"

"It is hidden in the Green Gate."

Caleth was already moving to the map. "What is the Green Gate? Show me."

She came to the table. Korroc watched Aron not step back from her, and watched what it cost him, and thought better of the man for both. She leaned over the great spread sheets with their weights at the corners — Drezen in the middle, inked and re-inked, the Gray Road a long pale scar across the west, the fane's little mark — and her finger went out past all of it, down and to the left, to the southwest of the citadel, and stopped on a place where the mapmaker had drawn nothing at all.

"There," she said. "It is hidden by a veil. Maybe two."

"So if it is hidden," said Caleth, "how can I get there?"

"I can lead you there."

"And what if I don't trust you?"

She straightened, and shrugged. It was a very small shrug.

"That is up to you."

Nageru stepped forward.

He had been standing at the end of the table all this while with his hands open at his sides and the blood gone dark and stiff on his torn wraps, and he had not said a word since *we will go.* Now he said, quietly, "My friend. Think about this. She has been touched by Desna herself."

Caleth turned round. "And you think we can trust her because of that?"

"I do." Nageru nodded once. "She is not what she used to be. That is obvious."

Korroc watched the half-elf look at the monk for a long moment, and then back at the succubus, and frown. It was not his thinking frown. Korroc had known Caleth Aranor a year and had learned to tell his frowns apart the way you learn the different sounds a forge makes; that one was the one he wore when a sum came out and he did not like what it came to.

"Besides this veil," Caleth said, "what defenses does the Sanctum have?"

"Jerribeth." She said the name flatly. "Templars. A basilisk at the front door. Followers of Baphomet. Blackfire adepts." Another of the small shrugs. "It is a fortress."

"How many troops did he have there when you were last there?"

"Honestly?" She looked at the map, not at him. "Only a handful. But they were formidable."

"Tell me about the Blackfire adepts."

"They are another faction in the army. That is all."

"It sounds as though they are casters. Arcane? Divine?"

"They are what Vang is." She hesitated. "They are dangerous. Not as dangerous as him."

"What do you mean? What *is* Vang?"

"He is their leader. He is —" and she stopped, and Korroc saw a thing he had not seen on her yet, in the tower or on the floor of the fold or anywhere: she was at a loss. She stood there with her mouth a little open and her hands empty, looking for a word, and there was not one. "He is not a single thing," she said at last. "Not any more."

"I understand he is a worm that walks," said Caleth. "What does that mean? What are his weaknesses?"

"I don't know. I have never met him. I would prefer to keep it that way." She lifted her chin. "But if I must, to help you, I will. He wants nothing more than to catch me and turn me back to what I was. That is what Jaruunicka kept telling me, while she was trying to coax me out."

"Jaruunicka is the hag?"

She nodded.

*That's a name for her, then,* Korroc thought. He thought of the voice in the court of the ruined fortress, low and warm and wheedling, *there now, come out, nobody is angry.* He was glad Thane had put knives in her. He did not often let himself be glad of a thing like that, and he let himself, a little, now.

"Has Vang drunk one of the elixirs?"

"I don't know."

Caleth nodded. He looked round the room at the three of them.

Nageru stood as still as a post. Thane, by the window, gave one short nod. Korroc shrugged. He did not know what else there was to ask; the half-elf had asked it all, and asked it better than Korroc would have, and in the right order besides.

He looked, while he was shrugging, at Aravashnial.

He did not quite know why. Habit, maybe. Three nights ago at the fane he had looked up from the fire and found Nageru's eyes already on the elf, and his own had gone there too, and the two of them had looked at each other over it and said nothing. He had said nothing about it since. He did not know what there was to say.

The elf sat in his chair with his leg up and his hands folded on his knee, and watched the map, and the woman leaning over it, and Caleth's finger on the empty place to the southwest. His face did nothing. Nothing at the name of the Sanctum. Nothing at Vang's. Nothing at the place on the map. He might have been listening to a report on the state of the granaries.

Korroc looked away again before anyone could catch him at it, and found that he did not feel any better for having looked.

---

"How will you get there?" said Irabeth.

It was the first thing she had said in some while. She had stood at the head of the table through all of it with her hands flat on the wood, and Korroc had watched her face do sums the whole time — three hundred souls, and walls short of bolts, and a demon in her command room under a half-elf's protection — and come up every time with a number she did not like.

Caleth bent over the map. "I think we should fold back to the fortress where we found her," he said, "and go from there." He frowned. "And speaking of which. The Bell of Mercy."

Irabeth squinted at him.

"A silver bell," Caleth said. "Very old, and very holy, and it has strong protections against evil laid into it. It is still in the tower. We could not move it. We should decide what to do about it — find a way to bring it here, or rebuild the place it hangs and hold it there." He straightened. "I leave that to you. Whatever you think is the best move for Drezen."

Irabeth nodded slowly, and the look on her face said very plainly that she had not the first idea.

"I will think about it," she said. She looked up, round the room. "It's late. You've only just got back. We'll talk this through in the morning when you've all slept." Her eyes went to the succubus and stayed there. "For tonight I have to work out what to do with her."

"Give her a room," said Thane, from the window. "And see she's left alone."

Irabeth frowned at him. Then she looked at Sosiel, who had not moved from the fire, and something passed between them that Korroc could not read and did not try to.

"This meeting is dismissed," she said. "I suggest you all get some rest."

Sosiel came forward. He did it without hurrying, and without any show of courage, which Korroc thought was the most courageous way there was to do it. He stopped an arm's length from the succubus, as Caleth had in the tower, and said something too low for Korroc to catch, and she looked at him a long moment and then nodded. They went out together, the young priest and the demon, and the door closed on them, and in the quiet after it Horgus Gwerm finally shut his mouth.

People began to move. Aron went to Irabeth's elbow. Anevia slid off the table and stretched until her back cracked.

Korroc bent to gather his armor.

Across the room Caleth had gone to the elf's chair. Korroc was not trying to listen; he was trying to get a pauldron, a breastplate, a backplate and two greaves into his arms at once without dropping any of them, which took most of his attention. But the room was quiet and he heard some of it anyway.

"— a working that conjures mounts for a whole company. So we need not take horses."

Aravashnial looked up at him. He thought about it.

"I have it," he said. "Not in my head. In my archives. A scroll."

"Could I copy it into my book? Tonight?"

"You may have it. It is in the tower." The elf put his hands on the arms of his chair. "I will find it for you."

"Thank you."

Korroc got the backplate seated under his arm, and the breastplate on top of it, and the hammer and anvil gleamed up at him off the gold in the lamplight, unmarked, as though a spider the size of a house had never come near it. He grunted at it.

Then he went out, and down, and did not go to bed.

---

The Purity Forge never went fully out. That was one of the things he had come to love about it.

It burned low at night, banked, a long red breathing in the dark at the bottom of the pit, and the heat came up the stair to meet you three flights before you reached it. Korroc came down into it with his armor clanking in his arms and the axe silent on his back and felt it go into him the way warm water goes into cold hands. His shoulders came down half an inch. He had not known they were up.

Joran Vhane was at the far anvil.

He was alone. There had been a guard on the stair in the first weeks, and then not always, and then not at all; these days Joran was not to leave the citadel and not to be in certain places, and otherwise he was let be, and he spent most of his waking hours down here, where nobody but the four of them ever came. He had a pair of tongs in one hand and a horseshoe in the other, of all things, and he was looking at the shoe with the sour concentration of a master who has been given apprentice work and intends to do it better than it has ever been done.

He looked up as Korroc came down the last steps. His eyes went to Korroc's face, and then to Korroc's arms, and his eyebrows rose.

"What happened?"

Korroc dropped the whole armful on the bench. It went down with a crash that rang round the pit.

"Demons suck," he said.

Joran's eyebrows went up further.

He set down his tongs and came over — slowly, the way he did everything that was not at an anvil — and looked at the pile, and then picked up the backplate and turned it over in his hands, and Korroc watched him read it the way he would have read it himself. The plate. The edges. The hinges. The torn black stubs of leather where every strap had been, and the empty rivet holes, and the buckles that were not there.

"Damn," said Joran. "Stripped the straps right off."

"Aye."

"Clean." He ran a thumb over the plate. "Not a mark on the metal."

"That's what I said."

"What did it?"

"Spider." Korroc pulled the breastplate toward him. "Size of a siege tower. Hooks on the front legs." He made a shape with his hand, curling, and Joran looked at it and nodded slowly, a smith being told what a tool had been used for. "Went in at the straps. Every one. Like a man skinning a rabbit."

He had said that already tonight, he realized. He did not care. It was the right shape for it.

He found the leather, a good thick hide he had been keeping back for something, and laid it out, and took up his knife. He did not ask. He had not meant to ask. And when he looked up, a little later, from marking the first strap against the old holes, Joran was standing at his elbow with an awl in his hand and a pile of new buckles he had got from somewhere, already sorted by size.

Korroc looked at the buckles. He looked at Joran.

Joran looked at the backplate.

Korroc moved over.

They did not talk much. There was not much to say, and the work did not want it. Joran cut. Korroc fitted. Joran punched the holes and Korroc set the rivets and Joran held the plate steady on the horn while he did it, and once, when Korroc reached across without looking for the small hammer, it was already in his hand, and he did not know when Joran had put it there.

He thought about that afterward. He thought about it a long while.

Behind him, against the wall where he had set it down, the pack with the axe in it said nothing at all.

It took a couple of hours. At the end of it every strap was new and every buckle sound, and Korroc stood in the red light of the banked forge and put the armor on — breastplate, back, shoulders, the greaves, cinching each strap as he went and testing it with his weight — and it settled onto him the way it always had, heavy and exact, as though it had only stepped out for a moment and was glad to be back.

He put his hand flat over the hammer and anvil on his chest. He had more or less stopped doing that. He did it now.

Then he turned round to Joran and held out his hand.

Joran looked at it.

He looked at it for a long time, long enough that Korroc began to feel it hanging there in the air between them like a question nobody had asked. Then Joran wiped his own palm down his apron — slowly, front and back — and took it.

His grip was hard and dry, a smith's grip, all callus. Korroc held it a moment. He did not say anything. There was a great deal he might have said, and none of it would have been any better than the hand.

"Thank you," he said, at last, because something had to be.

Joran let go. He went back to his anvil. He picked up his tongs, and the horseshoe, and looked at the shoe.

"Get some sleep," he said, without looking up. "You look like a dropped crucible."

Korroc laughed. It came out of him startled, a short bark that went up the pit and came back down, and he picked up the pack with the silent axe in it and went up the stair still grinning, and did not stop grinning until the second landing.

---

The whispers started at breakfast.

He heard them before he understood them — the low run of a room with something in it, the way a hall goes when there has been a death or a fight or a promotion nobody expected. Two sergeants at the end of a bench, heads together. A pair of young paladins in the corridor outside the garrison hall who stopped talking when he came by and started again behind him. A clerk with a stack of ledgers, saying to another clerk, *in the command room, I heard, with its wings out —*

*A demon. They brought a demon in.*

Korroc ate his porridge and said nothing, and listened.

It was not as bad as it might have been. That surprised him. He had heard soldiers frightened before, and soldiers angry, and this was neither, quite. It was something in between — men turning a thing over to see which side was up. *Under the half-elf's protection, he said. Said if she's touched they'll deal with him. And the dwarves backed him. All four of them.* And underneath, more than once, the thing Thane had said in the tower: they would not fight the four of them. Not over this. Not over anything, maybe, after the north court and the Sword and the night the Banner went up.

He did not know whether that made him proud or uneasy. Both, he decided. Mostly uneasy.

Caleth came through the hall once, on his way somewhere with a scroll case under his arm and his face gray with not having slept enough, and Korroc watched him stop at the end of a bench where two men had their heads together, and look at them. That was all. He did not say a word. He only looked, the way Caleth looked at a wall carving he had decided was badly done, and both men discovered that they had urgent business with their porridge.

He did it twice more before he reached the door.

Korroc grinned into his bowl.

And then, a little before midmorning, crossing the upper court on the way to the forge, he saw her.

Sosiel was walking her across the far side of the yard, toward the old chapel, with nobody near them. She looked better. That was the first thing he saw. Somebody had washed the dried blood off her arm and bound it, clean white linen from shoulder to wrist, and the torn wing had been seen to — stitched, or splinted, or something he did not have the word for, a neat dressing along the edge where the arrow had gone through. She walked easier. She had the bow over her shoulder and a quiver at her hip, and nobody had taken either.

There were men on the walls. A dozen, maybe. Korroc watched every one of them stop what they were doing and look down.

Nobody said anything. Nobody moved.

The succubus did not look up at them. She walked beside the priest across the court with her eyes on the stones and her wings drawn in tight, the way a woman walks past a crowd she knows is talking about her, and went in at the chapel door, and was gone.

On the walls the men went slowly back to their work. Korroc stood in the court a moment longer.

*Sosiel took her to a chapel,* he thought. *To pray, or to let her be somewhere she'll not be bothered.* He did not know which. He was not sure it mattered.

He went on down to the forge.

---

Thane and Nageru were there before him.

That was unusual enough that he stopped on the stair. Thane, in the forge at midmorning on a day with no work in it — that was not so strange; Thane had been half-living down here since he made the punch dagger. But Nageru was sitting on the cold end of the long bench with his legs folded under him and his hands on his knees, and Thane was leaning against the anvil beside him with his arms crossed, and the two of them had the look of men who had been talking about something and stopped when they heard his boots.

"What?" said Korroc.

"Sit down, cousin," said Thane.

Korroc looked at him. He sat down.

For a while nobody said anything. Joran was at the far end of the pit, at the quench, and did not look round. The forge breathed.

Then Nageru said, "Since the wardstone."

Korroc waited.

"You have felt it," Nageru said. It was not a question. "All of us have. The thing it left. The — " he moved one hand, a small turning, as though he were feeling for the edge of a cloth in the dark — "the room it opened. We have each been finding out how far down it goes."

"Aye," said Korroc slowly.

He had. He had been finding out for a year. Fire in a library, and fire on a stair, and a healing that went across fifteen feet of empty air and landed as if he had laid his hand on the man. The bond that thickened when he wanted it to. Things he had not known he could do until they answered. *How far down it goes.* That was the right way to say it. He would not have found it himself.

"And lately," Nageru said, "I have felt something else."

Thane said nothing. Korroc looked at him and saw that his cousin's hand had gone to his belt, to the short heavy hilt there that crossed behind the knuckles — the punch dagger, his own make — and was resting on it. Not gripping. Resting.

And Korroc understood. He understood before anyone said another word, and it went through him like a bell going through a tower.

He had been feeling it for weeks. He had not put a name to it, because there had not been time, and because it was the kind of thing that sounded foolish when you said it out loud — a smith's fancy, a man getting soft about his tools. But it was there. It had been there since before the Gray Road, maybe since before the fane. When he took up his shield in the morning, it was — not heavier. Not warmer, exactly. *Readier.* The way a piece of iron goes in the fire just before it takes the color you want, when you have not seen the change yet but your hand already knows, and is already reaching for the tongs.

"My shield," he said.

He heard himself say it and was startled by how sure he sounded.

Nageru nodded as though that were the answer he had expected. He reached up, into the neck of his torn wraps, and drew something out on a cord — a small round of worked metal, plain, dull, that Korroc had seen a hundred times and never once thought about; it lived under the monk's tunic and came out of it about as often as the monk complained. It lay in his palm. He looked down at it.

"My amulet," he said.

"The dagger," said Thane. "The one I made."

They sat with that a while.

"It is in them," Nageru said at last. "The same thing that is in us. I think it has been going into them, a little at a time, because we carry them so close. The way warmth goes into a stone you keep in your hand." He closed his fingers over the amulet. "And I think, if we turned toward it — if we went to meet it, instead of waiting for it — we could make the bond stronger. Bring out what is already in them."

"Bring out," said Korroc.

"Yes."

"Not put in."

Nageru looked at him. "No," he said. "Not put in."

Korroc sat back against the wall.

*You cannot make a thing be what it is. You can only get out of the way of it, and let it say so.*

He had learned that in a ruined chapel two floors above this one, with his own armor changing its mark on his chest without asking. He had been learning it every day since. At this anvil, with Joran. In the tower last night, with a demon who had sat in holy ground and not burned. He had thought it was a thing about people. It had not occurred to him that it might be a thing about shields.

"Aye," he said. "Let's have a look at it."

Then he frowned, and looked at the stair.

"What about the lad?"

"Busy," said Thane. "He's got his nose in a scroll of the elf's. Horses, I think."

"He'd want to know."

"Perhaps," said Nageru. He turned the amulet over in his palm. "But think what he carries."

Korroc thought. He did not have to think long. He had stood at this very bench and watched Radiance flare up bright enough to cut every shadow in the pit into black paper, and throw the half-elf's own working back across the room at him, and lie there afterward looking innocent. A weapon older than all of them put together. It had been a great thing before Caleth ever laid a hand on it. It had *chosen* him; that was the story, and Korroc had never once heard the half-elf tell it any other way.

"It was already something," he said slowly. "Before he had it."

"Yes," said Nageru. "Ours were only ours. A shield. A dagger. This." He closed his fingers. "What is in them came from us. Radiance did not need him to wake it." He was quiet a moment. "Perhaps with him it goes the other way."

Thane grunted. It was the grunt that meant he had no argument with that and did not intend to say so.

"We'll tell him after," said Korroc. "He can be put out about it then."

"He will be," said Thane.

"Aye. He will."

---

They talked for half an hour about how.

Thane wanted to know what it would cost, which was Thane. Korroc wanted to know whether it would hurt the shield, which he did not say aloud and Thane heard anyway. Nageru answered what he could, which was some of it, and said *I do not know* to the rest, plainly, without embarrassment, which was more than Caleth had ever managed on a forge floor.

"My order has a way of sitting," the monk said. "For the long meditations. It is not a prayer, exactly. It is a way of — paying attention. All the way down." He looked at the two of them. "I can show you. You need not believe anything. You need only sit."

"I can sit," said Korroc.

"For most of a day?"

Korroc looked at the monk. He looked at his cousin. Thane looked back at him with an expression of the most perfect neutrality, which in Thane meant that he was trying very hard not to laugh.

"I can sit," said Korroc again, with dignity.

Caleth put his head round the bottom of the stair while they were still at it.

He looked worse than he had at breakfast, and he had looked bad enough then; there was ink on two of his fingers and a hard-pressed look round the eyes that Korroc had learned meant he had been up most of the night copying something and had not yet admitted it. He had his coat on and Radiance on his back.

"I am going to the bell," he said. "Irabeth wants it brought here, if it can be done. She is sending a party to study it and start the work." He ticked them off on his inky fingers. "Soldiers. Aron. Anevia. Aravashnial. One of her wizards, who can do my part of it after today. People to make a camp. Engineers, to look at the tower and see what can be rebuilt. Twenty, all told."

"Twenty," said Thane.

"I can take four at a time." Caleth's mouth went thin. "It is better done today than tomorrow. Today it only costs me today."

"That's ten folds," said Korroc. "There and back."

"Yes," said Caleth. "I can count."

He looked at the three of them then — at Nageru on the bench with the amulet out on its cord, and Thane with his hand on his dagger, and Korroc sitting against the wall with his shield across his knees — and his eyebrows went up, very slightly.

"What are you doing?"

"Sitting," said Korroc.

Caleth looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded, as though that made a kind of sense he did not have the time to examine, and went back up the stair.

---

So they sat.

Nageru put them where he wanted them. Not on the bench. On the floor of the pit, on the warm stone near the forge, in a ring, close enough that their knees nearly touched. He had them each lay the thing in front of him — the shield on Korroc's knees, its rim on the floor; the dagger bare across Thane's open hands; the amulet in the hollow of Nageru's own palm — and then he told them to breathe, and to stop listening to the forge, and then to start listening to it again, and Korroc did all of it without the first idea what he was doing and with every intention of doing it right.

It was harder than it sounded.

His knees hurt within the hour. His back hurt after that. He kept thinking about things — about the straps, and whether he had set the third rivet on the left greave a hair too close to the edge; about the succubus walking across the court with her eyes on the stones; about Joran's hand, wiped front and back on his apron before he took Korroc's — and every time he thought about one of them Nageru seemed to know it, without opening his eyes, and said, very quietly, *there is always another breath,* and Korroc came back.

And then, somewhere in the second hour, he stopped coming back, because he was not leaving any more.

It was like the forge. That was the only way he would ever be able to say it. It was like the part of the work that had no story in it — the long hour of heat and draw and check and heat again, the hour that is only the difference between a thing that is nearly right and a thing that is right. He had never been able to explain that hour to anybody who did not already know it. He did not need to explain it now. He was in it.

The shield lay on his knees.

He knew it. He had known it a year — taken it up borrowed in the Gray Garrison, carried a goddess's sun on it that was not his to carry, and knelt in mud in a dream of fire while that goddess herself put the Father's mark on it with her own hand, as a courtesy, and he had wept, and thanked her, and never once since then gone into a fight without it. He knew its weight to the ounce. He knew every dent in it, and which fight had put each one there, and the place on the strap where the leather had worn pale from his hand.

He had never known it like this.

There was a thing in it. Not a spirit — not a voice, not like the axe. Nothing that would speak or want. A *grain.* That was nearer. The way good steel has a grain in it, running the way the smith drew it out, and if you work with the grain the piece goes where you want and if you work against it the piece fights you all the way and cracks in the end. He had felt grain in iron his whole life. He had never felt it in a *thing.* In what a thing was for.

And it was the same grain as the one in him.

The wardstone's. The fire that had come down into four of them in a room above Kenabres and had been working its way out ever since, through his hands and his chest and the bond and the gold. It had been going into the shield all this while, the way Nageru said — a little at a time, from his arm, from a year of holding it between himself and whatever was coming. It had soaked in. It was in the wood and the iron and the old enamel under the Father's hammer, and it was *waiting*, and it had been waiting for him to notice.

He noticed.

He did not do anything. That was the strange part, after. He did not pray, and he did not push, and he did not lay any working on it the way he laid the bond on Thane. He only turned toward it — paid attention, all the way down, the way the monk had said — and the thing in the shield turned toward him, and the two of them met somewhere in the middle, like the two halves of a weld coming together under the heat, and *took.*

He felt it take.

He opened his eyes.

The forge was the same. The light was different — later; the long red of afternoon coming down the stair. Thane sat across from him with the punch dagger in his open hands and his eyes still shut and a look on his face Korroc had seen on it perhaps twice in his life. Nageru was watching them both, with the amulet closed in his fist.

And at the far end of the pit, on an upturned bucket by the quench, Joran Vhane sat with his hands hanging between his knees and his horseshoe forgotten on the floor beside him, looking at the three of them.

Korroc did not know how long he had been sitting there. Joran saw him see, and did not look away, and did not get up. He only went on looking, with an expression Korroc could not read — not quite the face of a man watching something he did not understand. More the face of a man watching something he had once understood, a long time ago, and had put down somewhere, and could not now remember where.

Then he got up and picked up the horseshoe and went back to his anvil.

---

It was in the third hour, or the fourth, that the thread to Aravashnial went.

He had almost forgotten it was there. It had been on the elf since the day he walked home — since Korroc had sat on the edge of the infirmary cot with his gauntlets off and one hand on a chest that weighed almost nothing and laid it there, the same small warm line he kept on all of them, so that if the elf began to slip in the night Korroc would know it before the elf did. It had told him a great deal since. It had told him, only last night, among the stone trees of a petrified forest, that something was biting the man. It had shouted it.

It did not shout now. It went thin.

He felt it go thin all at once, the way a cord goes when the other end is carried away from you very fast — and then thinner — and then there was nothing on the other end of it at all.

His eyes came open. His hand had gone flat on the floor.

*Dead* — and then, at once, before the word had finished arriving: *No. Far.* It did not feel like dying. He knew what dying felt like down the bond; he had felt it go out of men, and it went out *slow*, like heat leaving iron, and it pulled at you on its way. This had not pulled. This had simply been *carried off.* As if someone had picked the elf up and set him down somewhere a very long way from here, past the length of any thread Korroc had.

Which was exactly what had happened, he realized. Caleth's fourth fold, or his fifth. Soldiers, and engineers, and Aron and Anevia, and the elf, to a ruined fortress at the far end of the Wound.

He let out his breath.

He did not like it. He sat there with the shield across his knees and the place in his chest where the thread had been gone empty and cold, and he did not like it at all. He had not known, until it was gone, how much of his attention it had been taking. How much of the time, these past days, some part of him had been sitting beside that cot.

"Brother," said Nageru quietly.

"It's nothing," said Korroc. "It's the elf. He's gone to the bell." He shut his eyes again. "I'll have him back when we're close."

There is always another breath. He took it.

---

When they stopped, it was nearly evening, and something had changed.

None of them could have said what. Korroc picked up his shield and stood, and his knees told him exactly what they thought of him, and the shield in his hand was the same shield — same weight, same dent, same pale worn place on the strap. It did not glow. It did not hum. If Rennick had come down the stair just then he would have seen nothing on it to ask about.

But it was *different*, and Korroc knew it the way he knew which way the grain ran in a bar of iron, and he could see on Thane's face that Thane knew it about his dagger, and on Nageru's that Nageru knew. A thing had been nearly right, and now it was right. And it would not be the last time. That was the other thing he knew, standing there. He could feel the shape of it, the way you feel the shape of a piece you are going to come back to: there was more in the shield than had come out today. There would be other days for it. Other sittings. It would take what he gave it and give back more, a little at a time, the way all good work did.

Work was never finished. That had always been the best thing about it.

He looked at Thane. Thane was turning the punch dagger in his hand, slowly, in the red light, with a little furrow between his brows that Korroc had not seen on him since they were boys.

"Well?" said Korroc.

Thane looked up.

"Aye," he said. Only that. And put the dagger away.

---

Caleth came back after dark.

They were in the garrison hall by then, the three of them, with bread and a pot of something brown, and he came in through the far door and walked the length of the room to their table and sat down on the end of the bench as though somebody had cut his strings. He did not take off his coat. He put both elbows on the table and his face in his hands and stayed like that a while.

Korroc pushed the pot toward him.

"Twenty?" he said.

"Twenty." Caleth did not lift his head. "Ten folds. I have done it. Aron has a camp going inside the fortress wall, and Anevia is already arguing with the engineers, and the wizard has seen it now and can do the carrying from here, and Aravashnial is sitting at the foot of the tower with his leg out, telling everyone who comes near him that the bell is not to be touched." He breathed out. "He was quite firm."

"How's the leg?"

"He says it is fine. I think it is fine." Caleth lifted his head at last and looked at the pot, and at the bread, and seemed to come to some decision about them. He took a piece of the bread. "I copied the working last night. Into my book. The one for the horses." He looked at Korroc. "We need not take real ones tomorrow."

Korroc considered this.

"These ones," he said. "The ones you make. Do they bite?"

"I do not believe so."

"Do they scream going down a riverbank?"

"I will be extremely surprised."

"Hm," said Korroc, and found that he was a little less unhappy about the morning than he had been.

Caleth ate his bread. Halfway through it he looked up and down the three of them — properly, the way he looked at things, as though he had just noticed something on them he had walked past earlier — and Korroc saw his eyes go to the shield where it leaned on the bench beside him, and to Thane's belt, and to the cord at Nageru's neck. He frowned, very slightly. He opened his mouth.

Then he shut it again, and went back to the bread. Whatever it was, he was too tired to ask.

Korroc liked him for that. He could not have said why.

---

He woke in the dark and did not know what had woken him.

The room was black. Thane was a slow breathing shape on the next cot, and past him Nageru, and past him the empty cot that should have been Caleth's. The half-elf had taken his books and his lamp to the little room two doors down, as he had the night before, to work late, and had not come back from it. The citadel was quiet. Somewhere a long way off a sentry called the hour on the wall, and another answered.

Korroc lay still and listened, and there was nothing to hear.

It was not in his ears. It was in his chest.

The thread. Not the elf's — that was still gone, empty, a cold place. The other ones. Thane's, close and steady. Nageru's, which was always the quietest of them, like a banked fire. And Caleth's.

Something was coming down Caleth's.

It was not a wound. He knew wounds; they came down the bond hot and sharp and with a shape to them, a cut or a crush or a burn, and he took his share and the man on the other end bled less for it. This was not that. This had no shape. It was only *heat* — a long dull fierce heat, rising, from somewhere on the far end of the line, rising and rising like a forge with the bellows going and nobody at the anvil — and under the heat, very faint, a thing he could only have called *fear*, if fear were a thing that could come down a thread, and he had not known until that moment that it could.

It went on. He lay there with his hand flat on his own chest, over his own heart, the way a man holds his hand over a pipe to feel the water go through it.

He thought of a pale spiral on a man's left shoulder blade, three turns, a hand's breadth across, in the gray light at Sesker's Gully. He thought of Caleth saying *it seems as if Vang is finding me through it.* He did not like that he thought of it.

Then, all at once, it stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. As if a door had slammed somewhere at the far end of the line. And after it there was only the ordinary small warm thread of the half-elf, two doors down, alive, and nothing coming along it at all.

Korroc lay in the dark a long time.

He thought about getting up. He thought about going two doors down and knocking, and he got as far as putting one bare foot on the cold floor. Then he thought about Caleth's face, opening the door, and what Caleth's face would do when it saw who was there and understood why — the half-elf who had shown them the mark on his back as though it had cost him a pint of blood to do it, and then put his shirt back on and not spoken of it again.

He put his foot back under the blanket.

*In the morning,* he thought. *If he wants it said, he'll say it.*

He did not sleep again for a long while.

---

In the morning Caleth looked as though he had been dragged backward through a hedge, set on fire, and put out with a bucket.

He was at the long table in the command room before any of them, with his coat buttoned to the throat and his spellbook open and his hand flat on the page, not reading it. He looked up when they came in and nodded to each of them in turn, as he always did. His eyes were very steady. There were shadows under them like thumbprints in clay.

Korroc sat down across from him.

"Sleep?" he said.

Caleth looked at him for a moment.

"Some," he said.

Korroc held his eyes. He let the moment go on a little longer than it had to, long enough that the half-elf would know it had been noticed, and that it was being let be on purpose. Then he nodded, and reached for the bread.

Caleth looked down at his book. After a while his hand moved on the page, as if he had only now remembered what it was there for.

---

Irabeth came in a little after, and Korroc stood up and unbuckled his pack.

Afterward it would feel as though the axe had been taken from him, and he had to keep reminding himself that it had not. It was his own hands that drew Fiendsplitter out, head first, the way it had gone in, and his own hands that laid it across the end of the command-room table in front of the commander of Drezen.

It said nothing.

He had half thought it would. It had been silent two days — since the tower, since he had jammed it into the dark and told it to be quiet — and it had stayed silent through the forge and the sitting and the night, and he had half thought that *this*, at least, would wake it. That it would know. That the moment his fingers closed on the haft it would start up in his head in all its voices at once, angry, offended, *what are you doing, where are you going, you cannot,* and he would have to stand here in front of Irabeth and be shouted at by an axe.

It did not. It lay on the table under the lamplight, long and dark and plain, the Father's hammer and anvil cut in the steel of its head, and it was as quiet as any axe.

Somehow that was worse.

"We talked it through," Korroc said. "Last night. Thane and me." He looked at the axe and not at her. "It's no good taking it. Not where we're going. Not with *her*."

Irabeth looked at the axe. Then at him.

"It wants her," Korroc said. "It wanted her in the tower so badly I had to stuff it in a sack. It'll want her every hour of every day we're riding, and every hour it'll be in my head telling me so, and I'll be a day's ride into the Wound with half my mind on a demon I've no wish to kill and an axe that can't be made to understand why." He shook his head. "It's a good axe. It's the Father's, whatever else it is. But I can't hear myself think with it."

"And so you're giving it to me," said Irabeth.

"Lending."

"Lending it to me." She looked at the axe for a long moment, and then up at him, and he saw that she was not entirely displeased. "What does it do?"

"Talks," said Korroc. "In your head. In Dwarven. Several of it." He saw her face and nodded. "Aye. And it knows demons. Smells them coming a long way off, before you'd ever see them. If anything comes at your walls while we're gone —" he paused — "you'll want to pick it up. It'll tell you where."

Irabeth considered that. He watched her weigh it — the drakes over the north range, the glabrezu through the courtyard wall that nobody had seen coming, a citadel short of bolts and short of soldiers — and saw her come out the other side of it with something that was almost gratitude, and was too proud to look like it.

"And otherwise?"

"Otherwise don't hold it by the haft," said Korroc. "Keep it somewhere it can see the door. And don't —" He stopped. He did not know how to say the next thing.

"Don't what?"

"Don't let it think it's been left," he said gruffly. "It hasn't. I'm coming back for it."

Irabeth looked at him. Across the table, he felt rather than saw Thane look at the ceiling.

"I'll see it's treated decent," Irabeth said.

He nodded. He put out his hand and laid it flat on the head of the axe, over the hammer and anvil, the way he had laid it on a hundred dead men's chests to give them the words. The steel was cold.

Nothing.

*All right,* he thought at it. *Sulk, then. You're owed it.*

He took his hand away.

---

Five went to the bell: the four of them, and her.

She came into the command room with Sosiel at her elbow just as Caleth stood up from the table. She was dressed for the road — the bow on her back, the quiver at her hip, the linen still white on her arm, a dark traveling cloak somebody had found her pulled close round her wings — and she stopped inside the door when she saw them all and looked from face to face, the way she had in the tower, as though she still could not quite make herself believe in any of them.

Then her eyes went down the table to the axe, lying there in the lamplight. And stayed on it.

Korroc watched her look at it. He watched her understand what it was, and what it was for, and that it was lying on a table and not on his back, and that it was because of her. He watched her not say anything.

She looked up at him.

He shrugged. "Couldn't hear myself think," he said.

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. It was not quite a smile. It was the place where one would go.

Caleth held out his hands.

---

The world folded.

It came back as cold, and wind, and the smell of fresh-cut timber and campfire smoke over old wet stone — and something else, something he felt before he had his feet properly under him, that made him put his hand flat on his chest in the middle of a fortress courtyard in front of twenty people.

*There you are.*

The thread to Aravashnial. Back. Small and warm and steady, as though it had never been anywhere, running off across the court to the foot of the tower where the elf sat on an upturned crate in the gray morning with his bad leg out in front of him on a second crate, a book in his lap, and a soldier standing near him trying not to look like he had been told to.

The elf looked up as they came out of the fold. His eyes went over the five of them, one after the next. They stopped on Korroc for a moment — on the hand on his chest — and something in the elf's face moved very slightly, as though he had felt something come back on his end too and did not know what it was.

Then he nodded, and went back to his book.

The fortress was different in daylight. Korroc had known it in the dark, by the sound of a roof and the stink of a hezrou and the crack of a worm's mouth on flagstones; now he could see it. The nave with its ribs of wall standing up out of the rubble. The tower, narrow and gray, with the open top where the bell hung, and the bell itself a small bright point of silver against the overcast. Tents in the court. A cookfire. Men with measuring cords at the tower foot, arguing, with Anevia in the middle of them saying something sharp. Two soldiers were dragging what was left of a dead drake out from under the fallen roof of the nave with ropes and a great deal of language, and he watched them for a moment with professional sympathy.

Aron Kir came across the court to meet them.

He had his side bound still under his coat and he walked a little carefully on it, and he stopped in front of the five of them and looked at each in turn, the way he looked at a wall he was about to tell somebody was going to fall down.

"Listen," he said.

They listened.

"When you need to rest," said Aron, "or when you're finished — whichever comes first — you come back *here.*" He pointed at the ground between his boots. "Not to Drezen. Here. You tell us what happened, or what's happening, before you go anywhere else." He looked at Caleth specifically, and then at Korroc, and then at Thane, as though he had a list and was going down it. "We need to be kept in it. Out here. We will not be forgotten about at the far end of the Wound while you four go off and do something I'll hear about in a month from a clerk."

Korroc nodded. They all nodded. Even the succubus nodded, a little after the rest, as though she were not sure she was included and had decided to be.

Aron looked at them a moment longer. Then he grunted and went back to his engineers.

Caleth stepped out into the open middle of the court, away from the tents. He took out his book and opened it and found the page with his inky finger, and stood there in the cold reading it over once more to himself, his lips moving. Then he closed the book and put it away and lifted his hands and began.

Korroc had seen a great deal of Caleth's magic in a year. Fire. Ice. The folds. Walls of force, and light, and that working in his voice that made demons do as they were told. He had never seen him make anything that *stood there afterward.* It took longer than he had expected — long enough that the soldiers at the drake stopped pulling and turned to watch — and at the end of it the air in front of the half-elf thickened and darkened and *drew itself up*, the way a cloud draws itself up into a shape if you watch it long enough, and there were four horses standing in the court.

They were gray, all four, a dark smoky gray that did not quite settle on any one shade. They were tall and very still. They did not toss their heads or stamp. Korroc walked up to the nearest and looked at it, and it looked back at him out of an eye with no white in it, perfectly calm, and he put his hand on its neck and felt warmth there, and muscle, and something under the muscle that was not quite a horse.

It did not smell of horse.

"Huh," said Korroc.

"One each," said Caleth. "Nageru, I assume —"

"I will walk," said Nageru.

"You will walk." Caleth swung up onto the nearest. He did it more neatly than usual; the thing stood like a rock for him. He gathered the reins, which were real enough to hold, and looked round at the rest of them, and then up at the sky, where a thin white sun was trying to get through. "We have about six hours with them. Then they're gone, and I can call them again in the morning. Let's not waste it."

Korroc put his foot in the stirrup. The horse did not move.

He hauled himself up onto its back. The horse did not move.

He settled himself into the saddle, armor and shield and all, with the grunting and the shifting and the small resentful noises that every horse he had ever sat on had found a reason to object to. The horse did not move. It did not even flick an ear.

He sat there a moment and thought about it.

"Caleth," he said.

"Yes?"

"I'll not say I like it." He patted the smoky neck once, gingerly. "But I'll say it's better than the last one."

Behind him, he heard Thane laugh.

---

They rode east out of the fortress gate, and then, as the morning went on, bending south.

The thread to the elf went thin by midmorning. He felt it go — not suddenly, this time, but slowly, a long fraying, mile by mile, as the horses' long smooth strides carried him out to the end of it — and by noon it was gone again. He put his hand on his chest when it went, and took it away, and said nothing.

He kept putting his hand behind him.

That was the thing he had not expected. He would be riding along, looking at nothing, at the long low ridges and the cracked brown ground and the succubus riding a little ahead of him on her own smoke-gray horse with her cloak drawn close — and his right hand would come up and back over his shoulder, of its own accord, looking for the haft. For the weight. For the cold steel that had ridden there since a crypt at Sesker's Gully. It was not there. His hand found his own shoulder and nothing else, and he would bring it down again and look at it, the way you look at a dog that has gone to the door for a man who is not coming home.

He had carried the thing a few weeks. A few weeks, and it had grown into him like a tooth.

*Couldn't hear myself think,* he had said, and it had been true.

He could hear himself think now. He was not sure he cared for it.

They met two lots of trouble on the road, and neither of them was much. The first was in the early afternoon: a little knot of demons, five or six, about the size of large dogs and all of them teeth, that came boiling out of a fold of broken ground beside the track as though they had been sitting in it waiting for exactly this. Nageru was among them before Korroc had his shield up. The horses did not shy, which Korroc thought was the most unnatural thing he had seen all day. He rode one down — it did not even turn — and caught a second with the hammer as it went for the succubus's stirrup, and felt the old honest shock of good steel meeting a skull all the way up his arm. The succubus put an arrow through a third at twenty paces without seeming to aim. Thane was off his horse and back on it with two of them dead behind him before Korroc had finished turning round.

The last one ran. Nobody chased it.

His hand went back over his shoulder, after, for the axe that would have been shouting about the one that got away.

The second was worse in its smell than its teeth — a single bloated thing that came lurching up out of a dry wash an hour later with too many arms, and stank of a midden, and went down under Nageru's fists and Korroc's hammer in about the time it took to curse it. Caleth did not even unsling Radiance. He sat his horse and watched with his hands on the reins and the look of a man calculating how much each fold today was going to cost him, and when it was over he said "Thank you," to nobody in particular, and rode on.

The horses went out at the end of the sixth hour, as Caleth had said they would.

They did not fade. Korroc had been braced for something — a slow going thin, a smoke going up — and instead his horse was simply not there, in the middle of a stride, and he was sitting on nothing with his feet in no stirrups, and then he was not sitting on anything at all. He came down on his back in the dust with his shield on top of him and his armor ringing like a dropped pot.

He lay there and looked at the sky.

Nobody laughed. He knew that was effort on all their parts. He was grateful for it.

"I did say six hours," said Caleth, from somewhere nearby, in a voice that was very nearly steady.

"You said *gone*," said Korroc, to the sky. "You never said *out from under.*" And he held up a hand, and Thane took it and hauled him to his feet without a word, and only Korroc was close enough to see his cousin's beard shaking.

---

They camped in a hollow under a low ridge, with the light going, and Thane cast the working for the night.

That was new. It had been Caleth's, always — on the long road north, and at the Gray Road a week ago — but Caleth sat down on a rock when they stopped and put his elbows on his knees and did not get up again, and after a moment Thane looked at him and then went into the middle of the camp and did it himself. He did it plainly, without fuss, with his lips moving and one hand open, the way he did all his praying, which was not often and not where anyone could watch. Korroc felt it go over him like a change in the weather. Not sleep. The thing instead of sleep. The night opening up into a kind of rest a man could take sitting up, with his eyes open and his hammer across his knees.

He looked at his cousin. Thane did not look back at him. He went and sat down on the far side of the fire with his knives on their cloth, and began on them with the whetstone.

So they sat up, the five of them, through the dark.

It was a long night and a quiet one. No moon again. The wind came and went across the top of the ridge. The succubus sat a little apart from the fire, with her back to a stone and her bow across her knees and her wings drawn in, and did not speak unless spoken to, and watched the dark. Nageru sat near her — not close; near — facing out. Caleth read his book by the firelight with his hand flat on the page, and every so often stopped reading and simply looked at nothing for a while, and then went on.

And Thane kept rubbing the back of his neck.

Korroc noticed it the third time. His cousin would be at the whetstone, slow and steady, and the stone would stop, and his hand would come up and press flat against the back of his neck under the short hair, hard, the way you press at a place a fly has just landed. Then he would turn his head. Not quickly. Just enough. And look out past the edge of the firelight, into the dark beyond the ridge — and then back, and pick up the stone.

The fourth time, Korroc said, "What?"

Thane looked at him.

"Nothing." He went back to the whetstone. Three strokes. Four. "Somebody's watching."

Korroc looked out at the dark.

There was nothing to see. There was never anything to see out here. He reached for it with the thing in him that felt for life — and found the five of them, warm, around the fire, and the faint dull scatter of small things in the ground and the scrub that were not worth the name, and past that, nothing. Nothing he could feel.

"I don't feel anything," he said.

"No," Thane agreed. "Neither do I." He rubbed his neck again. "That's the trouble."

Across the fire, Caleth had looked up from his book. He did not say anything. Neither did the succubus, though Korroc saw her head turn and her eyes go out into the dark where Thane had looked, and stay there, a long time.

Nothing came.

Nothing came all night. The wind went over the ridge, and the fire burned down, and Thane kept rubbing the back of his neck and muttering under his breath, and once got up and walked a slow circle round the whole of the camp, out at the very edge of the light, and came back and sat down again and said nothing. By the time the sky began to go gray in the east Korroc had stopped watching the dark and started watching his cousin, which he suspected was worse.

---

They had set camp near a rise.

Fifty feet, maybe sixty, and steep — not a cliff but near enough to one to make no difference to a dwarf in plate, a long raw slope of broken shale going up at an angle that would have had Korroc on his hands and knees for an hour and swearing for most of it. He stood at the bottom of it with his hands on his hips and looked up at the top and said a word in Dwarven his mother would have taken a spoon to him for.

Caleth looked at him sidelong.

"Come here," he said. "All of you. Close."

They came in around him — the three of them and the succubus — close enough that Korroc's shield was in Thane's ribs and Thane's elbow was in his. Caleth looked up at the top of the rise, measuring it, his lips moving.

"I can take all of us," he said. "A very short way. This is about as far as it goes, with five."

"How short?"

"That," said Caleth, and pointed up the slope.

The world *slid*.

It was not a fold. Korroc had been folded across the Wound a dozen times now and knew the feel of it, the cold half-second of nowhere. This was smaller, and sideways, and very quick — a step that went a long way further than a step ought to, the ground going out from under his boots and coming back up under them somewhere else, like missing a stair in the dark — and then they were standing at the top of the rise in a cluster with the whole slope below them, and Korroc's stomach was still somewhere about the bottom.

He looked down at it.

"Huh," he said. "That's a good one."

"Thank you." Caleth was already reaching for his book. "It is not a thing I can do often."

He called the horses again there at the top of the rise, in the thin gray morning, and they drew themselves up out of the air exactly as they had the day before — four of them, smoky, still, unbothered by anything. Korroc walked up to his and looked it in the eye.

"You," he said, "are going to give me warning this time."

The horse looked at him.

He got on anyway.

---

By midday there was green ahead.

He did not believe it at first. He saw it as a line along the bottom of the sky to the south, a darkness, and took it for the shadow of a ridge — and then they came over a long rise of broken ground and it was not a shadow. It was trees.

*Green.*

He had not seen green in — he did not know how long. Not real green, not growing green; nothing in the Wound grew that color. The grass in the north had been gray-brown and the scrub had been gray and the forest a week west of the fane had been gray all through, stone to the heart, standing up dead against a dead sky. He had forgotten what it did to you, to see a thing that color after so long without it. It went into the back of his eyes and stayed there.

They were bad trees. He saw that as they came nearer. Twisted, warped, wrong in the trunk — bent at angles no tree bent at on its own, the bark split and healed and split again into knots and burls like the knuckles of a gnarled hand, the branches going out crooked and coming back in on themselves. The leaves were the wrong shape. The green was the wrong green, in places, too dark or too bright, with a sheen on it that he did not like.

But they were trees. They were alive. He reached for them as the horses carried him in under the first of the branches, and *felt* them — the slow deep pull of wood growing, root and sap, the patient enormous greed of a forest for water and light, all of it bent and sick and going on anyway.

"It's alive," he said aloud.

"It is the Green Gate," said the succubus. She did not turn round. "It is the only green thing for a long way. That is why they call it so."

They went through.

It was not far. A mile, maybe two, under the bent branches with the light coming down green and wrong through the leaves. Nageru walked ahead with his face lifted, and Korroc thought he looked as though the trees were saying something to him that none of the rest of them could hear. Then the trees thinned, and stopped, and they came out on the south side of them into gray country again, and the succubus turned west.

She led them along the edge of the wood a while. Then away from it, across open ground, to the lip of a scar in the earth.

It was deep. A long raw gorge, cut down into the ground as though someone had taken an axe to the world and not been gentle about it, with sheer broken walls going down into shadow and a floor somewhere a long way below. Korroc got off his horse and went to the edge and looked down, and his stomach went somewhere it did not want to go.

"About a hundred feet," Caleth said, beside him.

"Aye," said Korroc. "About."

The succubus came up on Caleth's other side and looked down too, and then along the gorge to the west, where it bent away out of sight.

"We will need to go down," she said, "and follow it along to the place." She considered the drop, and then turned her head and looked at the four of them, one after another, and something came into her face that Korroc had not seen there before. It was nearly mischief. "I can carry someone."

Korroc made a noise.

He had not meant to. It came out of his nose, all at once, a great snort through his beard that was very obviously a laugh trying not to be one, and he clapped his mouth shut on it too late and stood there at the edge of a hundred-foot drop with his shoulders shaking.

Thane did not even look round.

"You can take the half-elf," he said.

Caleth turned and looked at him with an expression of the most profound betrayal.

"I'm sure," said Nageru, before anyone could say anything else, "that it is no trouble for us." He said it with perfect gravity. He was smiling with his eyes.

The succubus looked from one of them to the next. Then at Caleth.

"Well?" she said.

"No," said Caleth. "Thank you. I am quite capable." He looked down at the drop, and his jaw set, and Korroc watched him do the sum and not like the answer. "I have," he said, "my own arrangements."

So they went down every one of them a different way, and Korroc thought afterward that if anyone had been watching from the far lip it would have looked like a festival.

He went first, with his hand on his scale. The silver light came up off it, soft, the way it always did, and he stepped off the edge with his eyes shut — he always did that part with his eyes shut, and he did not care who knew it — and drifted. Slowly. Down. Turning a little in the cold air with his shield against his chest and his armor creaking, slow as a seed, past the broken face of the wall and the roots sticking out of it and a bird's nest with nothing in it, and his stomach behaved itself, more or less, the whole way.

Thane came after, on his own scale, with his arms folded and his face set, looking as though he were waiting for a door to be opened.

Nageru went down the wall.

Korroc saw him do it from halfway down and nearly forgot to keep drifting. The monk simply stepped off the lip of the gorge onto the face of it, and then *down* it, and his bare feet found ledges that Korroc could not see and would not have trusted with a cat, and he went from one to the next in long unhurried strides as though the wall were a stair someone had cut for him, and at the bottom he dropped the last ten feet and landed on his toes in the dust without a sound and looked up, mildly, to see how the rest of them were getting on.

The succubus came down on her wings.

He had wondered whether she could. The torn one, the one the arrow had gone through. She spread them at the lip — wide, dark, huge, wider than he had thought they would be, blotting out a great piece of the sky — and he saw her face tighten as the torn one took the air. Then she stepped off, and the wings caught, and she came down in a long slow spiral past him with her cloak streaming out behind, close enough that he felt the wind of her going by.

She looked at him as she went past. Drifting, armored, in a slow silver turn, with his eyes now open and very wide.

He saw her almost laugh.

And Caleth, at the top, waited until all four of them were down. Then he stood at the edge a moment with his hands at his sides, the way he stood before a hard thing. Then the air *slid* round him, and he was standing at the bottom beside them, a little white, straightening his coat.

"My own arrangements," he said.

"Very good," said Thane.

"Thank you."

The succubus folded her wings and looked at him. "I would not have dropped you," she said.

"I am sure," said Caleth, "that you would not."

---

"There is a front gate," said the succubus, as they walked the floor of the gorge. "And there is a second way in. At the back."

"Which is better guarded?" said Caleth.

"The front." She said it as though it were plain. "Obviously. The veil is on the front, and the basilisk. The back is not meant to be known." She did not look round. "I only know it because I came and went here once. As one of them."

Nobody said anything to that.

The gorge bent. The walls closed in. The light went gray and then grayer. The succubus walked ahead of them all, with her bow in her hand now and an arrow on the string, and the four of them strung out behind — Nageru close at her back, then Caleth, then Thane, then Korroc last of all, as he usually was, because he was the slowest and the loudest and somebody had to be at the back and he did not mind it being him.

He was thinking about the axe.

He knew afterward that that was the trouble. He was walking at the back of the line in the gray light, with his eyes on the ground and his mind half a Wound away on a command-room table, wondering whether Irabeth had found somewhere it could see the door — and up ahead the succubus made a sharp turn off the gorge floor, at a place in the wall where there was nothing at all to turn into, and walked straight into the solid rock.

And was gone.

Korroc stopped dead.

"Where'd she *go*?"

Ahead of him, Nageru did not stop. The monk walked up to the same place in the wall without a pause and stepped into it, and the stone took him as though it were water, and he was gone too.

Caleth turned round. He was grinning.

"It's an illusion," he said. "The wall."

Korroc stared at the wall.

It looked like a wall. It looked like *exactly* a wall — the same broken gray face as the rest of the gorge, the same streaks of old water, a crack running down it at a slant that he could see the shadow inside. He had walked past a thousand yards of that wall in the last hour. He could not tell it from any of them.

Caleth stepped through it. Then Thane.

Korroc stood in front of it alone.

He did not put out his hand. He thought, afterward, that he should have. That if he had laid his palm on it, as he had laid his palm on a floor in Drezen that was not a floor, he would have known it in an instant for the lie it was. But he did not. He had not been paying attention. That was the whole of it — he had been thinking about a sulking axe, and his legs were short and the others' were long, and he had been a dozen paces back and looking at the ground, and the stone had lied to him to his face and he had not even been close enough to call it a liar.

He said a word in Dwarven.

He shut his eyes and walked into it.

It felt like nothing. That was the worst part. It felt like walking through the space where a wall should be.

On the far side there was a narrow cleft going up, steep, with a floor of loose stone, and the others waiting for him a little way up it with expressions of great politeness. He went past them without a word and started up the cleft.

It went up a long way. It bent. And at the top of it, where it bent the second time, the succubus — ahead again; she had got ahead of all of them while he was busy being lied to — turned sharp to the left into the face of the rock, and was gone.

Korroc, at the back, stopped short.

"*Again?*" He said a much longer word in Dwarven, which he had learned from his father and his father had learned from a man who worked the deep mines and had a great deal to say about stone. "*Which* one this time?"

Ahead of him Caleth shook his head and grinned, and stepped through the left-hand rock as though it were a curtain.

Korroc went after him, and put his hand on it as he went, deliberately, and felt — nothing. Air. Cold air, where his eyes told him and told him and *told* him there was stone.

"Can't trust false stone," he muttered, on the far side. "Can't trust it. Stone's meant to be the one thing a man can trust." He looked at his own hand. "It's *meant* to be."

Nobody answered him. He had not expected anybody to.

---

They came out into a tunnel.

Rough stone, cut rather than worn, going north out of the dark of an enormous cavern at their backs. Behind them, to the south, a little mound of fallen rubble lay before the mouth of another tunnel that wound off into blackness. Ahead, the passage ran on, rough-walled, into the dark.

It was very quiet.

They stopped there, at the mouth of the passage, and got ready. Nobody had to say it.

Caleth went first. He put his hand out flat in front of him and spoke, low, and Korroc watched the half-elf's skin change. It went *rough*. That was the only word for it — gray, and grained, and rough, all over his face and his hands, like a man who had been rolled in fine sand and the sand had stuck, or like a statue that had just remembered how to breathe. It was not stone. Korroc knew stone. But it was a very good copy of stone, and it was the kind of copy that would turn an edge, and he looked at it with a smith's interest and a Stonelord's faint offense in about equal measure.

"Suits you," he said.

Caleth flexed his gray fingers. "It does not," he said. "But it will do."

Korroc went to the others.

He did it the way he had done it three mornings ago among the stone trees, without fuss: a bare hand on Nageru's shoulder, and the thread to him thickening under his palm until it would take half of whatever came; then Thane, who looked at the hand on his arm and did not ask; then Caleth, rough and gray now under his coat. Half. Half. Half. He felt them settle in him, three warm weights on the far ends of three lines.

He did not lay it on the succubus. He thought about it. He looked at her standing a little way off with her bow in her hand, and thought about what it would be to feel a demon's wounds come down a thread into his own chest, and what Torag would think of it. And then he thought that he had not the first idea what Torag would think of it, and that this was not the morning to find out.

Caleth went on casting. He laid a working over all of them that Korroc felt settle on his skin like a cool damp cloth — against fire, he said; there would be fire. And then one more. He lifted his hands, and spoke, and something went out from him in a ring — not a wall, not anything you could see, but a *line*, drawn round the place where he stood and going with him wherever he went, a few paces across, a circle of something clean and hard in the air that pushed against the dark of the tunnel the way warm air pushes against cold.

A circle against evil. Korroc had seen him lay it before. It sat round the half-elf like the hallowing had sat in the tower.

And the succubus was standing inside it.

Korroc saw it the instant the working went up. She was a pace from Caleth's left side, where she had been standing all along, and the line of the circle went round behind her, and she was *inside* it, in the clean hard air, with her black horns and her dark wings and her bow, and nothing happened to her. Nothing at all. She did not flinch. She did not step back. She looked down at her own feet, once, as though she had felt something change in the air and was curious what it was. Then she looked up again and went on watching the tunnel.

Caleth had seen it too. Korroc watched him see it. The gray rough face did not move.

Neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say that the tower had not said already.

---

Thane went up the passage alone.

He went the way he always went, a shape for three steps and a shadow for one more and then nothing, and the rest of them waited at the mouth of it in the quiet. The quiet was very bad. Korroc did not like it. It was not the quiet of an empty place. It was the quiet of a place that has been told to be quiet.

He reached for life, out of habit — and found the five of them, and nothing else, which told him nothing, because the last things they had fought in a place like this had not been alive at all.

Thane came back.

"Side passage," he said, low. "Right-hand side, forty paces up. Opens into a cavern. Big one." He paused. "There are three spiders in it."

Korroc's hand tightened on his hammer.

"Huge," said Thane. "Not moving. Standing like statues. The whole place is thick with magic — I could feel it on my teeth. But they're not." He frowned. "They don't feel like anything. I don't think they're the magic. I think they're *in* it."

Nageru nodded, once, and went up the passage. The succubus went with him.

Korroc went after the two of them with Caleth and Thane at his back, and came up behind them at the opening of the side passage, and looked past the monk's shoulder into the cavern.

They were spiders. They were the size of bull elephants. Black and jointed and glossy, with a cluster of eyes on the front of each, standing in the middle of a wide stone floor in a rough row, motionless, legs set, exactly the way a smith's tools stand on a rack when the smith has gone home for the night.

He knew them. He had fought three of them on the plain outside Drezen, with Aravashnial on the ground behind him.

The succubus had frozen. Korroc saw it. She stood at Nageru's shoulder with her bow half raised and her whole body gone still, the way a hare goes still in a field when the hawk's shadow crosses it, and her face had gone the color of the linen on her arm.

Thane looked at her. "What are they?"

She shook herself. It was a small movement, wings and shoulders, a creature shaking off cold water.

"Retrievers," she said.

Caleth swore, very softly, in a way he did not usually permit himself.

"We have fought some already," he said. And then, lower, so low that Korroc only caught it because he was standing at the half-elf's elbow: "So that is where they came from."

Korroc looked at him.

Caleth was not looking at anybody. He was looking at the three still shapes in the cavern, with his rough gray face perfectly empty, and Korroc knew exactly what he was thinking, because he was thinking it too. *Three of them, the size of elephants, coming over the rise behind a man walking home.* Thane had said it then. *It's not here to kill him. It came to fetch him.*

From here.

The retrievers moved.

---

They did not wake slowly. One moment they were standing on the cavern floor like tools on a rack, and the next all three had lurched and turned together, legs going, eyes coming round, in perfect silence, and the nearest was already coming at the opening where they stood.

Nageru was gone from Korroc's side before he saw him go.

The monk hit it twice — two blows, fast, a lamp bursting and bursting — and Korroc saw the blows land and saw them not do what they should have. The thing's hide took them like a smith's leather apron takes sparks. Thane was right behind him, coming in low at the same one with his knives, and the spider simply stepped *back*, quick and neat as a dancer, and the knives went into air.

The one standing apart from the other two turned its eyes on Caleth.

A ray came out of them. Korroc saw the half-elf take it full in the chest — and saw him go *stiff*, all at once, his rough gray skin going grayer and his arm half-raised and frozen there, as though the stone that was not stone had decided to become stone after all. For a terrible heartbeat Caleth stood in the mouth of the cavern like a statue of himself.

Then he shook it off. Korroc saw it go out of him, a shudder from the shoulders down, and he was moving again — and the spider that had done it went straight up the cavern wall, legs going, and hung from the ceiling upside down above them all like a nightmare in a cellar.

The third looked at Korroc.

He saw the ray coming. He did not know how he saw it — something in its eyes changed, a gathering — and he threw himself sideways, armor and all, a thing he did not do and was not built to do, and hit the stone floor on his shoulder and rolled. The ray went over him. It went past where his chest had been and struck the cavern wall behind, and he did not see what it did there because the spider was on him before he had finished rolling.

He got the shield up.

He got it up from the floor, flat on his back, without thinking, the way you get a hand up to a face — and the claws came down on it. One. Two. And he *felt* them land, and felt the shield take them, and there was something in the shield when it did that had not been there two days ago. He did not have time to know what. It was only there — a readiness, a grain running the right way, the whole weight of the blow going into the wood and the iron and the old enamel and *spreading*, the way a good shield spreads a blow and a bad one passes it on — and both claws skidded off the Father's hammer and anvil and found nothing.

*There you are,* he thought, and did not know whether he was thinking it at the shield or at himself.

The bite came over the top of it.

The mandibles found him. They went in over the rim of the shield and closed — on his shoulder, on his arm, on the plate over his chest — and *squeezed*, and he was lifted off the floor. Lifted. A dwarf in full plate with a shield on his arm, lifted up off the stone like a child picked up by the scruff, and held there in the jaws of the thing with his boots kicking a foot above the floor.

He brought the hammer up.

The jaws closed harder, and his arm was pinned against his own side, and the hammer would not come round. He felt the plate on his chest creak. He felt his ribs under it creak, which was worse. He hung there in the thing's mouth with his hammer arm trapped and his breath going out of him in short hard pieces, and he could not do a thing.

Somewhere across the cavern Radiance flashed, and he heard Caleth's glaive sing in the air, and heard it miss — the spider that held him danced back a step, holding him, swinging him with it, and the white edge went past a hand's breadth from its eyes.

Above them, the one on the ceiling breathed out cold.

Not breath — another ray, pale and white, and it went down past Korroc and struck Caleth, and Korroc felt it come down the thread to him, half of it, a long blue ache of frost in his own bones that his own blood hated worse than anything. He grunted round it. And then the thing let go of the ceiling and dropped on the half-elf, legs and jaws and all, and the bites and claws went in hard.

The one that held him clawed at him.

He saw them coming, the claws, from where he hung — five of them, it seemed, though it could not have been five, from every side — and he turned what he could turn. The shield took one. The plate took another. A third went past. Two got through, under the arm, at the hip, and he felt them go in.

All right. All *right*.

He let go of the hammer's weight in his mind, and reached for the other thing — the warm thing, the Father's thing, the one he had been giving away to other people for a year — and turned it round, for once, and laid it on himself. Gold went through him. The wounds pulled in at the edges. He breathed.

Then he woke the boots.

He felt them come alive on his feet the way they always did, a hot quick hum up through the soles and into his shins, and the world slowed down around him like honey going cold. The spider was still holding him. It was still swinging him. But it was swinging him *slowly* now, and his hammer arm had a gap to work in that it had not had a moment ago, an inch, two inches, and an inch was all a smith ever needed.

He swung four times.

The thing threw him round in its jaws, side to side, like a dog with a rat, and two of the swings went into air and two of them did not. Two of them came down on the joint where its jaw met its head, *crack* and *crack*, the old honest shock of adamantine on something hard all the way up his arm into his teeth, and he felt something give in there under the hammer.

Caleth came out from under the one that had dropped on him — Korroc did not see how — and was beside him, and Radiance came round twice into the thing that held him, and the second time it struck the glaive *lit*. White and holy and terrible, a smite like a sunrise, and the light went into the spider's hide where the hammer had cracked it and went deep. Korroc felt the whole construct shudder around him. It did not let go.

Across the cavern the one on Nageru struck at the monk with its jaws and with one claw, and Korroc saw it try to catch him — the mandibles snapping where the monk had been a moment before — and Nageru was not there. He had ducked under. He was always under.

An arrow went over Korroc's head.

Four of them, quick, one after another, so fast he heard them as a single long hiss — and three of them went into the spider on the far side, the one that had dropped on Caleth, *thock thock thock*, deep, in a row down its side. Korroc twisted his head in the jaws and saw the succubus standing at the cavern mouth with her bow drawn back to her ear for a fifth, her face perfectly calm.

Nageru changed.

Korroc did not know another word for it. The monk had been fighting the way he always fought — fast, light, quick fists — and then he simply *settled*, his feet going wider, his weight coming down into the stone, the way Korroc himself settled when he rooted, and the next two blows he threw came from somewhere a great deal deeper. They came from the floor. The construct he hit went back a full step under each, and shuddered, and its legs folded under it all at once like a table with the pegs knocked out, and it went down and did not move.

And then the thing that held Korroc screamed — a scream with no voice in it, metal shearing — and its jaws opened, and he fell.

He landed on his back. Again. He was getting tired of it. And above him, rising up behind the thing that had held him with both knives in its back to the hilt, was his cousin.

Thane had got behind it. Of course he had. He pulled the knives out, and the construct collapsed forward onto its face beside Korroc with a long grinding crash, and lay there.

"Cousin," said Thane.

"Aye," said Korroc, from the floor. "Thanks."

The third one had Caleth.

Korroc saw it from flat on his back, looking up past the dead one: the spider the succubus had shot, the one that had come down off the ceiling, with the half-elf in its mandibles now and lifted off his feet, the way Korroc had been. Bites and claws had gone into him and the gray rough skin had turned most of them and not all. Korroc felt it come down the thread — half of it, hot — and the cold under it that was still in his bones.

He did not get up. There was not time to get up.

He threw out his hand.

He did not reach for the Father. He reached for the other thing — older in him than the wardstone's gift, the fire in his blood that had always come paired with the cold in his bones, so that he had never been able to have the one without the other. It was always there. It was always hot. He opened his hand and let it out.

Three rays of fire went up from his palm.

They were not gold. They were *red* — forge-red, raw, the color of iron the instant before it goes white, and they went up one after another in a quick hard line and struck the construct in the side where the arrows had gone in, and he smelled its hide begin to scorch.

At the same instant Caleth was not in its jaws any more.

He was not anywhere. The mandibles closed on nothing. And then he was behind it — out of the air, a pace back, on its flank, exactly where a man should stand if he wanted to put a glaive into a thing that was busy looking the wrong way — and Radiance went in.

And skidded. The hide turned it. Korroc heard the half-elf say something under his breath that was not a prayer.

Three more arrows went into it. The succubus had not moved from the cavern mouth.

Then Nageru was on it, fists, everywhere — and it still stood, shuddering, its legs going stiff and wrong — and Thane came round the far side of it, quiet, almost lazy, and put one dagger into a place Korroc could not even see, low, under the front of it where the legs joined. It sank to the hilt.

The construct shuddered from one end of itself to the other.

Then it folded down, slowly, like a tent with the pole pulled out, and lay still on the stone.

---

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Korroc lay on his back on the cavern floor and looked at the ceiling, where one of the things had hung, and breathed. His ribs hurt. His shoulder hurt where the jaws had been. There was frost still in his bones, and somewhere under his arm a wound he had not quite closed, and he was more tired than he had been in a week.

He sat up.

He got the wand out — the plain one, the old wood with the leather wrap, the one that lived against his ribs — and went round them with it. Nageru first, who had been bitten and clawed and had said nothing about either. Thane, who had a long shallow cut on his forearm that he had not noticed. Caleth, whose rough gray skin was cracked across the ribs like a dry riverbed. The succubus he looked at, and she shook her head, once, and he passed her by.

Then himself. Not the wand — he had been taking from it all morning and it had less in it than he liked. He laid his own hand on his own ribs, and spoke to the Father, short, the way he did when there was nobody to hear, and the gold went in.

He looked at the three dead constructs while it did.

*It came to fetch him,* Thane had said.

He thought of the elf at the foot of the bell tower that morning, a day and a half behind them, on his upturned crate with his book in his lap. The thread, coming back as they came out of the fold. *There you are.* And the elf's face moving, just slightly, as though something had come back on his end too.

He got up.

---

Thane went on.

Out of the cavern, back to the passage, and up it — and the passage ended in a stair, carved, going up into the dark. Korroc heard his cousin's soft feet on it, going up, and then stopping, and then a long silence.

He came up after, with the others. At the top of the stair Thane was standing in front of a blank wall.

It was a dead end. The stair went up, and ended, and there was stone in front of it, flat and gray and very solid. Korroc looked at it with the deepest suspicion he had ever turned on a wall in his life.

"Is that real?" he said.

"Aye," said Thane, without turning round. "That one's real."

Korroc put his hand on it anyway. It was. Cold, dense, honest, the stone of the deep places, and he felt it under his palm and was so glad of it that he nearly said so.

Thane did not ask the succubus. Korroc saw him think about it — turn his head an inch toward her, standing at the back on the stair — and then not. Instead he leaned in close to the wall, and looked at it, the way he looked at a lock. Not touching. Looking. His eyes went over the face of it slowly, left and right, up and down, along the line where it met the floor and the line where it met the ceiling.

Then he grinned.

It was a small grin, and very brief, and Korroc had seen it on his cousin's face a hundred times over a hundred doors, and it always meant the same thing.

Thane's fingers went to a place on the wall that looked like every other place on the wall, and pressed. Something clicked, deep in the stone — a small clean sound, a good mechanism, Korroc thought, somebody had known what they were about — and a seam appeared down the face of the rock where there had been no seam.

Thane drew it open a crack. One finger's width. He put his eye to it.

He stayed like that a long moment. Then he looked back at them and lifted two fingers.

Two.

He mouthed a word. Korroc could not read it. Caleth could; Korroc saw the rough gray face go still.

*Derakni.*

Thane edged the door open — very slowly, just wide enough for a dwarf turned sideways — and slipped through.

Korroc heard it start. The soft quick wet sound of knives going in from behind, four times, very fast, *one-two-three-four*, and then a scream — high, insect, furious, a sound like a saw biting a nail.

Nageru was through the door before the scream finished.

Korroc heard two blows. Lamp bursting, lamp bursting. And the scream went up higher and broke off in the middle, and he knew that sound now, the sound a demon made going home whether it liked it or not.

Then a second scream. The other one.

Korroc went up the last of the stair at a run, shield first, and came to the door, and stopped.

He could not get in.

Thane and Nageru were in the doorway. Both of them. Thane a step inside on the left and Nageru square in the opening, and on the far side of them, filling the passage beyond, the second derakni — long, chitinous, insect-wrong, all buzzing wings and hooked claws and a face like a nightmare about locusts — had come in at them hard. Korroc saw it bite at Nageru and saw the bite go in. Saw one claw follow it and land. Saw the second claw go past.

And he could not reach it. The doorway was a dwarf and a monk wide, and they were in it, and he was behind them with his hammer up and his shield up and nothing in front of either but his own companions' backs.

He said something under his breath that Torag would forgive.

Caleth came up beside him on the stair, and Radiance went *past* him — past Korroc's shoulder, past Nageru's ear, the long glaive reaching through the gap between the two of them like a needle through a buttonhole, at the demon's face — and the derakni jerked its head aside, and the glaive went past.

Thane took one step sideways.

Into the room. Out of the doorway, to the left, so that the demon had to turn its head to follow him. It turned its head.

Four knives.

Korroc did not see them. He heard them, the same quick wet four-beat as before, *one-two-three-four*. And the second derakni screamed, and went thin and black, and was gone.

---

They stepped into the room.

It was big. Thirty feet high, near enough; Korroc's eye measured it without being asked, the way it always did. And it was *tiled*. Every wall, floor to ceiling, in small square tiles of something pale and hard and faintly yellow — ivory, or a very good counterfeit of it — set close as scales, with almost no grout between them. Good work. He saw that at once, and resented seeing it. Somebody had been very good at this, and had spent a very long time on it.

And the tiles were patterned.

Lines. Pale lines in the pale tile, cut and inlaid, going round and round, turning back on themselves, crossing and not crossing — a maze. A whole wall of maze, and the next wall, and the next, lines in lines in lines, so that the eye went into them and kept trying to follow one and lost it and found another and lost that too. Korroc looked at it for three heartbeats and felt his head start to ache, and made himself look away.

To the east, the tiles stopped.

The whole of that wall was a carving. Floor to ceiling, thirty feet of it, cut straight into the living rock behind the tile — a figure. Tall. Goat-headed. Leering down at them out of the stone with its great curved horns going up into the dark of the ceiling and its mouth open in something that was meant to be a grin. It held a glaive across its body in both hands, a long one, with a blade at the end of it shaped like the curve of a crescent moon.

Korroc knew the face.

He had seen it once before, cut crude and new over a seated statue of Irori in a temple thirty miles west of Drezen, by men who had not cared how they cut it. This was not crude. This was the same face cut by somebody who had cared a very great deal. The stone of it was good stone, and the work was very fine, and the whole of it had been done to make a man standing at its feet feel small.

It worked. He did not like that it worked.

There were doors. Three of them — one in the north wall, one in the south, one in the west — set flush into the patterned tile, so that the lines of the maze ran across them and you only saw they were doors at all by the faint dark seam round each. Three doors, and a demon on the fourth wall, and nobody in the room but the five of them and the smell of what Thane and Nageru had just sent home.

Thane looked round at them.

He had his knives out still. He wiped them, one and then the other, slowly, on the cloth at his belt. Then he looked at the north door, and the south, and the west.

"Which one?" he said.

---

Nobody answered him.

Caleth stood in the middle of the room with his rough gray face turned up toward the carving, very still, the circle against evil sitting round him in the air like a held breath. Nageru stood at the doorway they had come in by, with blood on his wraps again and his hands open at his sides, looking at nothing — at the floor, at the tiles — with his head a little on one side. Listening.

And the succubus had turned round.

She was looking at the doors. At the three of them, one after another, the way a woman looks at the doors of a house she lived in once, a long time ago, and has had bad dreams about since. Korroc watched her look. He watched her eyes go from the north door to the south, and stop there, and then go on to the west, and come back. He watched her face. He did not know what he was watching for. He found he was watching anyway.

She had been here, she said. As one of them.

*Look at her, though.* He had said that. In a tower at the end of the world, with an axe screaming in his pack. *She's not evil. Something's happened to her.* He had believed it. He believed it now. He looked at her standing in that ivory room with her eyes going from door to door and her bandaged arm held close against her side, and he believed it with the whole of himself, the way he believed in the Father and in his mother and in good iron.

He had believed in stone, too. Until this morning.

He put his hand down at his side and laid it flat against the wall. On the tile, over the maze, cool and smooth. Under it, a finger's breadth down, he could feel the true rock of the place — deep, old, and very heavy, and very still, the way stone is still when it has been asked to keep a secret for a long time and has got used to it.

It told him nothing. Stone did not lie, he had always said. Stone only kept quiet. He had not, until today, understood how little difference that might make.

The goat looked down at him from the eastern wall with its crescent glaive across its chest.

And Korroc Stonevein, standing in the Ivory Sanctum with his shield on his arm and his hammer in his hand and his cousin waiting on an answer, found his right hand going up and back over his shoulder — of its own accord, the way it had all yesterday on the road — reaching for a haft that was not there. For a cold weight. For a voice in many voices, in Dwarven, that would have known which door the demons were behind before any of them had finished asking.

His hand found his own shoulder, and nothing else.

For the first time since he had laid it on Irabeth's table, he wished he had brought it.
