# Strangers' Faces

*Chapter Twenty-Eight — The Worm's House*

---

There were three doors, and none of them wanted to be found.

Thane stood in the middle of the ivory room with two wiped knives in his hands and read them the way he read every door, because it was the only way he knew how. North. South. West. Each one set flush into the tile so the maze ran straight across its face, line into line, with nothing to show where the wall stopped and the door began but a seam you could have hidden a hair in. Good work. A house built by somebody who did not want visitors and did not want the visitors to know it.

*Listen first.*

Nothing. The room had the hard, held silence of a place where the stone has been told to keep quiet, and had kept it so long it had forgotten there was anything else to do.

*Look second.*

He looked at the floor. That was always where it started. Walls lie; floors don't. And the floor here had been laid by the same patient, hateful hand as the walls, small square tiles set close as scales, the maze running underfoot just as it ran overhead, so that the eye went into it and wanted to follow. He did not let it. He looked past the pattern to the wear on it, which was a different thing entirely and much more honest. There was a dull path worn into the gloss of the tile from the stair door to the north. Another, nearly as deep, to the west. Soldiers' feet, servants' feet, the daily traffic of a house.

To the south there was almost nothing. A faint dulling, no wider than one man's stride. One man, going and coming, not often, and never in company.

He knew that kind of path. Every fortress had one. It led to the room that belonged to whoever owned the fortress.

*Touch third.*

He did not touch anything yet. He turned round.

The others were where he had left them. Korroc with his shield up and his eyes on the goat-headed carving as if he meant to have words with it later. Nageru by the stair door, blood on his wraps, head a little on one side. Arueshalae a pace behind Caleth, inside the line of the half-elf's circle, her bow low and her eyes going round the room the way they had been going round it since they came in. And Caleth himself, gray and rough-skinned under the stone that was not stone, his coat buttoned to the throat, turning on his heel to look at each door in turn.

"I have a spell," Caleth said, "that could probably tell us exactly where Xanthir Vang is."

He said the name as though it were a weight he had been carrying a long way and had just set down to rest his arms. He looked round at the three of them.

"We could check each of these doors, one by one. Or I could cast it, and we could go straight at him."

Thane thought about it. Not long. He did not need long; he had been in this fight before, in a different house, and the shape of it was still in him.

"I'd really rather not have things come up behind us," he said, "while we're fighting him."

Korroc grunted.

"That'd be Staunton all over again," his cousin said. "Every door in the place spitting something out at us, and him in the middle of it, and nobody watching our backs." He shook his head slowly. "That could be bad. That *was* bad."

It had been. Thane remembered it more than he liked to: the hall at Drezen, the doors banging open one after the other, minotaurs, demons, the arrow slits, and somewhere in the middle of it Staunton Vhane on one knee with five of Thane's stabs in him, and Thane shouting *don't kill him*, and the argument he had lost. That one was still lying where it fell. Neither of them had picked it up since.

Caleth looked at Korroc, and at Thane, and nodded once. "Then I won't cast," he said. "We check doors."

Thane went to the south one.

---

It fought him.

Not much. Not the way a good lock fights, with honest weight and a stubborn ward; this was a fussy thing, overbuilt, with a second set of pins sitting behind the first like a servant standing behind a chair, and the first time he thought he had it the second set dropped and he lost it all. He breathed out through his nose. Said a word in Dwarven his mother would have pretended not to hear. Started again.

*Listen.* The pins, one by one, each a tiny distinct voice. *Look* — not with his eyes now, with his fingers. *Touch.*

*Click.*

He eased the door back a finger's width, then a hand's, and put his eye to the gap, and then opened it the rest of the way and stood in it.

A big room. Bigger than the ivory room, and darker, lit by black candles set here and there in a ring on the floor, guttering, giving off a smell like burned fat and something sweet underneath. In the middle of the floor, inside the ring of candles, a circle of iron had been sunk flush into the stone, and inside the iron a rune had been cut. Seven arms. Seven long curving strokes going out from a center, each bent at the end like a hand reaching, and the whole of it laid so the arms seemed to turn if you let your eyes rest on it.

He did not let his eyes rest on it.

Cabinets against the walls. Bookshelves, north, east and south, crammed with scrolls and spines and boxes and things he did not have names for, glass and bone and metal. A writing desk. In the southwest corner, a long stone sarcophagus with its lid on. To the west, a short flight of steps going down into a smaller, rougher hollow, like a cave somebody had let into the corner of a room and forgotten to wall off.

And worms.

They were everywhere. Pale, finger-long, scattered over the floor and the desk and the shelves and the tops of the books, in the folds of the scrolls, on the lip of the sarcophagus. Some of them were dried to brown curls, like shavings off a bad plane. Some of them were still slick. None of them were moving.

He heard Korroc come up behind him and stop.

"Worms," said his cousin.

"Aye."

"Dead ones."

"So far."

They went in. Thane went first, along the wall, keeping to the edge of the room the way you keep to the edge of a floor you do not trust, and the others came in after him. Arueshalae did not. She stopped in the doorway and stayed there, half in and half out, with her bow low, looking into the room and then back over her shoulder into the ivory one, in and out, as if she had decided that somebody ought to be watching both and it might as well be her. He did not argue. He would have put somebody there himself.

Caleth had gone straight to the iron circle.

He did not step into it. He knelt at the edge of it, his coat skirts pooling on the stone, and looked at the rune for a long time without touching anything, his rough gray face very close to the iron. His lips moved. Then he stood up, slowly, and stepped back from it.

"Don't step in that," he said.

Thane frowned at it. "What does it do?"

"I'm not sure." Caleth looked at him. "But I think it could be extremely dangerous. I think it may be set to do something very bad to anyone who isn't — what he is." He paused. "Anyone good, I mean. Don't step on it."

They walked round it. All of them, the whole time they were in the room, as if it were a hole in the floor. Thane caught himself doing it even when he was on the far side of the room with his back to it, keeping the circle in the corner of his eye the way you keep a sleeping dog in the corner of your eye. He did not like a thing in a room that nobody could put a name to.

He liked it less in this room.

Korroc and Nageru went to the sarcophagus. Nageru set his palms against the lid and pushed, and stone ground on stone, and the lid went back a foot.

Korroc looked in. His face did something complicated.

"More worms," he said, and turned away.

Caleth had gone to the shelves.

---

Thane watched him do it, from across the room, because watching was what Thane did.

The half-elf went along the south wall slowly, the way he went along a gallery in a temple, with his hands behind his back and his head tilted to read the spines, and he did not touch anything for a while. Then he stopped. His hand came out from behind him and went to one book on the second shelf, a plain thing, dark leather gone soft at the corners, and drew it out.

He looked at the cover.

Thane was close enough by then to see it too. He had drifted that way without deciding to.

There was a mark pressed into the leather, a little left of center, not large. Three crescent moons, set side by side in a row, horns up, like three boats drawn up on a beach.

Thane knew that mark. He had seen it a hundred times on a hundred pieces of paper. On the edge of every page of Aravashnial's notes. On the sketch of a gnome-sized footprint the elf had torn out of his journal at the Ahari and put in Thane's hand. On the spines of the books in the study at Drezen. It was the elf's mark; it went on everything he owned the way a smith's mark goes on everything a smith makes.

Except it was not, quite.

On this one the middle moon was full. Not a crescent: a whole round disc, between two crescents, like a coin set between two parings of a nail.

The same mark. With a difference.

Thane's people knew about marks like that. Every house in Khar-Zadûn had carried one, his father had told him, cut into the lintel and the anvil and the hilt; and when a second son set up his own forge he did not take a new mark. He took his father's, and cut it with a difference — a notch, a bar, a line struck through — so that anyone who knew how to read marks could look at the work and say, *that is Thorek's house, but not Thorek's hand.*

He did not know that the Riftwardens did anything of the kind. He did not know anything about the Riftwardens at all, except that the elf was one, and had closed a hole in the world in a cave above the Ahari, and had said so.

He looked at Caleth's face.

Caleth was looking at the book. He was holding it in both hands, the way you would hold a bird that had flown into a window and might not be dead, and his face had gone perfectly still, the stillness that was not calm, the stillness Thane had seen on it once before beside a cot in Drezen's infirmary. He did not open the book. He looked at the mark for perhaps three heartbeats. Then he put it inside his coat, on the left side, and turned back to the shelves.

He did not say anything.

Thane did not ask. He thought: *that's another one.* And he went to look at the stairs.

---

The hollow at the bottom of the steps was rougher than the room above it, raw rock with the tool marks still in it, low enough that a man would have had to stoop. A dwarf did not. In the back corner, against the wall, there was a chest.

Iron-bound. Old wood, good wood, black with handling. Locked.

He knelt in front of it and did not touch it.

*Listen first.* He put his ear near the lid, not on it. Nothing inside moved. Nothing ticked. *Look second.* The keyhole: scratched round its lip, the bright scratches of a key going in a little crooked, often, by a hand that did not care. Nothing at the hinges. No thread across the seam, no pin, no hair of wire. He looked along the edge of the lid with his cheek nearly on the floor, and along the floor itself for the little square of different dust that says a plate is set there. *Touch third.* The plate round the keyhole had never been forced. Nobody had ever had to.

He did it now. A short honest lock, after the fussy one upstairs. It went over in his hands like a dog rolling onto its back.

*Click.*

Two vials, and a book.

The vials were glass, small, stoppered, lying on their sides on a fold of black cloth. Empty. At the bottom of each, and up one side where the last of whatever had been in them had run as they were laid down, there was a stain. A dried crust of color.

Purple.

He reached for one. He did not decide to; his hand went, because a vial is a small thing and he had picked up a thousand small things.

His fingers stopped a hair short of the glass.

It was not anything he could have named. It was not heat, or cold, or the crawl on the teeth that strong magic gave him. It was more like the feeling of putting your hand on a door in the dark and knowing, before you have pushed, that there is somebody on the other side of it holding it shut. The glass did not want to be touched. Or something in the glass wanted very much to be touched, and that was worse.

He took his hand back.

"Caleth!"

The half-elf came down the steps. He looked at the vials in their cloth, and Thane watched him feel it too: the tiny check in his breath, the hand that stopped just as Thane's had. Then Caleth took out a handkerchief, folded it twice, and picked the vials up through it one at a time, and turned each to the light of the stair, and looked at the purple crust for a long while.

"No idea," he said at last, and put them both away in a pouch at his belt. "But I could guess."

Thane nodded.

He could guess too.

He had sat in a council room in Drezen, a long while back now, across the table from Joran Vhane, and listened to the dwarf tell Irabeth about five vials that came from the Ivory Sanctum. A gift, Joran had called them. Something that would *wake* what was already in a person, power that had always been there and was hidden. Staunton had volunteered. Staunton had drunk one and lain three days on a cot with his muscles locked so hard his brother thought the bones would go, and screamed until there was no voice left, and kept on trying to scream after. And then he had woken up. *Stronger after. Faster. It was like somebody had opened a door inside him.*

Thane had heard that last sentence, at the time, and had not looked at Korroc, and had not looked at the half-elf, and had not looked at the aasimar either. Nobody had. They had all known exactly what it was describing, because the four of them had stood in the fire of the wardstone and had the same door opened in them, without asking, a year ago.

And here were two more empty vials, in a chest, in the room where the man who made them slept.

*So that's what it looks like,* he thought. *When it's done on purpose.*

There was a book in the chest as well, under where the vials had lain. A journal, by the look of it, cheap leather and a cord to tie it shut. Caleth picked it up and untied the cord and opened it, and Thane watched him read.

The half-elf read Abyssal. Thane knew that the way he knew Caleth read Dwarven, and more tongues besides than Thane had names for, and probably the backs of people's heads. He watched Caleth's eyes go along a line, and back, and along it again, and frown; and turn a page, and frown harder, the way a man frowns at a lock whose pins have been set in an order that makes no sense. *Code,* Thane thought. *It's written in Abyssal, and then it's written in something else on top of that.*

Caleth turned another page. Went still.

Then he shut the book. Hard, with a clap that went round the hollow like a hand on a table, and stood there with it closed in both hands and his eyes narrowed at nothing.

"I don't like what might be in that book," he said.

Thane waited.

"That's something for later," said Caleth, and put it away inside his coat beside the other one, and went back up the stairs.

He left it at that. Thane let him.

---

Upstairs, Thane went to the sarcophagus.

Korroc was standing a little way off from it with the face of a dwarf who has been made to look into a midden. "Worms," he said, as Thane went past. "More worms. I'm telling you."

Thane looked in. Worms. A long bed of them in the bottom of the stone box, a hand deep, dried and slick, the smell of them coming up sweet and wrong. Nothing else. No bones. No cloth. Nobody had been laid out in this to rest. It was not a grave.

He walked round it.

He did it slowly, the whole way, at the height of his own eyes, which on a dwarf is a useful height for a sarcophagus. Stone does not lie, his cousin liked to say. But masons do, sometimes, when they are paid to, and there is always a seam where they did it. He found it on the third side. A line in the carving that ran a hair too straight to be carving, where a panel of the base sat a thumb's thickness proud of the stone around it.

"Did you see this secret compartment?" he said.

Korroc looked over. "What secret compartment?"

Thane grinned at him. "Exactly."

It was locked, of course. Small, neat, a good mechanism hidden in the mouth of a carved leaf. He checked it close for anything nasty and found nothing, worked it, and felt it go. The panel came away in his hands.

Inside there was a bag, and a box.

He took the bag out first. Plain leather, gray, drawstring neck, about the size of a loaf. He opened it and looked inside, and then he closed it and looked at the outside of it, and then he opened it again and looked inside for a longer time, because the inside was very much bigger than the outside, and that was not a thing a dwarf liked to see in a bag.

"Magic," he said.

Caleth's head came round from the desk so fast Thane heard his neck crack.

He came over. They knelt on the floor beside the sarcophagus together, out of the line of the iron circle, and began to take things out.

Books, first. A good many books. Caleth took each one and opened it and looked at the first page and put it in a stack, quickly, the way a clerk sorts mail. Then he stopped, with one in his hands — black, heavy, with a clasp of some dark metal — and turned a page, and another, and Thane saw his whole face change.

It was not the stillness. It was the opposite. It was shock, plain open shock, the first time Thane could remember seeing it on him undisguised: his eyes went wide and his mouth came a little open and for a moment he looked exactly his age.

"This is Vang's spellbook," Caleth said.

He did not say how he knew. Thane did not ask that either. He watched the half-elf hold the man's — the worm's — the *thing's* book open in his rough gray hands, and look at a page of it, and the look on his face was the look of a man who has walked into the house of the person who killed his family and found the man's diary lying open on the table.

Thane did not know why he thought that. He thought it anyway.

Caleth closed the book. "Another for later," he said, and his voice was nearly level, and he put it in the bag.

The box was platinum. Pure platinum, solid, the length of Thane's forearm — he hefted it and whistled through his teeth, because what was in his hands would have bought a street in Kenabres before Kenabres burned. He checked it for traps and found none, and opened it.

Two things lay inside, on red cloth.

A small stoppered flask of something gold, that moved behind the glass like light rather than liquid. And an arrow. Just one. Laid in the box along its length like a jewel in a jeweler's case: straight as a plumb line, fletched in something white, with a head that caught the candlelight and held it a moment longer than a head of steel should.

Korroc had come over. He looked down at the flask.

"That's ambrosia," he said, and his voice had gone quiet with respect, the way it went quiet in a temple.

Caleth nodded. "Yes."

Thane looked at the arrow. He was the only one of them who used a bow with any regularity, and he did not like the bow much, and he picked up the arrow anyway and turned it in his fingers and felt its balance, and it sat in his hand like a thing that had already decided where it was going to land. He put it back in the box. Closed the box. Put the box in the bag.

They went on. More and more went into the bag, and the bag went on not being full.

Papers, that wanted reading. Rods and wands from a shelf, potions in a rack, scrolls by the armful. Things that were plainly magic and things that might have been. And then, a little way into it, a handful of smaller things, wrapped in nothing, kept loose together in one corner of the compartment as though somebody liked to put his hand in and stir them.

A signet ring, a man's, heavy gold. A holy symbol of Iomedae on a broken chain, the sunburst worn soft by thumbs. A holy symbol of Torag. A pendant in the shape of a single leaf, small and fine, mithral, on a chain so thin it would have broken if you breathed on it.

Korroc looked at them for a long moment.

"He keeps trophies," his cousin said, about halfway through, and nobody answered him.

Thane looked at the Torag symbol lying among the others on the stone, the hammer and the anvil, and did not say anything either.

He thought of a ledger. Staunton Vhane had kept trophies too. He had kept them in a room under Drezen and written every one of them down — *a warhammer, three shields* — and for all the hate Thane had for the man there had been a kind of terrible honesty in that. He had written down every name but two. He had at least wanted to remember whose they were.

These had no names. They had no anything. They were thrown in together, a ring and a leaf and two gods, in the dark at the bottom of a box full of worms, by something that did not care whose they had been. Only that they had been somebody's, and were his now.

There were more, as they went on. He stopped looking at them. They all went in the bag.

---

Caleth searched the writing desk himself. He went through it the way Thane went through a room, every drawer, the underside of every drawer, the backs of the pigeonholes; and Thane, who had gone back to the shelves, heard him stop.

Not the sound of a man finding something. The sound of a man not breathing.

Thane turned.

Caleth had a book in his hands. He was holding it the way he had held the other one, the bird-in-the-window way. And he lifted it, very slowly, and Thane saw the cover.

Three crescent moons, horns up, in a row. All three crescents, this time. No difference.

"Aravashnial's spellbook," Caleth said.

And something happened to his face. It was very small, and it did not get anywhere; it started at one corner of his mouth and stopped there, as if he had caught it and put it back. But Thane saw it. It was nearly a smile.

The spellbook Jerribeth had walked about reading in the cavern at the Molten Scar, while a dragon ate at the back of it. The one the elf had asked after, from his cot, before he asked about his staff. *He still has my book,* maybe; Thane did not know about that. He only knew the elf had wanted it, and here it was, in a drawer, in Vang's desk, like a thing put away to look at later.

Caleth held it a moment longer. Then he put it in the bag with the others.

There was one more book in the desk, under it. Caleth took it out and opened it, and Thane came over to see, because the half-elf had frowned at it in a new way.

It was full of drawings.

Collars. A collar on every page — the same collar, drawn and drawn again, from the front and the side and opened flat like a gutted fish. Thane knew it. He had picked it off a man's throat with a pick a hair's breadth from the skin, needle by needle, while the elf went rigid under his hands. He knew the seam. He knew the catch. Here it was, the seam and the catch and the ring of needles and the place at the throat for a stone, drawn in fine black ink, page after page, each one a little different from the last. Thicker here. Thinner there. A needle moved. A catch changed. As if whoever drew it had made one, and watched what it did, and come back to the table and made it better.

And around the drawings, in the margins, writing. Small, close, careful. Thane could not read a word of it. That was not strange; there were a great many things Thane could not read.

"Can you read that?"

Caleth turned a page. Then another. "No," he said slowly. "I do not recognize it at all."

Thane looked at him. In a year he had never once heard Caleth Aranor say that about a piece of writing.

The half-elf closed the book and put it in the bag, and did not say anything else.

---

Caleth stood up from the desk and looked at the sarcophagus for a long time.

He looked at the worms on the floor. At the worms on the desk and the shelves and the lip of the stone box. At the long pale bed of them inside it. Thane watched him do it and could see, though he could not have said how, that the half-elf was looking at something else as well, something that was not in this room at all.

"That must be where he sleeps," Caleth said.

Nobody answered. Everybody knew what Vang was. The elf had written it down for them months ago, and they had seen it since, in the scrying, a thing in robes that made no footsteps and came apart into a thousand writhing pieces. A worm that walks. A man made of worms.

A man made of worms would sleep in worms.

"We need to destroy that sarcophagus," said Caleth.

Korroc looked at him. "You sure?"

Caleth nodded.

Korroc took his hammer off his belt, and spat on his palms, and nodded back.

He went at it the way he went at anything that needed breaking: without hurry, and without stopping, and with a smith's eye for where the first blow should land so the second would have something to work with. The first blow cracked the lid. The second took the end off it. The third went into the side of the box and a great slab of carved stone came away and fell in the worms with a wet thud, and the worms spilled out across the floor in a dry slithering wave, and Korroc said something in Dwarven and kept swinging. Stone chips rang off the walls. The black candles shuddered in their ring. In a little while there was no sarcophagus, only a heap of broken stone and dust and dead white worms, and his cousin standing over it breathing hard with his hammer hanging at his side, looking satisfied in a way Thane had not seen on him since Drezen.

"There," said Korroc. "Sleep in that."

While he was doing it, Caleth had found a vial on a shelf. Not one of the purple ones; a small flask of thick clear glass, stoppered with wax, empty. He held it up to a candle.

"This is magic," he said.

Nageru, at the next shelf, turned his head. He looked at the flask in the half-elf's hand. Then, very deliberately, round the whole of the room: the shelves of glass and bone and metal, the scrolls, the rods, the bag on the floor that had swallowed half a library and was still hungry.

"My friend," he said gravely, "we seem to have found Vang's inner sanctum. I think almost everything in here is magic."

Caleth looked at him a moment. Then he laughed — a short real laugh, surprised out of him — and nodded.

"Fair," he said.

He lifted the flask anyway. He spoke over it, low, and moved his free hand, and Thane felt the heat of the spell start to come off him the way it always did before a fireball, the air beside him going tight and dry — and then the heat did not come. It went *into* the flask. All of it. The glass took it in like a throat swallowing, and for a moment there was a bead of red light at the bottom of it the size of a seed, turning, and then there was only clear glass again.

Caleth held the flask out to Nageru.

Nageru looked at it, and one eyebrow went up.

"Throw it," said Caleth, and he was grinning. "When it breaks, it will be a fireball."

The eyebrow stayed up. Nageru looked at the flask in his palm for a long moment, the way he looked at most things, as though it had told him something about itself and he was deciding whether to believe it. Then he chuckled, low in his chest, and stowed it carefully away somewhere in his wraps.

All the while, every one of them stepped round the iron circle in the middle of the floor, and nobody looked at it for longer than he had to.

---

The bedroom was through the west door of the ivory room, and it was nothing.

A good bed, with a soft deep mattress and a carved headboard, in the northwest corner. A wardrobe and a writing desk in the other. A rug on the floor, finely woven, with a pattern in it that Thane did not look at closely because he was tired of patterns. He checked the door and found no trap and no lock, opened it, and went through the room the way he went through any room, and there was nothing in it. Clothes in the wardrobe, plain and dark and good. Paper in the desk with nothing written on it. The bed made, and smooth, and cold.

He stood a moment with his hand on the footboard.

*Nobody sleeps here,* he thought. *It's a room for looking like a man.*

He did not say it. There was no use in saying it. He went out and shut the door behind him.

The north door of the ivory room was the last of the three, and that one Thane opened slowly. A crack. A finger.

Korroc's hand came down flat on the wood above Thane's head and stopped it.

"Voices," his cousin breathed.

Thane froze. They all froze. On the other side of the door, close, there was the low run of men talking: a laugh, a word he did not catch, the scrape of a boot. A shadow went across the crack of light at the door's edge, left to right, and was gone.

Thane counted it in his head. Not the shadow. The voices. More than three. Fewer than a dozen.

Caleth was beside him. The half-elf listened a moment with his head tilted, and then nodded, and said under his breath, "Soldiers. Let's go."

Korroc did not let go of the door. He did a better thing. He took his hand off it and took hold of the handle instead, and pulled, and in the same motion lifted his other hand and spoke.

The column of fire came down out of nothing in the middle of the room beyond. Gold and white, roaring, straight down from the ceiling like a spear thrown by a god, and it hit the floor and spread, and the men standing in it screamed.

Nageru was through the door before the screaming had properly started.

---

Thane came in on his heels, low, and read the room in the time it took to cross the threshold.

Long. Big. Wooden dummies, four of them, standing about the middle like men who had been told to wait. Racks along the west wall, empty. Two wide arches at the far end, black. And men: soldiers in dark half-armor with maces, a knot of them reeling in the smoke of Korroc's fire with their hands over their faces, and behind them at the back of the room, two more in long black robes, who were not reeling at all. They had their hands up already.

*Them,* he thought. *Those two first.*

The nearest soldier had got his mace up. Thane went in under it at him, mother's blade leading — and the man, half-blind with smoke, found it anyway and turned it, a hard clean parry that rang off Thane's steel and jarred all the way up his arm. Thane went back half a step, startled, and respected him for it.

Behind him Caleth's hand closed round a wand and pulled, the barest flick, and a bead of fire went over all their heads to the back of the room and burst among the soldiers there who had not yet found their feet. The heat came off it like a slap. Men went down, and some got up again.

A mace caught Nageru. Thane heard it, the dull meaty sound of iron on flesh. The aasimar turned with it and the next swing went past him, and a third soldier stepped into the gap and the third swing did not.

Then the floor went out from under Caleth and Korroc.

It was grease. Thane smelled it before he saw it: one of the robed men at the back had flung out a hand, and the stone under his cousin's boots and the half-elf's was suddenly slick and shining. And on top of it, a breath later, a cloud. Thick, yellowish-green, rolling up out of nothing over the two of them and the doorway behind, and the smell that came out of it was the smell of a tannery pit on the hottest day of the year.

Korroc came out of it. Thane saw him come, bent nearly double, his face gone the color of old cheese, retching and walking at the same time, which is a thing only a dwarf can do in plate. He did not slip. Thane did not know how he did not slip. He came out of the cloud and out of the grease onto clean stone and straightened, and swallowed, and looked as though he was going to kill somebody about it.

Nageru had found his rhythm. The man in front of him went down under three blows Thane did not see. The aasimar turned on his heel and hit another twice, and that one folded too. One of the robed men pointed at him, and the air round Nageru seemed to thicken — Thane saw the monk's arm, mid-blow, go *slow*, as if it had been dipped in honey. For a heartbeat. Then Nageru shook it off, the way a dog shakes off water, and the next blow came at full speed.

Thane was looking at the robed men.

Black robes. No armor. Hands moving. Nobody at the back of a fight keeps his hands moving like that unless his hands are the weapon, and Thane had sat in the command room at Drezen not three nights ago and heard a demon tell them what it was they were going to find in this house. *Blackfire adepts. They are what Vang is.*

He did not know if that was what these were. He did not much care.

He went through the soldiers.

Not round. Through. A shoulder down, a tuck, a roll under a mace that was looking for somebody taller, up again on the far side with his boots finding the stone a hand's width further than he sent them, the way they had since Sesker's Gully. And then he was at the back of the room and the nearest of the robed men was right in front of him, turning, his mouth coming open on a word —

The knife went in under the ribs.

The man's face went white and then wide. Thane felt the blade go home and felt it come out, wet, and the man staggered back a step with both hands over the wound and his spell gone out of him like a candle.

Caleth's wand coughed again. The second fireball burst at the back of the room, and Thane, who had been expecting it from the instant he went through, was already low and rolling, and the fire went over him. When he came up, the man he had stabbed was on the floor and not getting off it.

The half-elf came out of the stinking cloud behind him. Picked his way across the grease the way a cat crosses a wet roof, and did not slip either, and Thane would have liked to have seen his face doing it but did not have the time.

Two of the soldiers had Nageru again. One mace came down on him hard, and again, and Thane felt Korroc take half of it without needing to look; he knew his cousin's grunts the way he knew his own breathing. Korroc had come up beside the monk with his shield up. A mace came at him and the shield took it. Another, and the shield took that. The third got inside the rim, and Korroc took it on the hip and grunted again, a different grunt, and Thane saw blood come out from under the edge of his cousin's plate in three places that had nothing to do with that mace at all. Nageru's. Caleth's. His own.

Three bowstrings, quick, from the door behind them. One of the soldiers on Nageru sat down very suddenly against a training dummy with three feathers standing out of his chest. Arueshalae had not come any further into the room than the doorway. She did not need to.

Korroc put his hand on his own chest. Gold went through him, and Thane saw his shoulders come up an inch, and his cousin went back to work with his face still green.

Nageru was a storm. There were three soldiers within reach of him and then there were none.

And the second robed man, the one Thane had not reached, lifted both hands at Thane, and light came off them. Three bolts of it, darting and quick and quite unavoidable, and they took him in the chest, the shoulder, the side of the neck, three hot hard knocks like being hit with small stones.

Thane looked at the man.

"That," he said, "was a mistake."

He rolled forward. The man saw him coming and did not get his hands up again in time.

It ended with Caleth. The last soldier was still on his feet, square in front of Nageru, mace up, and the half-elf, from behind and to one side, laid Radiance over the monk's shoulder like a man laying a spoon in a bowl. The glaive went in at the man's collar. He went down.

Thane wiped both blades.

The room was quiet. Four wooden dummies stood about the middle of it, scorched now, with a dead man leaning against one. Empty racks for weapons and armor along the west wall. Benches. To the west, through the two arches, a pair of smaller rooms with tables and chairs in rows, like schoolrooms. The smoke of Korroc's fire hung under the ceiling. The stinking cloud was thinning in the doorway.

Caleth stood in the middle of it all and counted.

Thane watched him do it. The half-elf's eyes went over the floor from body to body, and his lips moved, and he frowned and went back to the beginning and did it again.

"There is one missing," he said.

Korroc looked up from wiping his mouth. "What?"

"One is gone." Caleth turned slowly in place. "There was one more, at the back. He got away."

Korroc looked round the room. "I didn't see him leave."

"Neither did I."

Thane had not either. He had been at the back of the room the whole fight, which was the place you saw people leave from, and he had not seen a soul go through either arch or past the door. He looked at the arches anyway. Black, empty, quiet.

"I expect," he said, "he had a spell for getting the hell out of here."

Korroc gave him a look that could have stripped paint.

But it sat with Thane, after, more than the joke did. A man had been in this room, at the back of it, in the smoke, and now he was somewhere else, and nobody had seen him go. Somebody in this house knew they were here. Somebody in this house was telling somebody else.

He put it away. He would take it out later.

---

The room after that was a kitchen, and empty, and the best room in the house.

A stove and a firepit and a table at the north end, the stove still warm. An open door into a pantry with good things in it, flour and salt and smoked meat and onions hung in a braid. A long table down the middle with stools round it, scarred with knives and rings from cups. At the south end, bookshelves, three of them, and two writing desks. A house's kitchen. Soldiers had eaten here an hour ago and laughed about something.

Thane did not let himself think about that for long.

Caleth was at the shelves. He took down a box, a small wooden one with brass corners, and turned it, and stopped.

"Uh," he said. "It says *off limits.*"

Thane laughed. It came out of him before he could stop it, the first real laugh since the gorge.

"Well then," he said. "We definitely open it."

Caleth opened it. He looked inside, and his eyebrows went up.

"Wine," he said. "Five bottles. And candied fruit." He held up a little twist of paper, and the smell came out of it across the room, sugar and orange peel. "The expensive kind."

"We'll enjoy that," said Thane.

Korroc, at the pantry door, grinned and shook his head. The box went in the bag. So did a stack of scrolls from the next shelf down, which Caleth went through one by one with the face of a man picking the good apples out of a barrel, all arcane, all careful hands.

---

North of the kitchen there was a wide hallway, and on the hallway there were doors: three pairs of them, and one single.

The first pair opened on a stair going down. Thane stood at the top of it and listened, and heard nothing coming up but cold air, and looked back at the others.

"Let's leave that," said Caleth. "And look round up here."

Thane nodded. He did not like leaving a stair unwalked behind him. He liked it better than walking down into the dark of this house with a dozen rooms still open at his back.

The single door was in the north wall. He knelt in front of it. No trap. No lock. And beyond it —

He held up a hand. He did not turn round; he did not have to. He felt them stop.

Something. A voice, low, too low to be words. The scratch of paper. And under both, so faint he nearly thought he had imagined it, a dry restless ticking, like rain on a roof very far away, or a great many small things moving in a small space.

He stepped to one side of the frame, where a man coming out would not see him first, and pushed the door open.

A room with one table in it. A big table, covered from edge to edge in scrolls and maps and loose papers, held down at the corners with whatever had come to hand: a dagger, an inkwell, a cup. In the middle of the table, a jar. A big glass jar, the kind you would keep pickled onions in, and inside it something moving. Locusts. A great many locusts, crawling over one another, twitching, and wrong — he could see that much from the door — misshapen, with legs bent at the wrong joint and wings that did not lie flat.

Three figures stood round the table.

Two were templars. He knew them by now the way a man knows a smell, the dark half-plate and the hard wary faces and the way they stood with their weight back, already halfway to their swords.

The third was not a man.

It was the height of a tall man, and dressed like a rich one, in red: a long coat of deep red cloth with gold at the cuffs, fitted close, beautifully cut. Out of the collar of it came a fly's head. Huge, faceted eyes, red as the coat. Mouthparts that worked. A pair of gauzy wings folded down its back over the red coat like a cloak, twitching, and all over its head and the backs of its hands a fur of thin black bristles, almost like hair. It stood at the table with one hand resting on a map, as easy as a lord in his own library, and it turned its great eyes toward the door.

"Coloxus," said Korroc, behind him, in the flat voice of a man naming a thing out of a book.

All three of them looked round.

Thane had already decided. Nothing in that room was going to explode out at them, nothing was going to fly at them; the jar was shut, the templars were not casters, the thing in red was where it was. He went back to the frame, and through it, and straight at the demon, and his mother's blade went in through the beautiful coat at the side.

The templar nearest him swung. Three times, fast, a good soldier's sword. Thane turned the first with his father's knife and slipped the second, and the third came in under his arm and opened his side along the ribs.

Nageru arrived. He did not slow down coming through the door; he hit the templar who had cut Thane twice before his feet had even settled, two sharp blows, and the man rocked. Behind them Caleth's hand came up and three darts of light went over Thane's shoulder into the demon's red coat, one-two-three.

The second templar came round the table and swung at Nageru and found him.

And Korroc, last through the door as always, put his own hand on his own chest and said a word, and gold went through him; and then he lifted his hand and opened it, and the gold went out from him too, a warm wave over all of them. Thane felt the cut along his ribs pull shut. Felt Korroc in it, the way he always felt Korroc in it, like a hand on the back of the neck.

The coloxus moved.

It did not attack. It swept one long red arm across the table, quite calmly, the way a man sweeps crumbs, and the jar went off the edge.

It hit the floor and burst.

And then the thing turned its red eyes on Thane, and lifted one bristled hand, and said a word.

---

He was standing in a room.

He did not know the room.

That was the first thing, and it was not frightening, not yet; it was only strange. A room with a table in it, and papers, and a smell of broken glass and something burning. He did not know how he had come to be standing in it. He looked down and there were knives in his hands. Two of them. He knew the knives. He knew the knives the way he knew his own hands, the weight of them, the wear on the grips, the balance. They were his. He did not know why he was holding them.

There was a noise. A buzzing, a great dry rattling roar of it, coming up off the floor. Something was crawling on the table. A great many somethings, brown, crawling over the papers, eating them.

There was a man in red with a fly's face.

He hit him. He did not decide to. The knives knew where the man was and went there, both of them, and he felt them go in, and the man in red with the fly's face made a sound like a kettle and went thin and black, like ink in water, and was not there any more.

*Good,* he thought. He did not know why it was good.

Somebody hit him. A man in dark armor, with a sword. The sword came in and he moved and it went past, and came in again and he moved again, and the third time it did not go past. It hurt. He looked at the man who had done it. He did not know the man.

There was another man. Tall, bronze-skinned, with blood on his hands and on the cloth round his hands, moving fast, hitting the man in armor; and the man in armor fell down.

He did not know that man either.

He knew that he ought to. That was the strange part, and it was getting stranger, it was getting to be the frightening part. He looked at the tall man with the bronze skin and the bloody hands and there was a word for him somewhere, a name, it was on the floor of his mind like a coin dropped under a table and he could see the shine of it and could not reach it.

*Cold.*

The room went white.

Something burst in the middle of it, a great soft bloom of cold, and the buzzing on the table stopped all at once. Little hard things went clattering across the floor round his boots. Ice. Bits of ice. The things that had been eating the papers were ice now, rattling on the stone like hail on a roof. Papers went up in the air, white with frost, and came down slowly all over the room.

And for one moment, standing in the cold with the frost settling on his beard, Thane came back.

It was like surfacing. He felt it. He felt the room come round him and *be* a room, the war room, the table, Vang's papers on the floor; felt his knives in his hands and know why they were there; felt the cut in his side and remember who had made it. He shook his head, hard, the way a man shakes his head coming out of cold water. The other templar was there. Right there. He knew him. He knew exactly what he was for.

He went at him, and his knives went in, and the templar went down.

*There,* Thane thought. *That's —*

---

It came back.

It did not come back like a blow. It came back like a tide, from underneath, so that he did not notice it coming until he was standing in it to the chest.

He was in a room. There was a table. There were people.

There was a woman by the door, with horns. There was a man with gray skin like a statue and a long white blade. There was a dwarf in gold and steel, with a shield, coming toward him. There was the tall bronze man with the bloody hands.

They were all looking at him.

He did not know any of them.

He knew that he ought to. He knew it the way you know there is a step at the bottom of a stair you have walked down a thousand times, in the dark, without counting. The knowing was there. It was the only thing that was there. Everything else had gone out of the room like a tablecloth pulled out from under the plates — the names, the reasons, the *why* of anything — and left only the knowing that there had been something on the table, once, and he had loved it.

Somebody was talking. He was talking. He could hear himself. The words came out of him in a stream and did not mean anything; he could hear that they did not mean anything, and could not stop them. Dwarven, and Common, and something in between. *Listen first,* he was saying. He heard himself say it. *Listen first, hinges and bolts — look — look second, marks at the — who picks it most — the plate, the plate round the —* and then other words, words that were not words, a long babbling string of them going out into the cold air of the room like water out of a cracked jug.

The gray man was shouting. "*He's confused!* It's a spell — he's under a spell — *Thane!*"

*Thane.*

He knew that word. He did not know where from. It was a good word. He held onto it.

The gray man had a wand in his hand. He pointed it, and Thane felt something come at him, something that wanted to close round him and hold him still, like a hand round a bird; and something in him that did not need a name to know a door when it saw one simply shut, and the thing went away again, and he did not even look at it.

He looked at his knife.

It was in his hand. The other one too. He did not know why. He turned the one in his right hand over, and looked at the edge of it in the light, and he did not know why, and he put it into his own leg.

It did not hurt much. It did not go deep. He watched it go in and he watched it come out, and he looked up, curious, at the room; and across the room the dwarf in gold and steel made a noise in his throat, and a red line opened on the dwarf's own leg, under the edge of his plate, in the same place, and began to bleed.

He looked at that a long time.

He did not understand it. It was a very interesting thing and he did not understand it at all.

The dwarf was coming at him. With his hands out. Not his hammer. His hands, open, the way you come at a horse that has got loose in a yard. And Thane did not want to be caught, he did not know why but he did not, and he stepped aside and the hands closed on air; and while he was stepping he put the knife into his leg again, a little higher, to see. Next to him the dwarf grunted, and the second red line opened on him just where the first had.

The bronze man came from one side, quick, very quick, and the dwarf from the other, and Thane went under the one and round the other, and they had nothing.

He was angry now. He did not know why. They would not let him be. They kept coming at him with their hands. He did not know them and they would not let him be.

He turned on the bronze man and put the knife in him.

He felt it go. In under the ribs, the way it went into anything; that was what it was for. The bronze man's face did not change. It was a very still face. Blood came out round the knife and down the cloth wrapped round his middle.

And next to him, again, the dwarf in gold and steel. A red bloom at his own side, under the plate. In the same place.

The bronze man hit him.

Twice. Hard. In the chest and the jaw, and the room went round, and he did not fall — and even through it, even with the room going round, some small part of him that was still watching noticed that the blows were strange. That they were *held.* That they came in hard as hammers and then, at the last instant, a hair before they landed, they *stopped being as hard as they could have been,* and landed only as hard as they had to.

He did not know why he noticed that. It meant something. He did not know what.

And the dwarf. The dwarf grunted, and touched his chest, and the dwarf's face went gray.

The dwarf lifted his hand, and gold came off it, warm, all over the room, and went into all of them — into the gray man and the bronze man and the woman by the door, and into Thane too, and the holes in his leg closed. He did not understand why the dwarf would do that. The dwarf put his hand on his own chest after it, and gold went through the dwarf too, and the dwarf's face came back a little from the gray.

And Thane went at him.

He did not decide to. He was angry, and the dwarf was there, and the knife was in his hand. Not that knife. The other one. The short heavy one that crossed behind the knuckles, that he had made himself, that he had sat a whole day on a stone floor to wake. He did not remember making it. He did not remember sitting. He knew it was his, and it was in his fist, and the dwarf's shield was up, and he went *inside* the shield the way he went inside anybody's shield, under the rim, into the gap, and put the punch dagger into the dwarf's side to the cross-guard.

The dwarf did not make a sound.

Blood came out down the gold and steel of him, a long dark rope of it, down his side and down his leg and onto the floor.

And the dwarf looked at him.

Not angry. Not afraid. The dwarf looked at him over the rim of a shield with blood running down his side, from a face Thane had known before he knew his own, and his eyes were full of the most terrible patient love, and he did not lift his hammer, and he did not step back, and he did not let go of whatever it was he was holding on to.

*Cousin,* said something, very far down.

---

He came back all at once.

Not like the last time. Not like surfacing. Like a shutter being thrown open on a bright day, so that the whole room was suddenly *there* in front of him at once, every edge of it, too sharp to look at.

The war room. Vang's table. Ice melting on the floor and papers everywhere, stiff with frost. The smell of blood.

His hand on the grip of the punch dagger, and the punch dagger in his cousin's side.

Korroc.

Thane let go of it.

He did not pull it out. He let go of it, the way you let go of a pan that has turned out to be hot, and it stayed where it was, and Korroc looked down at it, and then reached down and drew it out himself, slowly, with a grunt, and stood holding it, dripping.

Thane looked at his own hands.

They were wet. Both of them. He knew whose blood was on them. He knew it now, all of it, he knew every single thing — the leg, and the leg again, and the dwarf bleeding where he had bled; the bronze man, the knife in under the ribs; the hands coming at him open and him going round them, him *ducking his own cousin* like a boy ducking his mother's slap; the gold going over all of them, over him; and the shield, and the gap under the rim, and the cross-guard.

He knew it all. He had been there for all of it.

He had been there for all of it.

He had been *there.*

Something went out of his legs. He did not fall. He stood in the middle of the war room with his empty wet hands and a cold going through him that had nothing to do with Caleth's spell, a cold that came up out of the floor of him and had been there a long time, since the deep rooms under Drezen and a demon's laugh in his own mouth, waiting for exactly this, and found it.

*Not again. Not —*

He did not finish it. He could not afford to finish it. He did the thing his hands knew how to do, which was the only thing that was left: he put one wet hand flat on his cousin's side, over the hole, and prayed. Short, and ugly, and all the way down. *Shut it. Shut it. Let it be shut.* And the light went out of him and into Korroc and he felt the wound take it, felt the edges of it come in, felt the bleeding slow under his palm.

"Cousin," he said. "Cousin, I — I'm sorry, I'm — Korroc, I —"

He did not have the rest. There was no rest. He had never in his life called his cousin by his name.

He went to Nageru. The monk was standing where he had been, quite still, with one hand pressed to his side and blood coming out between the fingers. Thane put his hand over the monk's hand and did it again, the same short ugly prayer, and the blood stopped.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I didn't — I'm sorry."

Nageru looked down at him.

"Brother," he said gently. "I know."

Thane turned back to his cousin. Korroc was putting the punch dagger away for him. That was what he was doing. He had wiped it on his own sleeve, and was sliding it back into the sheath on Thane's belt, carefully, the way you put a knife away for a child.

"Cousin." Korroc's voice was rough. He cleared it. "Cousin. Listen to me."

Thane could not look at him.

"*Look* at me."

Thane looked at him.

"It was a spell," said Korroc. "The thing in red put it on you. I watched it. You'd no more say in it than in the weather." He put his hand on Thane's shoulder, the big hand, heavy, the hand that went out over the dead to give them the words. "You weren't attacking anybody. You couldn't help it. There's nothing you could've done."

*I was there,* Thane thought. *I was there for all of it. I watched.*

He did not say it. He had not said it the last time either.

And then he understood the other thing. It came to him slowly, out of the pieces, the way a lock comes, one pin at a time. The leg. The dwarf's leg. The side, and the dwarf's side. The bronze man's fists, landing, and the dwarf's face going gray.

"You didn't drop it," he said.

Korroc did not pretend not to know what he meant. "No."

"The link. When I — when I was stabbing my own leg like a madman, you felt every — and when Nageru hit me, you took half of —" He stopped. "You could have dropped it. You could have cut me off it. I'd have bled for myself."

"Aye," said Korroc. "I could've."

"Why didn't you?"

His cousin looked at him a long moment.

"Because you're on it," he said. As if that were the whole of the answer. As if there had never been any other.

Thane looked down at the floor. Walls lie. Floors don't. There was blood on the floor between their boots, a good deal of it, and he could not have told whose.

He took out his cloth, and his mother's knife, and began to wipe it. *No Stonevein has ever sheathed a wet blade.* He had wiped it after demons and men and things he had no name for; he had wiped it, in the deep rooms under Drezen, when it had touched nothing but the air round his cousin. He wiped it now. The blood that came off on the cloth was Nageru's. He wiped his father's knife, and that was clean, and he wiped it anyway. The punch dagger was in its sheath, where Korroc had put it, and he took it out and wiped it too, and the blood on that was his cousin's, and he wiped it a long time.

Across the room, by the door, Arueshalae was watching him.

She had not come in. She had stayed in the doorway all through it, he realized, bow down, out of everybody's way, with her back to the passage and her eyes on the room. She was looking at him now. Not with pity. With something quieter than pity and a good deal more frightening, a look he could not read, as though she were looking at a thing she recognized from a long way off.

Then she looked away, out into the passage, and was only a woman keeping watch.

---

He felt a hand on his shoulder.

Not Korroc's. Lighter, and cooler, rough under the fingers with the stone that was not stone. Caleth. The half-elf had come up beside him without Thane hearing him, which did not often happen, and he stood there with his gray hand on Thane's shoulder and his eyes on Thane's face.

"I understand," Caleth said.

That was all. Not *it was not your fault*, which was what people said; not *don't think of it*, which was what people said when they wanted you to stop thinking of it in front of them. *I understand.* Two words. And under them, so far under that Thane would not have heard it at all if he had not been standing in the bottom of himself just then, where the sound carries, something else. Something that had weight. As if the half-elf had said a great deal more than he had said, and only two words of it had come out into the air.

Thane looked at him.

Caleth's face did not change. His hand stayed on Thane's shoulder a moment longer, and pressed, once. Then he took it away.

*That's another one,* Thane thought. He did not know what it was. He put it with the others, under the floorboard, with the mark that was not quite the elf's and the face at the bedside and *I have been told*. He was beginning to have a great deal under that floorboard.

"There is something," Caleth said, "that I need you to look at."

---

It was the papers.

Most of them had gone everywhere, flung off the table by the cold and lying all round the room stiff with rime. But some had not moved. Thane saw that as soon as Caleth pointed — a short stack at the near end of the table, held down by nothing at all, lying exactly where they had lain before the ice went off, as though the blast had gone round them.

"Don't read them," said Caleth. He was standing well back from the table himself, his face turned half away. "There are runes. On the top sheet, there, and I think on the next one. I looked at them once and looked away. I think —" he chose the words — "I think it is a trap. Something that goes off if the paper is read."

Thane looked at the stack.

Not at the writing. At the stack. The edge of it, the way the sheets lay, the corners. The paper was good, heavy, cream-colored. There was writing on the top sheet in a close hand, and in among the writing, in the margin and down the middle, there were marks that were not writing: shapes, curling, drawn in a darker ink than the rest. His eye wanted to go to them. His eye wanted very badly to go to them, the way the eye goes into the maze on the walls of the ivory room.

He did not let it.

He was glad. That was the strange thing, and it came to him as he knelt down to bring his eyes level with the edge of the table: he was *glad*. Here was a thing that needed doing, and only he could do it, and it needed his hands, and his hands were his again. He had never in his life been so glad of a lock.

*Listen first.*

He listened. Paper does not make sounds. But he listened anyway, because that was how it started, and the room went quiet round him.

*Look second.*

He looked at the runes the way he would look at the face of a lock: not reading it, not asking what it said, only looking at its *shape.* At how it was made. And a rune, it turned out, was made like anything else. There were strokes in it that went somewhere and strokes that came back, and places where a line met a line and closed, the way a ward closes in a lock, the way a door closes in a frame. A rune that was not closed did not hold anything. He did not know how he knew that. He knew it the way he knew a lock with a missing pin would not turn. The thing wanted *finishing*. It wanted to be read, and in the reading, to close, and in closing, to go off.

So it must not be allowed to close.

*Touch third.*

He reached across the table for the inkwell that had been holding down a map, and drew it to him, and wet the tip of his smallest finger in it.

He did not look at the rune. He looked at the line next to it. At one particular place, at the bottom of the curling shape, where one stroke came round to meet another and very nearly touched it and then, at the last, did; he saw the meeting out of the side of his eye, and kept his eye on the side, and put his inked fingertip down on the paper just there. Pressed. Drew it, slowly, a quarter of an inch across the place where the two lines joined, so that the join was a smudge of black and the two lines were not two lines meeting any more but two lines and a blot.

He did the same on the second sheet. And then, because there was a third mark half-hidden under the edge of the second, on that one too.

Then he sat back on his heels.

Nothing happened. That was the whole point of it. Nothing happened, and went on happening, and the papers lay there with three black smudges on them like a careless clerk's.

He wiped his finger on his cloth.

"It's done," he said.

Caleth let out a breath. He came to the table and looked down at the top sheet, carefully, then less carefully, and then began to read.

Thane watched his face. It went harder as he read. He turned a page, and another, and pulled the frost-stiff ones off the floor and read those too, and laid them out on the table side by side.

"Names," he said, half to himself. "Agents. Hidden ones." He turned a page. "Tunnels. Under — I don't know where that is. Here. Shrines, hidden ones, in places I —" A page. "Army movements. Ours and theirs." Another. He went still over it. "Maps. And schedules." He looked up at the rest of them. "For the elixirs. Where they go. When."

Nobody said anything.

Caleth gathered them up, every sheet, smudged and frosted and clean, into one careful stack, and squared its edges against the table.

"These go to Irabeth," he said.

"Aron first," said Thane. His voice came out rougher than he meant. "He told us. Back to him before anywhere else."

Caleth looked at him. Then nodded. "Aron first. And then to Irabeth."

He put them in the bag.

Korroc had got out his wand, the plain old one with the leather wrap, and was standing in the corner running it over his own side where the punch dagger had gone in, slowly, with his eyes shut. Thane watched him do it and did not go to him, because if he went to him he would have to say something, and there was nothing.

---

The second pair of doors on the hallway opened on a barracks.

Nine beds, along the east wall and the west. A big wardrobe against the south. Blankets pulled square, boots under some of the beds, a cup on a sill. Nothing else; nothing that mattered. Thane went through it anyway, because he went through everything, and at the end of it he stood in the middle of the room and counted beds.

Nine.

Seven men in the room with the dummies. Two at the table with the coloxus. Nine.

Which came out even, and left over the one who had gone out of a room without anybody seeing, and nowhere to put him. He filed that, too.

---

The last pair of doors stood in the north wall of the hallway, and Thane put his ear to them and heard it at once.

A voice. A woman's. Low and even and steady, going on and on in a cadence he knew, the cadence of a thing being said that is not meant for anyone to hear, that is meant for something else to listen to. Under it, faint, a ringing. Metal on metal.

He looked back.

"Casting," he said.

"Let's go," said Caleth.

And then, before he pushed the doors, Thane did a thing he had never done before in his life. He turned round and looked at them. All of them. One after another. Korroc, with his shield up and his face still a little gray. Nageru, with blood dry on his wraps and his hands open. Caleth, rough-skinned, Radiance up. Arueshalae at the back, an arrow on her string.

He looked at each face until he knew it. Until the name was there, under his hand, where he could reach it.

*Cousin. Nageru. The half-elf. Her.*

Then he pushed the doors.

---

A forge. That was the first thing, and the second thing was that it was not right.

It sat in the northwest corner, a forge, with an anvil beside it, and it was burning — Thane could see the glow of it, a hard white-red — and there was no smoke. None. Not up the flue, not out of the fire. A fire with no smoke is a fire that is not burning anything, and he did not like it. A weapon rack against the north wall with good weapons on it, clean, oiled, gleaming. Another rack further south, with weapons on it that were not good: notched, cracked, a hilt hanging loose, a blade bent a hand's width out of true. Two long tables, east and west, piled with tools and jars and bundles of herbs and books and things he had no names for. Chests under the western table.

And a woman.

She stood at the eastern table with her back to them, in long black robes, with a sword laid across the table in front of her, and her hands moving over it. The blade under her hands was glowing. Not the hot white of the forge. Something else, darker; a sick purplish light that ran along the steel from the hilt to the point like oil running down a gutter, and sank in.

"She's enhancing weapons," said Caleth flatly. "With magic."

The woman turned round.

She was not old and not young. She had a hard narrow face, and her eyes went over the five of them in the doorway and came back to Thane, at the front, and they were not afraid. They were *furious*. The fury of a smith interrupted in the middle of a weld.

"Who are you?" she said. "And what do you think you are doing?"

Thane did not answer her. He had his knives out, and he went.

He went straight across the room at her, and he was at the table before she had finished the last word, and his mother's blade went in. Her eyes went very wide. She stepped back out of it, quick, quicker than he had thought she would be, with her hands already moving —

And something came out of the air behind him.

He felt it before he saw it. Felt it the way you feel a door open at your back on a cold night: a breath of air, a change in the room. And a *smell*, a smell so bad it had weight, the reek of a swamp that something had died in and gone on dying in for a month. He knew that smell. He had smelled it in a ruined fortress a week's ride west, in the dark, with a bell over his head.

He turned.

It filled the doorway. It was a great squat toad of a thing, warty, gray-green, bigger than a cart horse, with a wide mouth full of teeth and long arms that ended in claws, and it was standing square in the doors they had come in by with its back to his friends and its little eyes on him.

*Hezrou.*

It came at him. Bite and claw and claw, all at once, and he turned the bite with his father's knife and slapped the first claw away with his mother's, and the second came in under both of them and laid his side open again, along the same ribs the templar's sword had found. He grunted. It reached for him with both hands, to pull him in against that mouth, and he went out from under its arms like water out of a fist, and was clear.

Behind it, he saw Nageru.

The monk had come up at its back while it was busy with Thane, and his fists were *alight*. A clean hard holy light, the color of morning, flaring off his knuckles; and he set his feet and struck, and struck, and struck, five times, so fast they were one sound. The hezrou screamed. It began to turn round to him.

Caleth came in at Nageru's shoulder, Radiance reaching past the monk the way it had reached past Korroc on the stair, for the thing's turning face. The hezrou knocked the glaive aside with one great arm. And again, with the other. And the third time it did not, and the white blade bit in deep, and the thing screamed again.

Korroc had come in last. Thane saw his cousin set his boots on the stone of the floor and *settle*, the way he did, as though the floor had come up into him; and then the hammer came round twice, and the hezrou's whole bulk shuddered with it.

Thane turned back to the woman.

She had gone round the end of the table, putting it between them. He went over it. Both knives, *in* and *in* and *in*; and the fourth she twisted away from, and it caught only cloth. She was bleeding. She was not done. She lifted a hand and pointed it at him like a woman pointing at a dog that has got into the kitchen, and light came off it, three darts of it, and took him in the chest and the side and the shoulder. He felt the sting of each one and felt Korroc take half of each.

The hezrou brought both fists down on Nageru. Nageru was not there. Nageru struck back, holy light, and the thing staggered, and stayed up.

"Down!" said Caleth.

Thane went down.

The fireball came past the hezrou in the doorway, over Thane's head, and burst at the back of the room among the tables and the jars. The heat of it came down on him like a hand. He was rolling already and it went over him and past him and did not touch him at all. When he came up the woman was on the far side of the room with her robe smoking, alive, having thrown herself flat behind the western table; she had dodged most of it, too.

And Korroc said something in Dwarven, low, and the boots on his feet woke.

Thane heard them. He always heard them, a kind of humming in the stone. And his cousin went from slow to fast in no time at all, and the hammer came round once, and twice, and three times, *crack crack crack*, into the hezrou's warty side and its shoulder and its great flat head; and the thing went thin and dark all at once, and there was nothing in the doorway but the stink of it, already going.

Thane did not wait. He went round the end of the western table.

The woman saw him come. She was getting up off the floor. She had her hand half raised.

He put the knife in.

She sat down against the table leg, slowly, the way the soldier had sat down against the training dummy; and looked at him; and then did not look at anything.

---

He wiped both blades.

It was very quiet after. The forge burned in its corner without smoke. Korroc was breathing hard. Nageru stood in the doorway where the hezrou had been, with the holy light going out of his hands, and the light left them bronze and bare and bloody, and very still.

Thane looked at the woman a while. Black robes. Hands that moved when she fought. The weapons on her table.

"Blackfire," he said. "You think?"

Caleth was looking at her too. "I think," he said slowly, "that she is what we were told they were. I don't know that she is."

Neither did Thane. It sat badly with him, not knowing; he had spent his whole life naming people. *That one, and that one.* This house would not give him its names. It gave him nothing but faces, and fewer of them than there ought to have been.

Korroc had gone to the weapon racks.

The good one first: he looked along it with a smith's eye, swords and axes and a long-hafted mace, clean, sharp, and shook his head slowly. Then the damaged one, the notched blades and the bent ones, and his face went harder.

"She was mending them," he said. "These. And then —" he jerked his chin at the eastern table, where the sword she had been working still lay, its blade dull now, with the faint sick purple sheen still in the steel — "doing *that* to them. Making them unholy."

Nageru came in from the door and looked at the racks.

"Korroc and Thane could turn those," he said, "on the forge at Drezen."

Korroc nodded. But he was not looking at the weapons any more. He had picked up a tool from the western table — a pair of tongs, small, fine, the jaws filed to a shape Thane did not know — and he was turning it over in his hands. He weighed it. He opened and closed it, slowly, and listened to the pivot. He held it to the light of the forge and looked along it.

"These tools," said his cousin, and his voice had gone very quiet, "were made by a master."

He went along the table, picking them up one after another: a file, a graver, a little hammer no longer than a finger, a set of punches in a roll of leather. He held each one as though it might break, and set it down again as though it might be hurt.

"I'm taking them," he said. "Back with us. All of them." He looked round at the rest of them. "I'll not leave a master's work in a place like this. They'll not mind whose hand they're in, so long as it's a good one."

Nobody argued with him.

Everything went in the bag. The tools, in their leather. The ruined weapons, and the good ones, and the one on the table with the purple in it, which Caleth wrapped in a cloth before he would touch. The chests from under the western table. The books and jars and bundles. All of it, into the gray bag, which took it all and was still not full, and which Caleth tied shut at last and slung across his body under his coat, as though it weighed nothing.

---

They stopped for a little after that. Not long. Long enough.

Nobody said they were stopping. They simply did. Korroc sat down on the edge of the western table with his hammer across his knees and his eyes shut, and Caleth stood at the forge with his back to the room, looking into the smokeless white of it as though it were a page he could not read. Arueshalae took the doorway, without being asked. Nageru sat down on the floor with his legs folded under him, in the middle of the room, and breathed.

Thane sat down on the floor beside him.

He did not know why. He had meant to go and check the door. He sat down instead, with his back against the leg of the table and his knives across his knees, and for a while neither of them said anything.

The monk's wraps were dark with blood at the side. Thane looked at it.

"You pulled them," he said.

Nageru did not ask what. He did not open his eyes.

"Your punches. When I — just now. You hit me twice and you pulled them both."

"Yes."

Thane looked at the floor. The maze was not on this floor. It was only stone. He was very glad of it.

"That's twice," he said.

It came out of him before he had decided to say it. He heard it go, and could not get it back, and sat there with it in the air between them.

*Twice.* In the deep rooms under Drezen, with a demon in him laughing in his own voice and his mother's and his father's blades going at his cousin high and low, and Nageru stepping in front of it and putting him down with two blows, two blows that came in like hammers and at the last instant were not. And now. Two blows again, and again not.

He did not say the rest of it. He did not say *I was there for that one too. I watched you do it. I watched my hands.* It was right there behind his teeth. It had been right there behind his teeth for — he did not know how long. Weeks. Longer. And he could feel the shape of the words in his mouth, and he sat there with his hands on his knives and could not move his tongue to make them.

Nageru opened his eyes.

He did not look at Thane. He looked at the forge, at the white heat with no smoke, and was quiet so long that Thane thought he was not going to answer at all.

"Yes," he said. "Twice."

That was all. He shut his eyes again.

And Thane understood, sitting there on the floor of a dead woman's forge in the house of a thing made of worms, that the aasimar had heard all of it. The words he had said and the ones he had not. That the door was standing open, and the monk had seen it standing open, and had not walked through it. Had not *pushed*. Had only said *yes*, and *twice*, and closed his eyes, and left the door exactly where it was, open, for Thane to come through or not.

He did not.

*Later,* he thought. He knew what kind of word that was. He had used it before.

But it was nearer, this time. He could feel how near. A door you have stood in front of twice is not the same door it was the first time.

---

He got up after a while and went to the hall.

He told himself it was to check it, and it was. He stood in the wide passage outside the forge doors with his hand on the frame — he did not notice he had put it there; his hand went to frames the way other men's went to their hats — and looked up the length of it and down. Nothing. The kitchen door. The barracks doors, and the war room's single one. The doors of the room with the dummies, away at the end. And the first pair of doors, still standing open the way they had left them, on the stair going down into the dark.

He went and stood at the top of it.

*Listen first.*

Cold air came up the stair, steady, the way it comes up out of a deep place that goes a long way further down. Nothing on it. No voices, no feet. No sound at all.

But there was a man in this house who had gone out of a room without anybody seeing him go. There were nine beds and nine dead soldiers, and a forge with no smoke, and a bed that nobody had ever lain in, and a stone box full of worms that his cousin had broken to gravel. Every room in the house so far had had something in it, and none of them had had him.

Somewhere in this place a man was telling something what he had seen.

Somewhere in this place, Vang was listening.

Thane stood on the top step with the cold coming up past him and his wiped knives in his hands, and looked down into the dark, and found — for the first time since he could remember, at the top of any stair — that he was not entirely sure he knew whose hands they were.

He looked at them until he was.

Then he went back to tell the others they should be moving.
