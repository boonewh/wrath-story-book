# Who Has Business Inside
*Chapter Fourteen — The False Credential*

The wand was nearly dead, and Korroc held it up to the firelight the way a man holds up a coin he's not sure is good.

Thane watched from across the fire, whetstone going slow along his mother's blade. He wasn't in the conversation. He rarely was, this late — the four of them had learned the shape of each other's silences well enough that he could sit at the edge of the light and be counted present without spending a word. Caleth had asked something about charges, about the reserve wand Korroc kept and never used because it was almost spent, and now the half-elf was turning the near-dead one over in his long fingers with the particular stillness he got when a thought had walked into him and sat down.

"Watch," Caleth said, to no one.

He cupped his hand around the wand. Closed his eyes. And Thane, who read hands the way other men read faces, saw the half-elf's fingers glow — not the wand first, the *hand*, light welling up under the skin like a lamp behind a shutter — and then the wand caught it, drank it, the wood going warm-bright for the length of a held breath.

Caleth handed it back. "Try it now."

Korroc thumbed it. Something in the dwarf's face came apart and reassembled. "You gave it back charge." He said it flat, the way you'd say *the river's running uphill*. "Caleth. You gave it back *charge.*"

"I funneled some in." The half-elf shrugged, and it was a real shrug, an *it's-nothing* shrug, and Thane believed it right up until Aravashnial arrived.

Aravashnial came out of the dark the way he did everything now — without asking the ground's permission. The Riftwarden's eyes found the wand and sharpened on it, and something moved behind that weathered elven face that Thane didn't have a name for and didn't like not having a name for.

"Let me see that." Not a request. He took the wand, turned it, and turned that same attention on Caleth, and it was the attention of a locksmith who has just heard a bolt he built click in a way it should not click. "How did you do this?"

Caleth spread his hands. "I realized that if I concentrate I can push magic into things that run on charges. That's all."

*That's all.* Thane's whetstone paused a half-stroke. Because the elf didn't move for a long moment, and when someone who has forgotten more magic than most cities will ever hold goes still like that over a *that's all* — that is a door with more behind it than the frame shows.

"We should talk later," Aravashnial said.

And that was it. Aravashnial drifted back to his tent, and Caleth watched him go with a face Thane couldn't get behind, and Thane filed the whole thing where he filed things — under the floorboard of his attention, marked *unopened*, to be looked at when there was time to look. There was never time to look. That was the war.

He wiped the whetstone's dust off his mother's blade and went back to the edge of not-quite-talking.

He got perhaps an hour of it before the prisoners started screaming.

---

It came from the cages — one voice at first, then the panicked animal murmur of a dozen men who have decided the thing happening to one of them is coming for all of them.

Thane was up before Korroc, but Korroc was moving before Thane had finished standing, and that was the way of them. They reached the cages together. A soldier had a torch high. Inside, on the churned mud, a man lay bowed backward like a strung bow, heels and skull grinding down, every muscle in him at war with every other. Foam at the mouth. Broken glass beside his hand, catching the torch in small wet stars.

"Food's passed through the bars," the guard was saying, fast, the way men talk when they need you to know it wasn't them. "They're searched, sir, every one, hands and mouths and seams—"

"The top's open," Thane said. He didn't look up from the glass. *Look at the floor first, lad. Walls lie. Floors don't.* The floor said: the shard had come from above and outside. The floor said: someone had thrown it in. "You can't search the sky."

Korroc had the door opened. He put the rest of the prisoners to the far wall with three Dwarven words and a look, and went in, and Thane went in a half-step behind and to the side — not to help Korroc, Korroc didn't need help with a seizing man — but to stand in the gap between his cousin's broad back and eleven desperate strangers, mother's blade low and easy, the wedding-knife reversed along his left forearm. A doorway. He'd made himself a doorway, and no one was crossing it.

Korroc knelt. Sniffed. His nostrils flared and his mouth went thin.

"Shadow Blood."

The name meant nothing kind. Korroc laid a hand on the man's chest and the golden thing that lived in him now — the wardstone's gift, the warmth that could reach into another body and *take* what was wrong there into himself for a moment and give back health — the golden thing went to work. The bowed spine eased. The heels stopped grinding. The prisoner dragged in one enormous shuddering breath and lay still, weeping the boneless way of men who did not choose to.

Sosiel arrived with soldiers and a litter and took him toward the infirmary tents. Nageru had come up in the meantime and was crouched at the bars, and the aasimar had a way of gentling frightened men that Thane could admire without ever once being able to copy. The prisoners talked to him. No one had the drug, they swore. The man had just — found it. Laying there. On the ground.

"So," Thane said, wiping nothing off a clean blade because his hands wanted something to do, "we're hunting invisible men now."

Korroc's head came up.

Thane knew that look. It was the look his cousin got the exact half-second before the ground told him something. And then Korroc swore — loud, ugly, three generations of Dwarven in it — and said, "*Thane.* It's a distraction. The tents. Now. Go check our things. Make sure nothing's missing. Nothing's broken."

Thane was already gone.

---

He didn't run. Running is for men who want to be seen arriving. He *slid* — the low fast slide from cover to cover that the wardstone's fire had sharpened into something close to unseen, the shadows themselves seeming to loosen and let him through. Between the wagons. Under the mess awning. Past the picket line, where a horse turned one ear and decided he wasn't worth waking for.

Their tent had been opened. Not the flap — the *possessions.* His pack tipped, Korroc's spare kit strewn, Nageru's things gone through with the particular carelessness of someone who had been looking for one specific thing and had not cared what he broke on the way. Thane crouched inside the ruin of it and made himself breathe. The blueprints. Thorek's blueprints, his father's hand, his father's first signature at the corner of a citadel his father had helped dream — those rode in Caleth's spellbook, on Caleth's body, never here. *Good.* Whoever this was, they didn't know to want them. Yet.

He backed out low.

And from the deep shadow along the tent-row, he watched a knife open Aravashnial's tent.

Not a hand holding a knife. Just the knife — a slit walking down the canvas six feet from where the elf surely still slept, the blade doing its work with nobody attached to it. Thane's stomach did the cold slow turn it did when the world showed him a door that shouldn't be there and was.

He didn't shout. Shouting is a gift you give the enemy: *here I am, and here is my exact intent.* He worked his hands and his lips through the small water-calling the old faith had left in them, and above the empty air where the knife hung he made a fist of water and dropped it.

The figure *sheeted* into being under the soaking — a body-shaped hole in the dark, dripping, small.

Then a streak of green spat out of that dripping shape and hit Thane in the shoulder, and the acid of it ate through his coat and bit the meat beneath, and the world went white at the edges for a heartbeat. He'd been made. He rode the pain the way he rode all of it — banked it, fed it into the small clean flame that had been burning in his chest since a hillside in the Fourth Crusade, and let the flame make him *patient* instead of stupid.

The figure was visible now. Not fully — it had backed into the blackest slice of shadow between two tents — but visible enough. Short. Halfling-short, or gnome-short. And Thane, who had carried one unproven name in his teeth for three chapters of this war, felt the tumblers of a very old lock begin, at last, to turn.

He went in.

He didn't lead with the point. He led with the pommels — mother's blade and father's knife both turned haft-forward, because he wanted this one *breathing.* You cannot question the dead. The figure dodged the first strike, ducked the second, and got a hand on his wrist and spoke a word, and something cold and wrong washed up his arm toward his heart trying to open a door in him — and Thane, who had spent his life deciding who got to come inside, shut it. *No. You've no business here.* The spell slid off him like rain off slate.

Then a warhammer the size of a barrel came down out of the dark.

Korroc had arrived — and not on his short legs, because his short legs and a crowd had lost him the race; Caleth had put a hand on him and *folded* the two of them across the whole camp in a single step, the half-elf materializing beside the tent-row with Radiance already lit and the dwarf swinging before his boots had settled. The hammer caught the small figure across the shoulders and drove it staggering.

Aravashnial's voice came sharp from inside the canvas.

And then Nageru was simply *there* — the aasimar had heard, somehow, across the whole width of camp, and had run, and running for Nageru now meant crossing distance faster than the eye could grant it — and his fist took the figure across the jaw and sent it crashing down, stunned, dazzled, done.

Behind Aravashnial, as he walked calmly out of his own tent, the tent *detonated* — a flat crack meant for a sleeping man's back — and the Riftwarden ducked it without seeming to hurry, untouched, and did not so much as turn to look at what he'd escaped.

Caleth spoke a word and the small figure locked rigid, held in the air of Caleth's will.

Thane crouched, breathing, shoulder screaming, and turned the soaked hood back with the point of his mother's blade.

"Nurah," he said.

He'd known. He'd known and been told, gently, over and over, that knowing and proving are different rooms with a locked door between them. He reached for his cords and began, without any pleasure at all, to bind the hands of the woman he had been right about.

Boots. Torches. Irabeth Tirabade came through the tent-row like a portcullis dropping, half-orc jaw set, sword already out. "*What is going on in my camp.*" It wasn't a question and Thane didn't treat it as one. She took in the ruined tent, the char, the bound halfling, the four of them standing over her — and her face did the thing a good commander's face does, which is decide everything in a breath and show none of it.

"Command tent. All of you. *Now.*"

---

They set Nurah in a chair. Caleth let the holding go, and she sagged into being a small tired woman again, sullen, dripping, her round face closed like a shop after dark.

Nageru put his own face down level with hers, and there was no gentleness in it now, none of the thing he'd used on the caged men — only a terrible earnest need to *understand.* "Why. What are you doing."

Irabeth stood over them both. "You were sent to us by the Queen. By Galfrey herself, as one of her own representatives."

Thane stood by the tent-wall where he could see the door and everyone in front of it, and thumbed the edge of his father's knife, and said nothing, and watched Nurah decide how much lie was left worth telling.

She decided none. She sighed — a whole life came out in it — and nodded, and the nod aged her.

"I've been working for Staunton," she said, and then she slumped, and told them the rest.

Thane listened the way he searched a room: for the load-bearing pieces, and for the seams. She'd come to the Worldwound at twenty, she said, youngest of some Isger lord's train — an ambitious brute named Axilar who'd dragged her to the Fourth Crusade as scribe and tactician, into the very failure to take Drezen that had eaten Thane's father and uncle. *This ground,* Thane thought. *She lost her innocence on this exact ground the same season we lost our fathers to it.* He filed the coincidence and did not soften toward her for it. He'd learned that lesson young: the same fire that tempers one blade cracks another, and the crack is a choice.

She spoke of years in Isger and Cheliax before that, of growing *used* to fiends, then curious, then — as she said it her closed face opened just enough to show what was inside, and Thane wished it hadn't — *admiring.* Of the night tiefling assassins and their demon-led pack tore her master's legion apart in front of her and killed the man who owned her, and how she had stood in the blood of it and felt not horror but *release.* She'd pledged herself on the spot. Been passed up the chain of that horror — a shadow demon, a place called Raliscrad, and at the top of it something she called Minagho who had looked into her ruined soul and seen a use for it, and sent her back across the line wearing the crusade's own colors. A saboteur. A doorway held open for years, letting rot in one careful piece at a time, and no one had ever once thought to check whether her credentials were forged.

Thane felt his jaw tighten. That was the part that lived under his ribs and would for a while. Not the demons. The *years.* The devout men she'd arranged the deaths of, one clean unsuspected knife at a time, while sitting at their fires and laughing at their jokes and being — she said this almost proudly — a lucky, generous, gifted traveling companion. *Much like you four,* she did not quite say, and did not need to.

Getting placed at the Queen's elbow had cost her handlers months. When Galfrey had asked her to go and help these four jumped-up mythic knights, she'd been *annoyed* — to leave the Queen's side, so close to the great prize. And then she'd understood. Four crusaders lifted up by a wardstone's golden fire were a bigger thing to bring down than one aging monarch. Her masters in Baphomet's church had given her the assignment she called her masterwork.

*The undoing of us,* Thane thought. *That's what she calls the four of us. An assignment.*

Across the tent, Anevia stood in the shadow of the entrance, arms crossed, watching the confession with a face Thane had learned to read over hard months. She'd told him, back before Drezen was even in sight — *Something might be strange with Nurah. Watch your back.* She wasn't gloating now. She caught Thane's eye once, one flat exchange between two people who make their living being right about others and taking no joy in it, and she gave him the smallest nod. Not *I told you so.* Just: *Yes. This one. You saw it too.* He gave it back. That was the whole conversation and it was enough.

"Where is Staunton," Thane said. First words he'd spent since the name.

She flicked her chin north. "In the citadel."

"Do you answer to Staunton?" Nageru asked.

"No." Almost dismissive. "I have a handler."

"And?"

"I check in," she said, "when I've either finished you off, or you've gotten into the citadel."

Caleth leaned in, Radiance dimmed at his side. "Who's your contact."

She slumped further, and for the first time something crossed her face that wasn't sullenness — something Thane read, uneasily, as *fear.* "Her name is Kiranda."

"How do we know her."

Nurah's mouth curled. "She's a succubus. You'll know her."

"Of course," Thane said, to the tent-pole, dry as old bone.

Irabeth had heard enough. "We haven't the men to send her back to the Queen — not with the assault at dawn. We keep her. Under guard, day and night, until she can be returned for judgment."

"She's a caster." Korroc, low, reasonable, the voice of a man who has been surprised by prisoners before. "She turns invisible. How do you hold a thing like that in a war camp?"

"I've watched her cast." Irabeth's tusks caught the lamplight. "She *sings* to do it. She needs her voice and she needs her components. We've taken the components. We'll bind her mouth and we'll keep it bound, and there will be eyes on her every hour of the dark." She looked down at Nurah with something Thane thought was almost pity and definitely wasn't mercy. "You'll not sing your way out of my camp, advisor."

They gagged her. Thane watched the cloth go across the round tired face and felt none of the satisfaction he had thought he would feel. He'd been *right.* Being right, he was learning, was a cold room. He'd wanted the door to this thing shut for so long, and now it was shut, and behind it was only more war.

He went and let Sosiel look at his shoulder, and he did not sleep much.

---

Morning came grey off the Ahari, and the map of Drezen lay open on the command table like a body on a slab.

Aron Kir set his blunt finger on it — four times, one after another, at the four corners. "Watchtowers. Still manned. Still active." He tapped each in turn. "The army moves up the bridge and these four tear it apart from above before it ever reaches the wall. You'd be chopped front and top at once. They come down first, or we don't come at all. Small job. Strike team."

"Manned by what," Nageru asked.

Aravashnial answered by opening his book. He turned it and slid it across, and there on the page in the Riftwarden's careful hand was a thing with the head of a goat, eyes lit from inside, and where its feet should have been there were *flames* — hooves of fire, the ground beneath them scorched in the drawing. "Brimoraks," the Riftwarden said. "You have not met these. They are not pleasant. One garrisons each tower — but a brimorak can open a rift and bring more."

"And they breathe boiling blood," Aron added, helpfully.

"They do." The Riftwarden's eyes turned, unerringly, to Caleth. "Fire does not hurt them. *And neither does electricity.*" He let that sit on the half-elf a moment. "But they mislike the cold."

"Mislike," Caleth said.

"It *hurts* them." Aravashnial closed the book. "One more thing. If you've warded yourself against fire — even their touch will burn through it. Their bodies are alight. Don't lay hands on one bare and think your ward will save you."

Behind the towers, Aron said, was the tiefling army entire, waiting on the flat. Take the towers and the road opened. Fumble at the towers and the road became a slaughter-chute.

Caleth had gone quiet in the way that meant he was building something. "How tall?"

"Forty feet, near enough."

"And the span between them?"

Aron spread his hands — he didn't know the yards. But the map, he swore, was true to scale. Caleth walked two fingers across it, measuring proportions off the drawn distances, lips moving. When he looked up he had it.

"I give everyone the walls," he said. "You'll climb them like flat ground, straight up. We take the farthest tower first, from the back where the army can't see the base, and once we're on top I fold us from roof to roof." A flicker of the old worry — the miser's care he took now with his dimension-doors, hoarding them like a man who has learned exactly how many he gets in a day. "One tower's spaced too wide for a fold to reach from the others. That's the one we walk up. "We go after dark."

Thane looked at the four little marks on the map and thought about his father's hand, somewhere under Caleth's coat, having drawn walls very like these. Then he put the thought back under the floorboard where it lived, and went to sharpen his blades.

---

They went out under a moon that couldn't decide to show.

Nageru drew the fire-ward over himself first — a thing that shimmered and faded, boiling-blood insurance. Korroc's plate began, within a hundred paces, to *creak* — the old betrayal, the forge-hot dwarf and his honest steel that had never once learned to lie. They didn't fight it. They just went slow, and Thane went ahead of all of it, out where the creak became one more small night-noise, and made himself the quiet part of the dark.

Three times a tiefling wandered out from the tower-line, drawn by the sound of a dwarf in armor pretending to be a breeze.

Three times Thane reached it first.

The first never saw Korroc at all — Thane came up out of its blind quarter, mother's blade going in under the ear where the neck meets the skull, one hand over the mouth, and lowered it to the ground like setting down something fragile. He wiped both blades on the dead thing's rag of a cloak before he moved on. *No Stonevein has ever sheathed a wet blade.* The second turned at the last instant and got a look at him and got nothing else. The third he took from behind entirely, and it folded without a word, and he cleaned the daggers and moved up, and behind him Korroc's creaking crept along in the dark like a secret the night had agreed to keep.

They reached the base of the farthest tower.

Caleth touched each of them — a cool tingle, and the wall stopped being a wall and became a road that happened to go up. Thane went first because going first was his. He climbed the forty feet with the wardstone's ease in his hands and feet, quick and silent, and stopped just below the lip. Listened. *Hinges and bolts speak.* He heard one thing moving up there. One weight, one slow patrol-drag of something heavy across stone. He edged his eyes over the crenel.

One demon. Head of a goat, eyes like two coals, and where it walked the stone smoked under its hooves.

He edged back down and held up one finger to the faces climbing below him.

Caleth came up past him — the mage first, this time, because the mage had the opener. Caleth's head cleared the lip and his hand came up and five bolts of raw force leapt off his fingers and hammered into the brimorak's chest, and the thing spun, screaming, coals blazing, trying to find the direction the pain had come from.

Then it was a fight, and a fight is a fight.

Nageru came over the wall and drove a fist into it, and the demon staggered back and tore the air open — a rift, red at its edges — and a *second* brimorak stepped through, and the first one filled its lungs and breathed, and a jet of boiling blood roared out at Nageru. The aasimar twisted, and the searing gout broke against his fire-ward and *slid,* the heat refused entry. Korroc came up over the crenel behind him, planted his boots, and Thane saw the dwarf do the thing that still made the hair stand on his arms — reach *down,* through the sole of his own foot, into the stone of the tower, and pull the tower's stubbornness up into himself until he stood there rooted, immovable, a piece of the wall that happened to hold a hammer. Korroc went for the summoned one.

Thane went for the gap. He always went for the gap. He rolled low, meaning to come up behind the first summoned demon in the soft blind seam where its goat's eyes couldn't follow — but the thing's claw came down and caught him short, stopped his tumble dead, and its flaming longsword swept across him in the same motion. Korroc's arm flashed out to block it — too slow, or the demon too fast — and the burning edge found Thane anyway, laid a line of fire across his ribs that his fire-ward, if he'd had one, would only have made worse. He rode it. Banked it. Fed the flame.

Caleth folded — one blink and the half-elf was behind the first demon, Radiance rising. Nageru slammed the original again, a full pivot of his whole body behind the fist. The demon answered by breathing a second time, not blood this time but *smoke* — a rolling black cloud that swallowed the tower-top, swallowed Nageru and Caleth both. Thane saw them vanish into it and saw, a heartbeat later, both their free hands clap across their own mouths. *Don't breathe. Don't drink it in.* They didn't. The demon raked Nageru across the chest with the flaming sword, and out of the smoke came Nageru's grunt.

Korroc reached into the stone again — Thane watched him do it, watched the dwarf go a shade more grey and solid, watched the strength of forty feet of tower pour up his legs — and swung the hammer with everything the stone had lent him.

It connected.

The demon buckled.

And Thane was there, out of the black seam behind it, and he put his mother's blade into the one place a buckling thing cannot guard, and the brimorak *screamed*, a goat's scream and a furnace's roar at once, and came apart, and was gone.

One down.

Two more stepped onto the tower.

Not from a rift — Thane clocked it even in the middle of it, filed it: *no summoning-tear, they just arrived* — one went straight for him and one for Nageru, and both struck from behind, two flaming longswords finding two backs in the same instant. Thane's ward of fire he did not have; he took the burn and turned inside it. Caleth thrust Radiance clean through the original summoned demon and it shrieked and was banished home to whatever pit had made it. Nageru's fist found the one on him. Korroc turned and smacked the one carving at Thane's back — and the demon countered fast, a serpent's strike sliding past the dwarf's guard, driving for the joint of Korroc's plate where a good hit would open him to the bone —

— and the *stone* took it. Korroc's rooted stance, the tower in his veins, turned what should have been a killing pierce into a shallow one. The sword got in. It got in far less than it wanted. Thane saw his cousin grunt and hold, grey and immovable, bleeding a little instead of dying, and something in Thane's chest — the small clean flame — burned a half-shade hotter and gladder.

Caleth plunged Radiance into the demon on Nageru. Gone. Thane sidestepped behind the last one while it fixed all its coal-eyed hate on Korroc, and his two blades crossed once, quick, and the brimorak came apart in the dark.

Silence, but for wind and their breathing and the small tick of cooling stone where hooves had burned.

Caleth eyed the catapult crouched on the tower's crown. "Should we take that apart?"

Thane nodded, and the two of them fell to it — pins pulled, the throwing-arm's cord cut and coiled off, the ratchet fouled. Not destroyed. Drezen was his father's citadel too, in a way, and a wrecked engine was a wrecked engine forever, but a *disabled* one could be a friend again if the war turned. He left it that way on purpose. A door barred, not burned.

They crossed the battlements to a gap where a fifth tower had stood once and stood no longer, and there Caleth gathered them close and folded them all to the top of the next tower in one cold step.

Empty. No demon.

"Those last two," Caleth said, working it out aloud. "They weren't summoned. They *teleported.* From other towers." He looked at the three remaining marks in his mind's map with new respect. "They can move between them."

They fouled that tower's catapult too, and Caleth folded them to the next, and on that one the demon was there but all the way across the roof, so far off it had time to see them land. Thane ducked low and slid around the black bulk of the engine, meaning to come up its flank — but he didn't need to. Nageru went *straight across,* blinding-fast, the whole width of the tower eaten in a breath, and drove his fist full into the goat's face; and Caleth was already there beside him with Radiance sunk in its chest; and the demon flailed and struck at three of them and hit none; and Korroc was still crossing the stone when Thane's daggers flickered out of the dark at its back and the thing was simply gone. He wiped both blades. Slow. Always.

Last tower. They walked down to it and up it, and there was no fight left in it — they fouled its engine and stood a moment on the crown of the four towers they'd unmade, breathing, the citadel a black crown against a paling sky beyond.

Caleth raised his hand and spoke, and light spread across the tower-top — a broad soft blaze, the agreed sign, the *door is open* to the army waiting below.

Down on the Ahari the crusade began to move — the whole dark mass of it lifting and flowing up the bridge toward the citadel, a slow tide of crossbows and tabards and the Queen's colors, no longer afraid of the sky.

Thane watched them come. His father had died trying to take this ground with an army much like that one and had not lived to see it move.

"Time to head in," Korroc said.

---

Caleth pointed to the corner towers of the citadel itself, high and black. "I can put us up there."

They gathered close. He cast. The cold step took them.

The top of the citadel was a charnel house.

Thane's boots came down on stone slick with cooling blood, and the roof stretched away littered with the dead — tieflings, halflings, a dozen and more, sprawled and heaped and flung. Korroc muttered something to the gods. Caleth turned a slow circle, Radiance up, throwing pale light across too many faces.

Korroc crouched by a tiefling, turned its arm, studied its throat, its hands. "These aren't defensive wounds." His voice had gone flat and careful. "They didn't fight back. Any of them."

Nageru said one word. "Succubus."

Thane looked at the nearest dead halfling — a small body, hands open and empty, no cuts on the forearms where a fighting creature throws them up to save its life. Something crossed him — not the bodies exactly, but the *silence* of them, a dozen deaths and not one raised hand. Kiranda. *You'll know her.*

"Just feeding," Thane said, low. "Or getting ready for us."

Nobody had an answer that was better than the question. They moved to a door set into the roof.

Thane went to it first, and here his hands slowed, and the old grammar came up in him without his choosing it. *Listen first.* He laid his ear near the frame and heard the hinges' faint song and no bolt thrown behind. *Look second.* He read the plate around the keyhole in the failing dark, the wear-marks, who used this door and how often — and found it well-trafficked but unwarded, honest steel, no false plate, no trap-seam. *Touch third.* His fingertips found the metal around the hole and it told him no one had ever forced this lock; it opened for the people who belonged behind it, and closed after them, and had never once been picked.

"Not trapped," he said. And then, because his father's blueprints lived under Caleth's coat and Thane had studied them until he could walk them blind: "According to the map — my parents' map, theirs and the dwarves who worked with them — this opens on a corridor. Stair going down at the end of it."

He worked the latch and slipped through into flickering torchlight, one shadow among the wall's many, and motioned the rest down and low behind him.

Ahead, a figure crossed from the corridor through a further door and was gone. Thane held up a flat hand — *wait, down* — and went ahead alone, quick and quiet to that further door, and put his shoulder to the frame, and rolled low through it into the room beyond.

And came up in a butcher's dream.

Beds — dozens, jammed rail to rail with barely a hand's width between them, a barracks built to cram bodies. And bodies it had: to the west, a heap of a dozen headless human corpses; to the east, in a separate obscene pile, a dozen human heads. Thane's stomach turned over hard and he recoiled a half-step before his training caught him and put him back —

— because there was someone *alive* in the room.

A woman, standing among the beds. Furred armor. A greatsword near as long as she was tall. Wild hair, and horns — not growing from her skull but bolted to the pauldrons of her armor, curving up past her ears. She saw him and her lip peeled back and she *snarled,* wordless, and Thane read *fight* in every line of her before she'd moved an inch, and read *evil* off her the way you read heat off a forge.

Then Nageru came through the door like a thrown spear.

The aasimar's fist took her in the ribs before Thane had settled his own weight, and Caleth was a step behind with Radiance already swinging, the blade coming down hard across her guard. She screamed something — a language, real words, none of them any tongue Thane knew — and hauled the greatsword up and brought it down at Nageru. He slid; it hit the floor and threw sparks; she wrenched it back up and caught him across the body with the return, a heavy ringing blow. Korroc came in last, filling the doorframe, and even as his hammer swung Thane saw the golden thread draw taut — saw some of the wound Nageru had just taken bleed *sideways* out of the aasimar and into the dwarf, Korroc grunting as he absorbed what wasn't his to make his companion's lighter to carry. The hammer found her. Nageru hit twice more, both fists. Caleth's blade bit again.

She kept *yelling* — the same incomprehensible torrent — and swung the greatsword into Nageru once more, Korroc taking a share of that hurt too as he struck her again in the same breath.

And Thane, mid-stride, blades up and hungry, stopped.

He didn't decide to. Or — he did, but it happened faster than deciding, the way the lock-read happened, some deep doorkeeper's part of him throwing up a hand before his mouth caught up. He *looked,* the way he'd been taught to look at a threshold before he crossed it: not at whether the thing in front of him was wicked — she plainly, radiantly was — but at whether she had business being killed in this exact doorway. And the room answered him. Twelve headless allies to the west. Twelve heads to the east. Someone in this citadel of monsters had killed a full crew of the citadel's own — and this snarling evil woman was standing in the middle of it, alone, alive, and had been swinging at *them,* not fleeing.

He pulled up short. Put his empty palm out. Kept the other blade low.

"We can't understand you," he said.

Nageru's fist hung back, half-cocked, the aasimar throwing him a *what in every hell are you doing* look. Thane didn't have time to explain the shape of the door he'd just seen.

"Don't kill her," he said. Louder. Then, switching his grip, turning both daggers pommel-forward the way he'd done with Nurah: *breathing. Take her breathing.*

Caleth read it in half a second — Caleth always did — and pulled a wand instead of pressing with Radiance, and spoke, and the furred woman locked mid-swing, frozen, the greatsword arrested over her head.

"Nageru," Caleth said, calm now. "Take her sword. Bind her hands."

Nageru did it, still frowning, prying the greatsword from her paralyzed grip and cording her wrists. Korroc lowered his hammer and looked at Thane across the barracks with one thick eyebrow climbing his forehead.

"Um," Thane said.

He shrugged, and swept his blade-hand at the two heaps of dead. "Look at the bodies. What if she killed them for the same reason we're here to?"

Korroc's frown deepened, the honest frown of a man who has bled for thinner things than hunches. "That's a thin thread to hang a life on, cousin. She radiates evil. You can feel it same as me."

"I know." Thane didn't argue it. He'd felt it too, felt it strong. "I know. I just needed to be sure." He held his cousin's eye. *A doorkeeper doesn't ask whether the trespasser is wicked. She asks whether they've business inside.* He didn't say it. It wasn't a thing you said. But Korroc had known him fifty-one years, since they were dwarflings under two brothers' one roof, and after a moment his cousin's frown eased and he nodded — not agreement exactly, but *trust,* which was better.

"All right," Korroc said.

The woman came down off her rage slowly, and it was a bad thing to watch. The snarl went out of her and something small and folded came into its place, a meekness so total it was almost worse than the fury.

Caleth crouched to her level. "Well. You don't seem to speak our—"

"I am Jestak."

"Oh." Caleth blinked. "You do speak Common. What are you doing here?"

"I led the siege crew." Her voice had gone flat and tired, the horns on her shoulders suddenly ridiculous. "All the men you saw dead. Mine."

"Did you kill them?"

"No." A flash of the old fire, quick and gone. "*Tieflings* killed my halfling crew. I killed the tieflings."

"You fought your own allies."

"Lord Vhane was not happy that we failed." The name landed in the room and Thane went very still. "He sent tieflings to kill us for it. We couldn't hold the south." *The towers,* Thane thought. *The bridge. The very ground we just took off him tonight. Staunton butchered his own crew for losing the thing we won.* "So I killed the ones he sent."

Caleth's voice didn't change. "Where do we find Staunton Vhane?"

"I don't know." She shook her wild head. "I've not been here long. I know this quarter, and the new chambers below. Not the whole citadel."

"New chambers."

"Below." She nodded down at the floor. "A dungeon. Anything that matters goes down there. Happens down there."

Caleth and Nageru traded a look. "Anything. Like a weapon. Like the Banner."

"The Banner's below." She said it plainly, no bargaining in it. "I know that much. And Joran Vhane's down there often."

Thane's head came around. "Wait. *Joran* Vhane."

"Staunton's brother."

"He has a brother." Thane heard his own voice go somewhere he hadn't sent it. The cold slow turn in his stomach came back, harder. Two Vhanes. A brother. He looked — he didn't mean to, but he looked — across the barracks at Korroc. His cousin. His father's brother's son. Two brothers who had built this place dreaming of a sky-city and died together, loyal, in the same season, in the same lost cause, never once turning on each other. And somewhere under this floor were two other brothers off the same forge, and one of them had grown up into the monster whose name Thane had carried north like a stone in his boot. He filed it. He didn't say a word of it. Some doors you note and leave shut.

"Yes," Jestak said, "and he's not happy with how his brother treats him. Find Joran and he might help you."

Thane spat on the stone. "You really think he'd turn on his own blood for us."

She nodded, hard, urgent now. "We're treated like nothing here. Killed for a whim. There are people in this place who'd help you gladly if you got them clear of the demons." Her eyes had gone wet. "I don't want to die. I'll swear to you. I'll help you through the whole citadel — I'll join you, I'll—"

"Whoa." Thane's hand came up. "I'm not so sure about that."

"She sounds like she means it," Korroc said, slow, weighing it the way he weighed everything. "Truly means it. But." He looked at her, not unkindly. "If we come up against something strong down there — something with a will that pushes on yours — I can't promise she holds. I can't promise she wouldn't do their bidding again the second they leaned on her."

Jestak's eyes went wide and wet both. "I *promise.*"

Caleth stood and lifted both hands, gentle, final. "We keep the sword. And I'm going to send you out — there's a war on outside these walls, our army against the tieflings, and I can put you past it. Across the Ahari there are camp workers. Supporters. People who'll take you in." He tore a page from a book of his own notes, wrote a few quick lines on it, folded it, and reached down to pin it to a strap of her furred armor. "Find someone who looks like a leader and can read. That gives you passage. Don't lose it."

She looked at the note pinned over her heart and said nothing. The tears came properly now, running down through the grime, and she didn't wipe them.

Caleth crouched once more and put his face level with hers, the way Nageru had put his face level with Nurah's — but where that had been a demand, this was only a question. "You ready?"

She nodded.

He touched her arm, and spoke, and she was gone — sent out over a battlefield to strangers who might or might not read, with a scrap of a half-elf's mercy folded against her chest.

Caleth stood and straightened his coat and looked around the emptied barracks, at the twelve heads and the twelve bodies and the dozens of jammed beds.

Korroc looked at Thane and nodded — the same nod he'd given before, the one that meant *trust,* the one that had passed between their two lines for longer than either of them had been alive.

Thane shrugged, and let one corner of his mouth go up, and wiped his mother's blade though it had touched no one this time, because the hands wanted the ritual.

Then all four of them turned to the far door — the one over the stair his father's map said went *down* — and remembered, at the same moment, what waited past it: an entire citadel they had not yet set foot in, and beneath even that, new rooms no dwarf had drawn, no map had marked, no blueprint in any spellbook had ever named. A dungeon. A banner in the dark. A monster's abused brother who might be a friend or might be a snare. And somewhere below, or above, or already walking the corridors between them, a succubus named Kiranda, waiting on a check-in that would never come, wondering — Thane thought, standing at the head of the stair with his blades clean and his fathers' war finally at its threshold — exactly where her spy had gotten to.

He put his ear to the next door, and listened first.
