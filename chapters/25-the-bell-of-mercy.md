# The Bell of Mercy

*Chapter Twenty-Five — Someone Who Might Listen*

---

Thane had left the infirmary step at dark. Nageru took it after.

He did not decide to. He came across the west court with a basin of clean water that someone had asked him to carry, and set it inside the door where it was wanted, and came back out, and the step was empty and still warm from the dwarf, and he sat down on it. That was all.

The court was quiet. A sentry walked the wall above, and his boots went away along the stone and came back. Somewhere a horse complained. The Worldwound's long wind came over the parapet and went down into the yard and moved the dust in small circles, and gave up.

Behind him, in the long room, a man was asleep who had been taken by a dragon and was now not.

Nageru had seen him brought in. He had helped to carry the last of it — the arms, while Thane and Korroc had the rest. He had felt how little there was of him. He had seen the feet. He had stood for a time at the end of the cot and watched Sosiel cut the robe away and Korroc lift the elf as though he were a child, and then he had gone to find water, because water was always needed and nobody ever thought to fetch it until it was gone.

He put his hands on his knees, open.

He did not meditate. He let the court be what it was.

It was not easy tonight. His body was tired in the ordinary way, from three made things the size of bull elephants, and the ordinary tiredness was a thing he knew how to set down. But under it there was another thing, and it had been there since the spiders, and he could not quite find the edge of it to take hold.

*Been walking. For days.*

Four words. A cracked voice. And then sleep, so quickly and completely that it had looked, for a moment, like the other thing.

He had been glad. He was glad still. A man the whole citadel had stopped speaking of in front of Caleth had walked back across the plain on his own two feet, and the gladness of that was real and plain and Nageru did not doubt it.

But he had stood on the field afterward, while Korroc knelt with his hand on the elf's chest, and looked at the three made things lying where they had fallen. Their eyes dark. Their legs folded. They had not been sent to kill. Thane had said so — *it came to fetch him* — and Nageru had watched the nearest of them reach for the fallen man with an open claw, carefully, the way one reaches for a thing one has been told to bring back unbroken.

Somebody had wanted him back.

And somebody had let him walk for days first.

Nageru breathed, and let the thought sit where it was, and did not follow it. *Expectation is a poor substitute for observation.* He had observed a man come home. He did not yet know what else he had observed.

---

The infirmary door opened behind him, and Korroc came out.

It was the first time the dwarf had left the bedside since noon. He came out slowly, the way a man comes out of a hot forge into the night, and stood on the step above Nageru and stretched — both arms up, the whole plate of him creaking — and rubbed his face with his bare hands. He had not put his gauntlets back on. He looked down at Nageru without surprise.

"Thought that was you."

"Brother."

"He's sleeping." Korroc let his arms fall. "Proper sleep. Not the other. Sosiel says leave him be till morning and he'll know us." He breathed out through his nose. "I could eat a horse. I could eat *my* horse. I'd enjoy it."

He started down the step.

And Caleth came out of the dark of the court and stopped in front of him.

Nageru had not seen him come. That was rare enough to notice. The half-elf had been somewhere in the shadow by the well, perhaps, or along the west wall, waiting — and he had waited, Nageru understood, for exactly this: for Korroc to come out of that room alone.

"Korroc."

"Lad." Korroc peered up at him. "You've not eaten either. I can see it on you."

Caleth did not answer that. He glanced once at Nageru on the step, and Nageru saw him decide that Nageru did not need to be sent away, and was oddly moved by it.

"I'm suspicious of this whole situation," Caleth said.

He said it quietly, and quickly, as though it had been in his mouth for some hours and he had been holding it behind his teeth.

Korroc's face did not change. He only looked up at the half-elf, and waited.

"How did he possibly escape," Caleth said, "without any spells?"

There was a silence in the court.

Nageru had not thought of it in those words. He thought of it now. The collar. The needles. *The wearer cannot cast. At all.* A wizard, and a wizard of the kind that was a little frightening even to his friends, made into a man with nothing in his hands but his hands. And then a cage — there had been a cage; Caleth had seen it, in the water — and then the Worldwound, and days of it, and then Drezen.

Korroc stopped, and looked at Caleth for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly.

"It does seem odd," he said. "Doesn't it."

"Can you cast a spell," Caleth said, "to know if he is lying?"

Nageru watched Korroc hear it. He watched the dwarf understand what was being asked, and of whom, and he watched him not like it, and decide that his not liking it was not the point.

"It'll take some effort," Korroc said. "Drawing on what we've been given. But aye. I can."

Caleth nodded. His hand had gone to the left side of his coat. It stayed there.

"We'll wait for him to wake," he said. "In the morning. And ask him some questions." He paused. "If you can be there, I would appreciate it."

Korroc's face was grave.

"Aye," he said. "I'll be there."

Caleth nodded once more, and turned, and went away across the court into the dark toward the west range, toward his own door, with his hand on his coat.

Korroc stood looking after him.

"That lad," he said at last, very low, "has been sitting on that floor since noon wanting to ask a man whether he's who he says he is." He shook his head. "And not wanting to know."

Nageru said nothing.

"Come on." Korroc nudged him with a boot. "Food. Before I eat the horse."

Nageru got up and went with him. At the corner of the court he looked back once, and the infirmary windows were dark except for one, low, where a lamp had been left burning by a cot, and nobody was sitting under it now.

---

The horns went before dawn.

Nageru was awake before the first of them had finished. He did not remember waking. There was the dark, and then the long brass note coming over the walls and into the stone, and then he was on his feet with his wraps tied and the door open, and it was still dark, and the note was still sounding.

Then another horn. Then a third, from the north wall, and from somewhere in the city below, bells.

He went out and down.

They met at the bottom of the tower stair as though they had arranged it. Thane coming across the court at a dwarf's fast roll with his knives already where his hands wanted them. Korroc behind him, buckling his shield onto his arm as he came, and swearing in Dwarven, and something else under the swearing that was not Korroc at all — Nageru saw the dwarf's head jerk aside, once, as if a wasp had come near his ear. Caleth last, coat on, coat fastened, face very calm.

Nobody spoke. They went up.

There were officers on the tower top already. Irabeth, half-armored, at the parapet with a spyglass. Aron at her shoulder. A knot of soldiers at the ballista. A paladin Nageru knew by sight but not by name, shouting down the stair for runners. The sky in the east had gone from black to the dark gray of a bruise, and against it, beyond the walls, there were shapes.

Nageru counted them and stopped counting.

Drakes. A great many. Long-necked, long-tailed, leather-winged, circling out past the walls in slow wide loops with riders crouched on their backs, and the ballista on this tower and the next one over going off — *thunk*, and the long shudder of the arm, and a bolt going out into the gray and finding nothing. The drakes were wide of it. They were keeping wide. They went around the citadel and around it again, and never quite came in.

It was a familiar sight. They had seen it from these walls before.

Nageru looked out over the citadel, and over the dark roofs of the city below it, and watched the drakes go around.

"They are circling," he said. "Trying to find a weak point to attack."

Irabeth lowered the glass and nodded without looking at him.

He watched them go around again. Out, and around, and out. Never close. Never committing. Every drake in the sky, every rider, every eye on every wall of Drezen turned outward to watch them do it.

Every eye.

He frowned.

"Wait," he said, and started to turn. "Are they only a distraction —"

The courtyard below them broke open.

It was the sound before anything. A great grinding roar of stone giving way, the sound of a wall that had stood for a long time deciding all at once that it would not, and under it a crack like a tree going over in a storm. Nageru was at the inner parapet before the sound had finished. Dust was going up out of the courtyard in a gray column. And in the dust, rising up out of it, straightening, there was a shape.

It was larger than the gate it had come through. Its head was the head of a dog that had been made by someone who had heard dogs described and hated them. It had four arms. Two of them ended in hands, with claws. The other two, the great upper ones, ended in pincers, like a crab's, each large enough to close around a man's waist.

He had seen one before. Ten miles from here, coming off a rock.

"*Glabrezu*," said Caleth, flat.

And then, in a voice Nageru had almost never heard from him: "How in the *hells* —" He was at the parapet, white with fury. "How was that not *seen*?"

Nobody answered him. Nobody knew.

Out past the walls, the drakes stopped circling.

They came in.

---

They came in from every side at once, the whole sky of them folding inward on the citadel like a hand closing, and the ballista went off and went off and a drake screamed and fell somewhere to the north, and the rest kept coming. Nageru saw two go over the west wall low enough to touch the merlons. He saw riders on the east range dropping down off their saddles onto the wall-walk. He heard steel, suddenly, everywhere.

On Korroc's back, the axe was screaming.

Nageru could not hear it. He had never been able to hear it. But he could see it, the way one sees wind by the grass — in the set of the dwarf's jaw, in the way his eyes went wide and then narrowed, in the snarl he gave to nobody that was half Dwarven and half something older. Fiendsplitter had found a demon. Fiendsplitter had found *demons*. It was, Nageru gathered, beside itself.

Thane had stopped. One moment, in the middle of the tower top, with drakes coming down out of the sky and a demon in the courtyard below, the dwarf stood perfectly still and bowed his head. His lips moved.

Something settled over the four of them. Nageru felt it the way he felt a change in the weather — a closing, a holding, a hand set flat between him and the world.

Thane's head came up.

A drake came over the tower.

It came low and fast with its rider flat along its neck, and as it passed it opened its mouth, and what came out was not fire. It was green-gray and it was heavy and it fell, a great clot of it, bursting as it fell into a cloud. Nageru was not under it. He did not decide to be elsewhere; his body had already decided. He felt the edge of it pass, smelled it — sour, sharp, like something left too long in a cellar — and turned, and saw that Caleth had not been so fortunate.

The half-elf was in the middle of it. Nageru saw the acid take him across the shoulders and face and saw him bow under it with his teeth bared and his eyes screwed shut, and come up out of it smoking.

Korroc was at the inner parapet.

He had Fiendsplitter in his hand. Nageru had not seen him draw it. And he looked down into the courtyard at the thing in the dust, and up at the drakes coming in, and down again, and Nageru watched him make the sum.

Then the dwarf put one boot on the parapet and went over it.

Nageru was at the edge before he had thought about it, looking down. Korroc was falling. And then he was not falling — he was *drifting*, slow, upright, a hundred and more pounds of armor and dwarf and screaming axe settling down through the air of the courtyard like a seed off a thistle. A silver light at his belt. Terendelev's scale. He was going down to it by the slow road because there was no fast one, and he was going anyway.

*Of course he is,* Nageru thought, and something in his chest that was not quite a laugh came and went.

Another drake.

It came over the tower top from the other side and breathed as it came, and again Nageru was not there, and Thane was not there, and Irabeth went flat behind the merlons and the soldiers threw themselves aside — and the cloud found Caleth. Again. It found him squarely. Nageru heard him make a sound through his teeth that was not a word.

Below, a third drake had seen Korroc.

It turned in the air, tight, and came across the courtyard at him, and swung its long tail at the drifting dwarf the way a man swings a flail at a bird. Korroc saw it come. He did not try to move; he could not, hanging there. He only turned in the air, slowly, and put his shield in the way.

The tail struck the shield and slid off it. It did not even move him. He went on drifting down, turning gently, shield up, like a man being lowered on a rope who has been mildly inconvenienced.

Nageru breathed out.

A fourth drake. Another cloud on the tower top. This time Caleth was moving when it came — Nageru saw him throw himself aside — and the cloud caught only the edge of him. Only. He came up out of it with one side of his coat smoking and his face set.

Another drake swept at Korroc and missed by a long way.

And Caleth was beside Nageru.

He had something in his hand — the long ivory rod from the riverbed, the one that rattled. His fingers had closed on it. Nageru saw one of the gold runes along it go bright under his grip, and then go dull.

Caleth touched Nageru's arm. He touched Thane's shoulder.

The world folded.

---

It came back as dust.

He was in the courtyard. The flagstones under his bare feet, gritty with fallen mortar. The smell of broken stone. The noise of the battle overhead gone suddenly far and high, and the noise of the thing in front of them very near.

Caleth spoke again, quickly. He turned to Thane.

And Thane was gone.

Nageru did not see him go. There was a dwarf beside him with two knives in his hands, and then there was air. The half-elf had made him into nothing. Nageru had seen him do it before, but never so quickly, and never with so little ceremony. Thane did not even have time to look surprised.

Then Caleth did a third thing.

He did not speak for it. He stood still and drew something up out of himself — Nageru felt it, that deep gold place the wardstone had put in all of them — and the air around him *bent.* Not visibly. Nageru could see nothing. But the courtyard within thirty feet of the half-elf had changed in some way he could not name, as though the light in it were being refracted through water. Nageru had felt it once before, on the plain, when a ray of killing cold had gone into that space and come out of it as fire. *What comes in does not arrive as what it was.*

Above them, a drake breathed down into the courtyard. The cloud fell. It came into the bent air and it was not acid any more. It was flame — a soft rolling ball of it that broke harmlessly over Caleth's head and was gone.

Caleth did not look up. He was looking at the demon.

The glabrezu had turned toward them.

It was not looking at Nageru. It was not looking at Caleth. It was looking at a piece of empty air a little to Caleth's left, and its great pincers were opening, and it was turning its whole body toward that empty air, and Nageru understood with a cold small shock that it could see Thane.

Thane went in anyway.

Nageru saw it only by its effects. A blade from nothing, a sudden dark line along the demon's flank where there had been no line before. The demon shrieked and swung at the air with a pincer and the air was not there any more. A second dark line. It had been ready for him, and he had cut it twice regardless.

Nageru went.

He felt the smite come up in him as he moved — that old sure warmth in the fists, the thing his Oath had always been, now carrying the gold of the fane along with it. He crossed the ground between him and the demon the way he crossed ground now. He did not think about it. He was at the demon's knee and he did not remember the space between.

Both fists.

Left, into the body, under the lowest rib, where the great weight of it hung on the joints. Right, up, under the dog's jaw.

Light went off his knuckles each time like a spark off a struck anvil.

The demon roared. It was a sound he felt in his teeth.

Somewhere above and behind them, Korroc's feet touched stone at last. Nageru heard the clank of it at the far end of the courtyard, much too far, and heard the dwarf start toward them at his heavy determined run, and heard, a moment later, a word in a deep voice and felt a wash of warmth go past him toward Caleth. The half-elf straightened under it. The worst of the acid-burns along his jaw went pale.

And a drake came low across the far end of the yard at Korroc, and swung its tail, and missed him. But its rider did not stay on.

Nageru saw it, in the corner of his eye. The rider let go of the saddle as the drake went past and *dropped*, and landed on the flagstones in front of Korroc lightly, lightly, on its toes. And straightened. And opened its wings.

It was not a cultist. It was not a man.

It was beautiful, the way a knife in a jeweler's window is beautiful, and it had wings like a bat's, and it smiled at Korroc.

An incubus. Nageru knew it the way one knows a smell.

Korroc stopped. Nageru saw his head jerk, as though the axe had shouted something new.

Then the glabrezu came for Nageru, and there was no more room in the world for anything else.

---

It came with everything.

Pincers first. The right one came down and across in a great scything arc and Nageru was under it. The left one came the other way, low, at his knees, and he was over it — but the clawed hands were waiting for him on the far side of the pincer, as though the pincers had only ever been there to put him where the hands wanted him. One claw raked his ribs. He felt it go through the wraps and through the skin under them, four lines, hot.

The other found his thigh.

Then the head came down. The jaws. He felt them close on the air beside his ear with a sound like a trap.

He stepped back. Breathed. Felt the blood start on his side.

Behind the demon, Caleth was moving.

He had the rod again. Again a gold rune went bright and dull under his fingers. He spoke, quick and low, and the world went *fast.* Nageru felt it go into him, through him — his heart going quicker, his limbs lighter, the whole courtyard slowing very slightly as though everything else in it were wading. Caleth had laid it on him and on Thane and on himself all at once.

And then the half-elf was gone. Not folded out — sliding. Nageru saw him step sideways into nothing on one side of the demon and step out of nothing on the other, directly behind it, so that the glabrezu stood between them: Nageru before it, Caleth at its back.

Caleth did not attack it.

He had a second rod in his hand now. A plainer one. He did not look at the demon at all; he looked up, at the drakes coming down on the courtyard from every side, at the riders on their backs. He touched the rod and he spoke and he flung out his free hand toward the sky.

Cold went up out of him.

A great white cone of it, roaring, the air itself crackling as it froze, and Nageru felt it go past his head like a winter coming through a door — and none of it touched him. None of it touched the empty air where Thane must be. It went *around* them, threading itself between the people Caleth cared for like a needle through cloth, and burst up into the sky over the courtyard.

It found the drakes.

Ice ran over them. Nageru saw it happen — a white skin forming over scales and wings in an instant, as fast as frost forms on a cup of water poured out on a winter morning. The drakes stiffened in the air. Their wings locked. And they fell.

They fell into the courtyard all around him. Three of them, four. They hit the stones with a sound like crockery thrown down a stair, and the ice on them shattered into a thousand bright pieces and went skittering out across the flagstones — and the drakes beneath it, stunned, stupid, bleeding, floundered up onto their feet again.

In front of him the demon had turned its dog's head toward the half-elf behind it.

That was all Thane needed.

Nageru saw three wounds open in the demon's side, one-two-three, out of nothing, in the place where the great body joined the upper arms. Deep. Dark. A dwarf's blades, in at the seams, the way Thane had always gone in at the seams of things. The glabrezu threw its head back and howled at the sky.

And then it was not there.

There was no body. There was a smell, sulfur and something rotten, and a shape like a black smoke going thin, and then a scorch on the flagstones where its feet had been. Gone home. Back to the Abyss, whole, to be somebody else's trouble.

Nageru did not stop to watch it. There were drakes on the ground.

He went to the nearest. It was still getting up, shaking its long head, flecks of ice flying off its scales, and its rider was half-crushed beneath it. Nageru did not trouble with the rider. He struck the drake twice, where its long neck met its chest, and the second blow went in and found the thing under the scales that a drake needs to live, and the drake folded down onto the stones and did not unfold.

Across the yard, Korroc's boots had begun to glow.

Nageru knew that light. He had seen the dwarf wake those boots in the excavation, running under a drake with his arm up. Korroc took one step — one — and was across the space between himself and the incubus as though the flagstones had been folded up and put away. Fiendsplitter went up.

And came down. Four times. Four great strokes, faster than Korroc had any right to swing a weapon that was not his, and Nageru saw the dwarf's beard part in the grin that was not his either.

The incubus was still on its feet. Barely. It was staggering.

A drake on the ground, ice still sliding off it, snapped at Caleth.

Caleth was not there. Nageru saw the jaws close on the place where the half-elf had been standing and saw the half-elf twenty feet off, a wand in his hand, his other hand reaching down — reaching for what looked like nothing. And closing on it. And then Caleth and the nothing were gone together.

They came out behind the incubus.

Nageru only knew because the incubus suddenly arched its back and screamed. A blade, from nowhere, into the place between its wings. It turned, too slowly, and there was nothing there to turn to, and then there was another wound, and it went thin and black and was gone like the glabrezu had gone.

*He can see him,* Nageru thought. He had watched Caleth's eyes. All through this, the half-elf's eyes had been going to places where there was nothing, the way one's eyes go to a friend in a crowd. Caleth could see the invisible dwarf. Of course he could. Caleth saw everything, and paid for most of it.

Nageru turned to the next drake on the ground.

It had seen him coming and it was turning to meet him, mouth open, and he went inside the mouth's reach and struck it under the jaw, and again under the jaw, and it went down.

Korroc, with nothing left in front of him, was coming across the courtyard toward Caleth at his heavy run, axe in one hand, shield on the other arm, looking about him for the next thing — and his boot caught on nothing at all.

He stumbled. Hard. He said something in Dwarven that made a paladin on the far side of the courtyard turn round to look.

Nothing had been there. Nageru saw Korroc look down at the nothing, and saw his face change as he worked out who it was, and saw him open his mouth to say so —

And Caleth reached out and took him by the shoulder, and reached down and took hold of the nothing, and the three of them were gone.

Nageru looked up.

There they were, on the tower top. Three shapes at the parapet, small against the gray sky. Korroc still visibly shouting about something.

He was alone in the courtyard.

Not quite alone. There was an incubus still standing near the broken gate, the one that had come down off another drake, and it was looking at him.

It smiled. It was a very good smile. It had been made to be.

Nageru felt it try something on him. He did not know what. A pressure, a warmth, a sense of being *wanted* — very old, and very skilled. It went past him the way the acid had gone past him, and he was elsewhere.

He hit it twice. The first blow took the smile off its face. The second took the rest.

It went thin, and black, and home.

He stood still in the empty courtyard and listened.

Above him, on the tower, steel. Drakes. Korroc's voice, and Irabeth's. And out past the courtyard — through the inner gate, and around the corner of the old keep, somewhere in the north range — another sound. Men shouting. Men screaming. And something roaring, over all of it, with a voice he had heard a moment ago.

A second voice. Almost the same voice.

He ran.

---

He went through the inner gate and around the corner of the keep, and he did not know the way, and it did not matter; the noise was the way. The haste was still on him. The ground went by under his feet the way it went by in dreams — a passage, a stair, an arch, the long lane behind the north range — and he passed a soldier running in the same direction and did not feel himself pass him.

He came around the last corner into a smaller court, and stopped.

It was another glabrezu.

It was as large as the first. It stood in the middle of the court with its back to a wall, and around it in a ragged half-ring there were soldiers, and paladins, a dozen perhaps, and several on the ground who were not getting up. A paladin of Iomedae lay against the wall with her shield split in two. A boy in a crusader tabard was crawling backward across the flagstones on his elbows, his legs not working.

And the demon held a man in one of its clawed hands.

It held him up off the ground, the way a child holds a doll, and the man was kicking and could not reach anything, and the demon was looking at him. Not killing him. Looking. Considering where to begin.

Nageru felt the smite come up in him.

It came up the way it had not come up in a long time — not the warm sure thing of an Oath being kept, but something hotter and higher, out of the bottom of him, the place where an eight-year-old boy had watched a caravan of people die and had not been able to do anything about it. He let it come. He did not try to hold it. His fists caught fire, holy and white-gold, and the light went up off them in the gray court and every soldier there saw it and some of them cried out.

The demon turned its head.

He was already there.

Once. Twice. Three times — into the forearm of the hand that held the man, and into the elbow, and into the great joint of the shoulder above it — and each blow the holy fire went off like a lamp bursting, white, roaring, and Nageru felt it go into the demon and *burn.*

The glabrezu bellowed. It was not a roar now. It was pain. And its hand opened.

The man fell. A paladin dived and half-caught him and they went down together in a heap, and dragged themselves clear.

The demon looked down at Nageru.

He saw the pincers come. He was not where they closed. He saw the clawed hands come after them, the way they had come before, and this time he knew it, and he went *through* the gap between them instead of around — and still one claw found him. It came in across his chest, high, and he felt it rake him from shoulder to breastbone, and he felt the blood come, and he did not care about it.

He had found his place. Inside the reach of the pincers, under the jaws, against the great body itself. A place where a creature that size could not easily strike at a man.

He struck.

Five times. He did not count them. He felt them. One into the belly. One under the ribs. One into the knee, so that the weight came down on that side and the body leaned. One, as it leaned, into the side of the dog's head. One more into the throat. His hands were fire. The haste in him was a drum. He was water, going in at every crack.

The demon staggered.

It was hurt. It was hurt badly. It stood swaying against the wall, black ichor running out of it in a dozen places, and looked at him, and Nageru looked back, and he knew — he did not know how; he had never known how — that it was afraid.

He breathed in.

The smite was still on his hands. He could feel it, waiting, white-hot. He reached into it once more, and it answered, and it burned brighter than it had all day.

Four blows. The last one he put into the center of its chest, both fists together, and the light went off his hands like the sun coming over a mountain, and when he could see again there was nothing in front of him but the wall, and a long black scorch on it in the shape of a thing that had stood there.

The court was silent.

Then somebody began to cheer, raggedly, and somebody else began to call for a priest, and the boy with the legs that did not work started to cry. Nageru stood with his fists slowly going dark and his chest bleeding down the front of his wraps.

He went to the boy first.

He knelt. He put his hands on him. The other thing came up in him then, the thing that was not a smite, the warmth his order had taught him to give away as matter-of-factly as he gave a blow — and the boy stopped crying, and looked up at him, and moved one foot.

"Stay here," Nageru told him. "Somebody will come."

Then he stood up, and looked up, past the roofs of the north range, at the top of the tower.

There were still drakes up there. He could see them, wheeling. He could see the flash of steel along the parapet.

He did not want to run all that way.

He did not have to.

He went down inside himself — down past the fire and the blood and the drum of the haste — to the still place. The ki. The thing he had been taught to find before he was taught to read. It was there. It was always there. He took hold of it.

He took one step.

---

He came out of it on the tower top, between a merlon and the ballista, and the wind of the height hit him in the face.

The fight was almost done.

He saw it the way one sees the last page of a book: all at once, and only the end. A drake lay dead across the far parapet with its rider beside it. Another lay at Korroc's feet with its head nearly off. Aron Kir was sitting against the stair-head with one hand pressed to his side and his face gray but his eyes open and furious. Soldiers were down, and soldiers were getting up.

And Irabeth was on her back.

She was getting up. Nageru saw that first, and something in him that had gone tight went loose again. She was on her back on the stones with her shield still up over her, and Korroc was standing over her — standing over her with one hand flat on her breastplate and his eyes shut and his lips moving. Nageru knew that look. He had felt the thread of it himself, in the cave with the dragon, and on the plain with the spiders. The dwarf had laid his bond on her. Whatever she took now, he would take some of it.

A drake lay beside her with Radiance's work all down its flank.

And Thane was on his knees on the back of another dead drake a few yards off, visible again, wiping his knives on its rider's cloak. He looked up and saw Nageru and raised his eyebrows a fraction, as though to say *you took your time*.

One thing was still moving. An incubus — the last one — had come up off the stones near Irabeth and was going, wings out, running along the parapet and leaping, and its great wings caught the air and lifted it out over the courtyard, away, toward the walls.

Caleth turned and looked after it.

He did not hurry. He raised one hand toward it, and spoke, one long precise phrase, the way one closes a door on a room one has finished with.

The incubus stopped in the air.

It hung for a moment against the gray sky, wings spread. Then it was not there. Not killed — *sent*. Plucked out of the world and put back where it had come from, like a misfiled page.

All along the walls of Drezen, the drakes were going.

Nageru watched them. Out over the walls, every one, in ones and twos, not regrouping, not circling. Simply leaving, as though some signal had been given that nobody on the walls could hear. The riders lying low on their necks. The sky in the east gone from gray to the yellow-gray of morning.

Then they were specks. Then they were gone.

He stood at the parapet and let his breath come back to him. It came back slowly. The haste was going out of his blood, and the gold was going out of his hands, and what was left was a man with a torn chest and four lines across his ribs and a deep plain weariness.

He looked at the empty sky, and then at the others.

"End of the week?" he said.

Caleth made a small noise. It was not a laugh. It was the noise a man makes who has been thinking the same thing and is annoyed to hear it said aloud.

"They were late," he said.

Nageru inclined his head.

"Indeed."

---

The infirmary had become something else.

It was the long room, and the cots, and the clean smell of Sosiel's salves under the other smell. But the cots were full and there were men on the floor between them and men in the doorway waiting to come in, and more being carried across the court, and the priests and the surgeons and the sisters from the cathedral were going up and down the rows at something close to a run. A man was screaming at the far end. Another was praying, loudly, to nobody in particular. Someone was calling for more water and nobody was fetching it.

Caleth stood in the doorway and looked down the room.

Nageru watched him look. He was not looking at the wounded. He was looking for one cot in particular, and not finding it, and his face did not change but something in his shoulders did.

Sosiel saw them from halfway down the room. He was on his knees beside a soldier, both hands red to the wrist, and he did not get up. He only lifted one red hand and pointed — at a narrow door in the side wall, half shut.

"I had him moved," he called. "Side room. Out of all this."

Caleth was already going.

Korroc did not follow. Nageru saw him stop in the middle of the long room and look around him — at the rows, and the floor, and the man screaming at the end — and saw the dwarf's whole face settle into the face he wore at a forge when the work in front of him was very large and there was no question of doing anything except starting it.

He was already pulling off his gauntlets.

Nageru went with Caleth and Thane.

The side room was small. It had been a storeroom once; there were still hooks on the walls. It held one cot and one stool and a high window with gray morning in it, and on the cot, exactly as he had been the night before, lay Aravashnial.

He had not moved. Nageru could see that at once. The blanket lay over him in the same folds. His hands lay where they had been laid. Only his chest moved, slowly, up and down, and the ring of small wounds at his throat had gone from red to a duller color, crusted and clean.

Through all of it, the horns and the bells and a glabrezu coming through a wall forty yards away, he had slept.

Caleth stood at the foot of the cot.

He stood there a long time. He did not sit. He did not go nearer. Nageru stood by the door and watched him and did not look away, because he had been asked, once, by a dwarf on a wagon-seat, to make sure his friends did not lose their heads, and had found the habit of it hard to put down.

Then Caleth turned around and went out, past him, without a word.

Thane looked at Nageru.

Nageru looked at the elf on the cot.

"He is very tired," he said.

"Aye," said Thane. "That'll be it."

---

Nageru went back to the long room.

He had not decided to stay away from it; he had only gone with the others because they were going. And when he came out of the side room and saw Korroc down the far end, on his knees beside a soldier with no more color in him than the sheet, with one bare hand on the man's chest and the other on his own forehead and his eyes shut, Nageru knew where he was needed.

He did not announce it. He took a basin from a table and went to the well and filled it, because the man at the end had been calling for water and nobody had fetched it. Then he brought it back. Then he knelt by the next cot.

His own hands were a small thing beside Korroc's. He had always known that. The warmth he could give away was a monk's warmth, a paladin's — enough for a soldier's leg, a boy's legs in a courtyard, his own ribs if he was careful. It did not flood a room. It did not go out over twenty men at once like water poured over a field.

But it was what he had. He gave it.

A woman with a drake's tail-spike through her arm, who swore at him the whole time and then cried. A man who had been breathed on and had lost the skin off one side of his face, and did not make a sound. A sergeant with a broken leg who wanted to talk about the glabrezu, and how big it had been, and how it had come through the wall like the wall was paper, and wanted to know how a thing that size had come all the way across the plain to the citadel without one man on one wall seeing it — and Nageru did not know, and said so, and the sergeant looked at him as though he had expected otherwise.

He worked. The morning went on. The light went from gray to white in the high windows.

Some while later — he could not have said how long — he looked up from a soldier's bandaged hands and found Korroc looking at him from three cots away.

The dwarf was sitting back on his heels, gauntlets off, beard full of somebody else's blood. He had the look of a man who has just looked up from a long task and found, to his considerable surprise, that he has not been doing it alone.

Korroc nodded at him.

Nageru nodded back, and went on.

---

It was some hours before Caleth and Thane came out of the side room.

Nageru knew they had gone in. He had seen it — had seen it, and understood it, which was not the same thing. He had been at the water-butt by the door, refilling a basin, when Caleth came across the court with Thane at his shoulder, and stopped on the infirmary step, and looked down the long room to where Korroc knelt.

The dwarf had a soldier under each hand. He did not look up. He could not have; there was a man on the cot in front of him who was going to die if Korroc took his hands off him, and both of them knew it.

Nageru saw Caleth see that.

He saw him stand on the step a moment longer, with his hand on his coat. And then he saw Thane — Thane, who did not volunteer, who had spent a year arranging never to be asked for anything — look at the dwarf, and look at the half-elf, and say, very quietly:

"I'll do it."

Caleth turned his head.

"I can cast it," said Thane. "Same as him. Near enough." He shrugged. "He's busy."

Caleth looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and the two of them went down the side of the long room to the narrow door, and in, and the door shut behind them.

Nageru went back to his basin.

He did not listen at the door. It did not occur to him to. But he found, through the rest of that long afternoon, that he knew exactly where the door was without looking, the way one knows where a fire is in a dark room, and that every time a shadow crossed the threshold of the long room he glanced up to see if it was them.

It was not, for some time.

---

When they came out, they came to find Korroc and Nageru together, and took them out into the court where it was quiet.

It was late afternoon. The light was going long and yellow across the flagstones, and over the west range somebody was hammering — the first sound of repair, Nageru thought; somebody had already begun. Korroc sat down on the edge of the well with a groan he did not trouble to hide. Thane leaned against the wall. Caleth stood.

"He's awake," said Thane.

"He woke," said Caleth, "a few hours ago. He tried to sit up and could not, quite. His ribs." He touched his own side, once, to show where. "I helped him."

He told it.

He told it the way he told anything that mattered — in order, from the beginning, in complete sentences, as though he were reading it off a page. Nageru had heard him report to Irabeth this way. He had heard him report a dragon this way. He listened now, and listened also to the shape of the telling, because a man reading off a page is not always reading all of it.

*How did you make it back to the citadel?*

"He walked," Caleth said. "From the cave where he was held. He did not know where he was. He started walking east, because the citadel or the trade road had to be east, and he thought he would find one or the other."

*Do you remember how you were captured?*

"He remembers the tower. He remembers waking in the cavern by the Scar, with the dragon. He remembers us arriving." The corner of Caleth's mouth moved. "He remembers four idiots teleporting into the middle of a small army."

"His word," said Thane. "*Idiots*. Said it like a man who'd been waiting a while to say it."

Korroc made a sound in his beard that was very nearly a laugh.

"He remembers Jerribeth casting," Caleth went on. "And then nothing. And then a cage."

Nageru remembered it too. He had been under the dragon at the time, with three of its claws in him, but he had seen the edge of it over the beast's shoulder — a great senseless burst of color going off on the far side of the cavern, every color at once, and in the middle of it the kneeling, roped elf going over sideways onto the stone. He had seen nothing after that. He had been busy. When he looked again there had been nothing on the floor but a coil of rope.

"So he never saw the man who took him," he said.

"No." Caleth's voice did not change. "He was not awake to see him. Neither, it seems, did anyone."

*How did you get out of the cage?*

Caleth's face did something, then, that Nageru had seen it do perhaps three times in a year. It was not a smile. It was the face of a man trying, against his own judgment, not to admire something.

"The guards," he said, "apparently believed that a wizard who cannot cast spells is helpless."

Thane snorted.

"They were lax. He got the chain of his shackles through the bars and around a guard's neck. When the man collapsed, he used the chain to hold him close enough to the bars to get a key off him." Caleth paused. "And he opened the cage."

There was a silence at the well.

"Aye, well," Korroc said at last, slowly. "I'd not want to be the guard."

*Did you see Vang there? Why didn't he stop you himself?*

"He does not think Vang was staying there," said Caleth. "It was a cave. Not the Sanctum. Not anything like a fortress."

"Different cave?" said Korroc. "From the Scar?"

"He did not say. I do not think he knows. He was not conscious for the journey between them." Caleth's hand went to his coat. "I told him it seemed too easy. That it was out of character for Vang. To make it so easy."

"And?"

"He said, *I know. And I don't know.*" Caleth's voice was very even. "He said it did seem easier than it should have been. But that then the retrievers came. The spiders. And he would not have been able to fight them alone."

Nageru thought of the three made things lying dark on the plain. Of the claw opening, carefully, over the fallen man.

*It came to fetch him.*

"Then I cast," Caleth said. "On him. To see whether there was anything on him that should not be. Any thread somebody might follow back here. Any evil." He paused. "There was none. Nothing. Not a trace of either."

"He let you," said Korroc.

"He asked me what I was doing. I told him. He said that made sense." Caleth's mouth tightened. "He was not offended. He would have done the same."

"And Vang?" said Nageru.

"Asked him questions. Many questions. How many we are in Drezen. What our defenses are." Caleth looked at them, one after another. "What the four of us can do. Our powers. Our capabilities."

The hammering over the west range had stopped. In the quiet Nageru could hear the wind going over the wall.

"What did he tell him?" said Korroc.

"Nothing." Caleth said it very simply. "He said — *it is not the first time a mission has gone wrong for me.* He said it would take a great deal more than that to get anything out of him." He stopped. "He was almost smug about it."

Korroc's shoulders came down an inch. Nageru saw it. It was relief, and it was the dwarf's own, and it was very plain.

Caleth turned and looked at Thane.

Thane had been leaning against the wall the whole time with his arms folded. He looked at the three of them. Then he shook his head, slightly, and shrugged.

"Nothing," he said. "Not a word of it rang false. Not one."

"That is good," said Nageru.

"Aye." Thane did not uncross his arms. "It is. If the spell took."

Korroc frowned. "Did it not take?"

"It went on him like it ought. Felt like it always feels." Thane's eyes were on the flagstones. "Only I've never had it on a man who's been through what he's been through. In a collar like that. With needles in him a week. I'd swear to every word he said in a court of law." He looked up. "I'd not swear the spell was working while he said it."

Nobody said anything.

Caleth stood in the yellow light with his hand on his coat and his face very composed.

"Then we know as much as we knew this morning," he said. "Which is that he is home. And that he walked."

---

They went to Irabeth.

She was in the command room with a fresh bandage on her forearm that she was plainly resenting and Aron beside her with another under his tabard, and both of them looked as though they had been going since before the horns. She heard Caleth out standing up. He told it as he had told it at the well, in order, and at the end of it he said, plainly, that he found the escape suspicious — and that he had looked, and could find nothing wrong with the elf's story. Not one thing.

Irabeth listened to all of it with her eyes on the table.

"I'll have to debrief him myself," she said when he had finished. "Properly. But not now." She was quiet a moment. "My own questions can wait. He's earned a night's sleep before I start in on him."

Nobody argued.

Then she lifted her head and looked at the four of them, and her face changed. It went from the face of a woman who had been glad that morning to the face of the commander of Drezen.

"However," she said. "If they keep sending attacks like that one, we have a problem."

"How bad?" said Thane.

"We have only so many soldiers up here. You saw what came through that wall. You saw what they did to the north range before Nageru got there." She looked at him, briefly, and away. "And we are running out of ballista bolts. We were short before this morning. We're shorter now."

"How many?" said Korroc.

Irabeth told him.

Korroc's face did not move, but Nageru saw him think about forges, and how many there were in Drezen, and how many hours there were in a day, and how many of those hours he had.

---

That evening Nageru ate in the long hall under the old keep, where the garrison ate.

It was crowded and loud and smelled of stew and wet wool, and there were men there with their arms in slings and their heads bandaged telling one another about the glabrezu, how big it had been, how it had come through the wall. Nobody paid him any particular mind. He found that he liked it. He took his bowl to the end of a bench and ate.

Across the hall, at a table near the fire, sat Korroc and Thane. And between them, with her sleeves still rolled to the elbow and stone-dust in the lines of her hands, sat Dagna Stonevein.

She was talking. Nageru could not hear her over the room, but he could see her — one square hand chopping the air, her face set. He gathered from the way her nephew was grinning into his bowl that she was saying a number of things about demons, and about walls, and about the general inconsiderateness of creatures who came through a perfectly good wall that a perfectly good mason had only finished pointing the week before, and who would now have to point it again. Korroc was nodding in all the places a son nods.

And then, slowly, Nageru watched her stop.

He watched her put her hand down flat on the table. He watched her look from one of them to the other, and something in her face went quiet and warm, and she said something short. Thane looked at his bowl. Korroc put his arm along the back of the bench behind her, not quite around her, and she did not move away from it.

Nageru did not go over.

He finished his stew. He did not hurry it. He watched the three of them, from time to time, through the noise and the smoke of the hall, the way one watches a lamp in a window across a dark street. It was not his to sit at. He did not want it to be. It was enough that it was there.

---

He saw Caleth again late, crossing the west court.

Nageru was on the wall above it, walking — he did not sleep well after fighting; he never had — and he looked down, and there they were. Two figures coming out of the infirmary door into the lamplight. One tall and upright, one leaning.

Aravashnial was walking.

Not well. He was in a borrowed robe a size too large for him, and his feet were bound in cloth, and he was leaning on Caleth's arm with a good deal of his weight. But he was walking, across the court, toward the west range and the narrow stair that went up to his own rooms.

Behind them, in the infirmary door, stood Sosiel.

He stood there with his arms folded, watching them go, and Nageru saw him lift his face to the dark sky with the expression of a man asking his god for patience and suspecting he would not get it.

At the foot of the stair, Caleth stopped and said something. The elf answered. Caleth's head went down, the way it did when he was listening to something he would not have chosen to hear, and then came up again, and they went up the stair together, slowly, one step at a time.

Nageru watched until they were inside.

A light went on, after a while, in a window of the west range. It burned there a long time.

He walked the rest of the wall, and then he went to bed.

---

The next days were quiet.

Nageru did not trust them, and he was not alone. The watch on the walls was doubled and then trebled and nobody had to be told to stay awake on it. Every forge in Drezen was making bolt heads. Korroc spent a day and most of a night at the Purity Forge with Joran Vhane and the two of them did not, so far as Nageru could tell, exchange ten words between them, and turned out bolt heads in a stream that the quartermaster's men carried away in baskets. Dagna and her masons rebuilt the wall the glabrezu had come through, and Dagna was heard on the second day to say that she had built it *better* this time, *and let the next one try it*.

Nageru helped where he was asked. He carried stone for Dagna, who did not thank him and did not need to. He sat with the boy from the north court, whose legs worked now but did not yet believe it. He sat in the cathedral on Paradise Hill in the morning, in the small corner of it that Sosiel had given to Irori, and was still.

And he watched Caleth.

He did not set out to. He found that he could not stop. Caleth went to the west range in the morning before his scrying, and again at midday, and again at evening, and once in the middle of the night that Nageru knew of. He went up the narrow stair to Aravashnial's rooms and was there sometimes a short while and sometimes a long one. He came down again with his face composed and his hand on his coat.

Nobody asked him about it.

On the evening of the second day, Nageru found him alone on the north parapet, where the quasit had come down with the torn book, a week and more ago now. He was looking north, over the dark plain.

Nageru stood beside him for a time.

"He is well?" he said at last.

"He is mending." Caleth did not look round. "His ribs. His feet. He sleeps a great deal. He reads, when he is awake, until Sosiel comes and takes the book away." A pause. "He has asked for his staff."

Nageru waited.

"There is more of it now," Caleth said. "Did you know? Aron's people found the rest of it in the rubble of the tower when they cleared it. Two more pieces. The foot of it, and the part above. I have had all four." He was quiet a moment. "I used them. When I scried for him. Every morning. A thing that is a person's own is the best focus there is, after blood. I carried them in my bag."

He said it plainly. Nageru heard what was under it, and did not say so.

"And now he has them back," he said.

"And now he has them back." Caleth looked down at his own hands on the stone. "He said, *You have been carrying this around?* And I said yes." He let out a breath that might, in another man, have been a laugh. "He looked at me as though I had confessed to something."

Nageru said nothing for a while.

"You did," he said, at last, gently.

Caleth turned his head and looked at him. It was a long look, in the near-dark. Nageru met it and did not ask anything, and after a while the half-elf looked back out at the plain.

They stood there until the stars came out.

---

On the third night, Nageru dreamed.

---

Sleep came quietly. At first there was nothing unusual about it.

He was walking.

His feet were bare, as they always were, and the stone under them was cold. He did not know where he was. He knew at once that the place was old.

Moonlight lay across broken walls. Vines had climbed the fallen stones and gone over them and down the other side. Roofs had come in under the weight of years, and somewhere in the dark, slowly, something was turning in the wind and creaking.

Then —

A bell.

One note. Deep. It came rolling across the ruins out of the dark and went *through* him — not past him, through, the way the old chant at the Sunken Fist had gone through him as a child, so that his bones hummed with it after.

*Gooooong.*

And for one instant, the ruins changed.

He saw them as they had been. The walls whole. Torches burning along them. Armored figures walking the battlements in the torchlight, slowly, the way men walk a wall they do not expect to have to fight on. A small shrine beneath a tower, with a lamp burning in it. And in the southeastern corner of the fortress, hung high in its own tower, a great bell.

Then it was gone. The ruins came back. The moon.

And he understood that he was not alone.

Someone was running.

A woman. He saw only pieces of her — black hair, very pale skin, the flash of something red, a cloth or a sash, going through a broken doorway and gone.

Then wings. Dark ones. Folded close as she ran, and opening as she stumbled, and closing again.

An arrow struck the stone beside her and broke. Another went through the edge of one wing, and she cried out, and kept running.

She turned.

He saw the horns.

Two. Black, curving back from her brow into the black hair. And her face under them, in the moonlight, very beautiful, and very tired, and he knew what she was. He had known it, he thought, before he saw the horns. He had known it from the wings.

A succubus.

She had a bow. She raised it, and drew, and fired back into the dark she had come from, and something in that dark screamed. Then she ran again.

The dream carried him after her.

He did not walk. He did not need to. The world moved past him, and he stayed where he was, and was always behind her. Courtyards, broken. Buildings fallen in on themselves. Trees standing dead in what had been gardens. And things moving in the ruins all around — shapes, prowling, in ones and twos and threes. He saw demons. He saw men, cultists, in dark mail, with bows. And he saw —

Something else.

Something very large. For one moment only, it was clear to him: a woman, if it could be called a woman. Tall — taller than any wall in this place had been built for — and long in the limb, unnaturally long, so that she went hunched beneath the broken arches to keep her head below them. Searching. Her head turning, slowly, this way and that.

Then the image slid away from him like a fish.

The running woman reached a tower. An old one, narrow, with its top fallen in. She went through the door at its foot and dragged the door shut behind her and set her back against it, and he heard things being moved, heavy things, wood dragged across stone, wedged. Then her breathing.

Then nothing.

---

The dream went quiet.

He was inside the tower. A small round room, with a narrow window high in the wall and moonlight coming through it in one pale bar across the floor. She sat beneath the window with her back to the wall and her bow across her knees.

She was exhausted. He could see it in the way she sat — not resting, only stopped. There was dried blood along one arm, from the shoulder to the wrist. Her wings were drawn in tight around her, the way a bird draws in its wings against cold.

For a long moment she did nothing but stare at the floor.

Then she spoke.

Not to him. To no one.

"*I cannot run again.*"

Outside, among the ruins, something moved. She closed her eyes.

"*I will not go back.*"

There was fear in it. He heard it. Not fear of dying — he had heard that fear in a great many voices, and it was not this. This was older, and deeper, and it had a floor under it that went down a very long way.

"*I will not become that again.*"

And then her eyes opened.

And she looked straight at him.

The dream stopped.

All of it. The wind outside. The small moving things in the ruins. The bar of moonlight on the floor, which had been trembling, very slightly, with the moving of the clouds, held perfectly still. He had not known a dream could do that. He did not think dreams could.

Her face changed.

Surprise. He watched it happen — the tiredness go out of it all at once, and the fear, and something come in under them both that he could not at first name. She was looking *at* him. Not past him, not through him. At him. As though he were standing in that room.

She could see him.

She rose. Slowly, the way one rises when one is afraid that moving quickly will make a thing vanish.

"*You heard me,*" she said.

She sounded as though she could not believe it. Her wings shifted behind her, uneasily, half-opening and closing again.

"*You are one of them,*" she said. "*One of the ones who took Drezen.*"

She hesitated.

Then:

"*Nageru.*"

He had never met this woman. He had never told her his name. He had never been near her in his life, unless —

Unless a cell, below Drezen. Unless a thousand butterflies scratched into granite with fingernails, and a prayer cut deeper in the middle of them. Unless a man's journal open on a desk and a name read out of it in Nageru's own voice. *Arueshalae.*

She knew his name.

Her gaze dropped, for a moment, to the floor between them.

"*I asked,*" she said, "*for someone who might listen. Before deciding what I am.*"

She looked back up at him.

"*And something answered.*"

---

The ruins opened under him.

He was above them now. High. He saw them spread out below him in the moonlight like a map on a table — an old fortress, hidden among broken hills, folded down into the land so that from more than a mile off it would be only one more line of rock. He saw its walls. Its courtyards. Its towers. He saw, small and clear, the old tower where she hid.

And his attention was pulled — he did not pull it; it was pulled — away from that tower, across the fortress, to another one. To the southeast.

To the bell.

The same bell. He knew it the instant he saw it. The voice that had begun the dream.

"*The Bell of Mercy,*" her voice said. It came with him through the vision, close, as though she were at his shoulder. "*They fear it.*"

Below him there were things moving through the ruins. He saw them now from above — the shapes, the prowlers, more of them than he had seen before. Ringed around the fortress. Coming in through the broken places.

"*They have surrounded this place,*" she said. "*They are hunting me.*"

And the enormous figure came again.

He saw her clearly this time. He wished he had not. A hag — he had no other word — a giant hideous woman, hunched and long, with claws on her hands that caught the moonlight the way iron catches it, and a smile on her face that went much too far across it. Much too far. From one side of her head nearly to the other.

"*This one leads them.*"

There was hatred in the voice at his shoulder. And under the hatred, the same fear.

"*She does not want to kill me,*" Arueshalae said. And then, after a pause: "*She wants to take me back.*"

The vision fell in on itself.

---

He was standing in the tower room again.

She stood before him. Not close. He saw, for the first time, how carefully she kept her distance from him: across the room, by the window, with the moonlight between them. She did not come nearer. She did not reach for him. Her hands stayed at her sides, open, where he could see them.

"*I know what you must think,*" she said.

Her eyes went, very briefly, to his wrist — to the string of worn wooden beads there, Horgus's beads, that he had worn so long he forgot them — and came back to his face.

"*A demon,*" she said, "*asking a holy man for help.*"

A small bitter smile came and went at the corner of her mouth.

"*I understand the absurdity.*"

Then it was gone. "*But I have nowhere else to turn.*"

Something struck the outside of the tower. Hard. The whole room shook with it, and dust came down out of the dark above in a thin gray rain. She looked toward the door — the barricade, the wood wedged against it — and when she looked back at him there was urgency in her face that had not been there before.

"*If you come,*" she said, "*do not come blindly. Remember what I have shown you.*"

And the fortress went into him.

He did not see it again. He *had* it. All at once, entire, the way he had the forms of the Sunken Fist after twenty years of practicing them — the broken approaches through the hills, and which of them were open and which were held. The courtyards, and what was in them. The narrow tower where she hid. And clearest of all, brightest, burned into him like a mark: the southeastern tower.

The Bell of Mercy.

"*If you believe nothing else I have said,*" she said, "*believe this.*"

She stepped toward him.

One step. Only one.

"*I do not want to be what I was.*"

Another blow on the tower. Harder. The floor jumped under his feet, and he felt the dream begin to come apart — not to fade, to *break*, the way ice breaks on a pond, lines running out across it from somewhere he could not see.

"*Nageru —*"

The dark came into the room.

It came from every corner at once, pouring, and the bar of moonlight went out under it, and the walls went, and her face went, and her voice went far away, as though she were calling to him from the bottom of a well.

"*Please.*"

The tower was gone. The ruins were gone.

And just before the last of it went, from very far off, he heard her one more time.

"*Find me.*"

---

He opened his eyes.

He was in Drezen. In his own narrow room in the east range, on his own pallet, with his own blanket over him. It was still night. The shutter over the window was closed and there was no light behind it.

For several breaths he lay still.

He could hear it. Very faint. Fading. The last of a bell's voice going out across a great distance — the long tail of a note, the hum that is left in the air after a struck thing has stopped being struck.

Then it was gone.

He did not move. He lay with his hands at his sides and looked at the dark ceiling and let his heart slow, and went through it. All of it. In order. The way Master Borin had taught him to go through a fight after he had fought it, move by move, so that the body would remember what the mind had not had time to.

The bell. The ruins. The running woman, and the wings, and the horns.

*Nageru.*

The hag with the smile.

*I asked for someone who might listen before deciding what I am.*

He had never seen that fortress. Not in his waking life. Not on any map. He had never been west of the fane, and before the fane he had never been west of Drezen at all.

He knew exactly what it looked like.

He knew where she was hiding.

He knew how to get there.

He lay still a while longer. Then he sat up, and crossed his legs, and put his hands open on his knees, in the dark, and did the only thing he had ever known to do with a thing that large. He sat with it.

He did not try to understand it. *Expectation is a poor substitute for observation.* He had observed a dream. He did not know what had sent it, or what it was, or whether the woman in it was the woman it seemed to be. He did not know if it was true.

But he knew it was not *his*. He knew his own dreams, the way a man knows his own handwriting. He had dreamed of the Sunken Fist and of his mother's lamp and of the caravan, a thousand times, and of the cave under Kenabres, and lately of a statue that kept its seat while a demon's face was carved over it.

This was not one of those.

*And something answered.*

He sat with that a long time. He thought of a hall full of monks who had not been there, remembering a temple back into being. *We have waited through generations. For the day our brother would awaken.* He thought of the word *son*, which he had not let himself think since the road home from the fane, and which he did not let himself think more of now.

*Something answered.* That was all she had said. She had not said what.

He did not know what had answered her.

He was very much afraid that he did.

He sat until the shutter went gray at the edges. Then he got up, and dressed, and went to find his friends.

---

Korroc was the first he found.

Nageru had thought he might be. The dwarf had been sleeping, the last few nights, in a room off the forge-yard so that he could be at the Purity Forge by first light, and he was there now, at the water-trough in the yard with his sleeves rolled up and his face wet, blowing like a horse.

He looked up as Nageru came across the yard, and stopped blowing.

"What," he said.

Nageru stopped in front of him.

He did not know what was on his face. He had not looked. He only knew that whatever it was, Korroc had read it from forty feet away, in the gray before dawn, and had put down the dipper.

"I had a dream," Nageru said. "It was disturbing. And I think we need to look into it."

Korroc looked at him a moment longer.

Then he dried his face on his sleeve and reached for his shirt.

"Caleth'll be up," he said. "He's always up. He'll be at the elf's, this time of the morning. Come on."

---

The west range stair was dark and narrow and went up three turns, and at the top of it there were two doors.

The first stood open. Nageru looked in as they passed and saw a small bedroom — a narrow bed, made, a borrowed robe folded at the foot of it, a stool, a window. No one in it.

The second door, a little further on, was half open, and there was lamplight behind it.

They heard Caleth's voice before they reached it.

"— try again?"

Nageru stopped in the doorway, and Korroc stopped at his shoulder.

It was Aravashnial's study. He had been in it once before, with Caleth, the day after the dragon, when the two of them had searched it and found what Caleth had found and Nageru had not asked about. It was the same room. The same high shelves, the same three stacks of paper on the long desk, the same window with the gray of the morning coming in. A candle on the desk, unlit.

Thane stood just inside the door, against the wall. He glanced at them as they came, and moved over without a word to let them in, and looked back at the desk.

Caleth stood beside it.

And Aravashnial sat behind it.

He was dressed. That was the first thing Nageru saw. A proper robe, his own, dark and plain. His hair combed back. His feet in soft boots. The bruising on his face had gone from black to green and yellow, and the swelling down the one side was nearly gone. He looked like a man who had been very ill and was not, any longer, very ill — only worn, the way a blade is worn after a long campaign, every edge of him a little thinner than it had been.

On the desk in front of him, in a neat row, lay four pieces of an old pale staff.

And beside them, on a sheet of paper covered in his own small crabbed writing, lay the collar.

Black metal. The green stone at the front of it, dull in the lamplight. Somebody had turned it so that the ring of needles faced upward, the way a smith lays out a piece he means to study.

Aravashnial was looking at the candle.

His face, Nageru thought, was very calm. Too calm. It was the calm of a man holding very still on purpose because he did not trust what would happen if he moved.

He lifted one hand. He spoke. It was a short phrase, a cantrip — Nageru did not know the words but knew the shape of them, the way one knows the shape of a common prayer in a language one does not speak. Caleth had said such things over a hundred campfires. The elf's fingers made the small turning motion that went with it, precisely, the way a master writes a letter he has written ten thousand times.

The candle did not light.

Nothing happened. Nothing at all.

Nageru felt it in the room — not a thing happening; the absence of one. The way one feels a step that is not there in the dark.

Aravashnial's hand stayed in the air a moment longer. Then he lowered it to the desk, and laid it flat.

"Again," said Caleth. Quietly.

The elf did it again. All the words right. Nageru did not need to know the words to know that; he could hear it. There was no hesitation in them and no error. He had heard Caleth stumble on a casting perhaps twice in a year, and it had sounded nothing like this. This was a man doing everything perfectly.

The candle sat there, unlit.

Caleth lifted his own hand. He said the same short phrase — Nageru knew it now; it was the same — and made the same small turning motion with his fingers.

The candle flared up at once, bright, and burned.

Aravashnial looked at it.

Nageru had seen him wilt once before. On the floor of a ruined library under Kenabres, when a burned half-blind elf had understood what had been done to the Blackwing. He had not thought to see it again.

He saw it now. It was not much. The shoulders, a little. The hands, flat on the desk, a little flatter. The face did not change at all.

"What," said Korroc, very low, "was that."

Caleth did not answer him. He did not look round. He had come close to the desk, and bent over it, and he was looking at the elf the way Nageru had seen him look at a lock he could not open, and at the Pauper's Thighbone on the road, and at the letter on the parapet — with the whole of himself.

"When you took this off me," Aravashnial said. He did not look up. He had laid one finger on the black collar. "What happened?"

The question was for the doorway.

"Nothing unusual," said Thane.

Aravashnial's head turned, slowly, and he looked at the dwarf.

"Picked it like a lock," Thane said. "It had a seam. Catch inside. Took a while. You held still." He paused. "Needles came out one at a time. You made a noise. Then it was off." He paused again. "Nothing else. Nothing I saw."

"Why do you ask?" said Caleth.

Aravashnial looked at the candle burning in front of him.

"Because," he said, "it is off."

Nobody answered that.

Caleth drew the stool over from the wall and sat down on it, on the near side of the desk, so that the candle and the collar and the four pieces of the staff lay between them. He reached out and turned the elf's hand over, palm up, on the desk, and looked at it, as though there might be something written there. He looked at the ring of healing wounds around the elf's throat. He took the collar up off the paper and turned it in his fingers and looked at the inside of it, at each of the needles, one after another, and set it down again exactly where it had been.

He spoke, under his breath. Something went out of him over the desk, faint and cool — Nageru felt it pass, the way one feels a draft — and Caleth's eyes went somewhere that was not quite the room.

They stood and watched him do it.

It took a long time. The light in the window went from gray to the pale clean yellow of a clear morning. Somewhere below, the garrison was at its breakfast; the sound of it came up the stair in pieces. Korroc shifted his weight and stopped himself. Thane did not move at all. Aravashnial sat with his hands on the desk and his eyes on Caleth's face, the whole time, and Nageru watched him watching, and could not read what was in it.

The better part of an hour went by like that.

At the end of it Caleth sat back.

He did not say anything. He looked at the collar, and at the elf, and at nothing. His face was very still. And then he turned on the stool and looked at the doorway for the first time, and saw Nageru, and Nageru saw him see something in his face that stopped him.

"Nageru," Caleth said.

Nageru did not know what was in his face. He had not looked. But he knew it was not the face he wore every day, the still one, the one his masters had given him. That one had been left somewhere in a tower room with a bar of moonlight on the floor.

"I had a dream," he said. "It was disturbing. I think we need to look into it."

---

He told them.

He told it in that room, standing, with the candle burning down on the desk and the collar beside it, because that was where they were and he did not think it could wait for another room. He told it in order. From the beginning. The cold stone. The bell. The ruins as they were and the ruins as they had been. The woman running, and the arrows, and the wings. The horns. The bow. The giant in the ruins. The tower and the door and the barricade.

*I cannot run again. I will not go back. I will not become that again.*

He gave them all of it. Every word she had spoken, as nearly as he could. He had always been able to do that — give a thing back the way it had been given to him; it was the first thing the Sunken Fist taught, before the forms, before anything. He gave them the dream stopping, and the moonlight holding still, and her rising slowly to her feet.

*You heard me.*

When he came to his own name, he stopped.

He had not meant to. He found that he had to.

"She said my name," he said.

The room was very quiet.

"*Nageru.* She knew it. I have never met her. I have never spoken to her." He looked at Caleth. "But I have spoken her name. Out loud. Twice. Below this citadel, in front of her cell. And at the fane, when we found the bounty."

"Arueshalae," said Korroc.

It was not a question. Nageru heard that it was not. Korroc had been in that cell. All four of them had. They had stood in it with the light of their lamps going up the walls, over a thousand butterflies.

"Yes," said Nageru.

"The succubus," said Thane. "From the cell. Black hair? Pale?"

"Yes."

"With a bounty on her head the size of a dragon's hoard." Thane's eyes went to Caleth. "*What you pay for something you're afraid of.*"

Caleth did not answer. He was watching Nageru.

Nageru went on. He gave them what she had said next — *I asked for someone who might listen, before deciding what I am. And something answered.* He gave them the fortress from above, the walls and the courtyards and the bell in its tower, and her voice at his shoulder naming it. He gave them the giant woman, the iron claws and the smile, and the voice saying *she does not want to kill me; she wants to take me back.*

He gave them the beads. *A demon asking a holy man for help. I understand the absurdity.*

He gave them the last of it. The step she took toward him — only one. *I do not want to be what I was.* The dark. *Please.* *Find me.*

Then he stopped.

Nobody spoke for a time.

Aravashnial was looking at him across the desk with an expression Nageru could not read. Korroc had folded his arms and was looking at the floor. Thane had his head on one side, the way he did when he was taking a thing apart in his mind to see how it had been made.

Caleth said, "You know where she is."

It was not a question either.

"Yes," said Nageru. "I could walk to it. If I knew where to begin walking."

---

They argued about it.

It was not a hard argument, as their arguments went. Nobody shouted. Nobody walked out. But Nageru had known it would come, and he had known, before it began, which way most of it would go, and he found as it went that he had been right.

"It's a trap," said Thane.

He said it without heat, the way a man says *it's raining*. "Has to be considered, anyhow. Somebody's put a bounty on her that'd buy a town. Somebody knows we've got her name. Somebody knows *your* name." He looked at Nageru. "Easiest way in the world to get the four of us out a week's walk from anywhere is to dangle something in front of the one of us who won't walk past it."

Nageru inclined his head. "It may be."

"But you don't think so."

"I do not know," said Nageru. "I am telling you what I saw."

Caleth was sitting on the stool by the desk with his elbows on his knees and his hands together. He had not said anything for some time.

"I have read every word Staunton Vhane wrote about her," he said. "Twice. I have stood in that cell." He looked at the floor. "A thousand butterflies. And the prayer. *I am ready. Finally ready.* And I believe that was carved. I believe it was carved by her, over years, with her own nails. I do not doubt a word of it."

He looked up.

"And I have always thought," he said, "that a demon is a demon."

He did not say it unkindly. Nageru heard that. He said it the way he said everything he believed: carefully, completely, as though he had arrived at it by a long road and was not going to pretend he had not.

Nageru nodded slowly.

"I have wondered," he said, "whether something might be acting on her. On what she is." He chose the words with care. "Something mythic. Or magical. Some power, that has changed her. The way the wardstone changed us. Or the way the elixir changed Staunton, but the other way."

Caleth looked at him.

"I know," said Nageru, gently. "That is why I was wondering."

Caleth held his eyes a moment longer. Then he looked away, and something in his face eased by the smallest degree, and he did not argue.

Korroc had been quiet all this time. Now he unfolded his arms.

"She asked," he said, "for somebody who'd listen before deciding what she was." He looked at the floor between his boots. "Seems to me there's a lot of that about, this last while. Fellows asking. Under this citadel." He did not say *Joran.* He did not have to. "And she's to be taken *back*, she says. And she'd rather not."

He looked up at Nageru.

"Aye," he said. "I'd go and look."

Thane let out a breath through his nose.

"Never said I wouldn't go," he said. "Said it might be a trap." He scratched his jaw. "A trap's only a door somebody's thought about first. I'm good with doors." He looked at the ceiling. "Good luck to her, I said. Back at the fane. When we read the bounty." His mouth twisted. "Didn't think I'd be asked to deliver it."

And that, Nageru understood, was the argument over.

---

The rest of it was the question of how.

"Can you not take us there?" said Thane. "Straight there. The way you took us to the elf in the cave." He turned to Caleth. "He's seen the place. He's got the bell. He can tell you every stone of it."

Caleth was already shaking his head.

"I saw that cavern," he said. "For eight seconds. In water. And it was a near thing even so, and I had seen it with my own eyes." He looked at Nageru. "This is a place I have never seen. Through the eyes of a man who has never seen it either, by way of a dream sent by somebody we have never met. If I am wrong about it by a little, we come out of the fold inside a wall. If I am wrong by a great deal, we come out of it somewhere else entirely, and I do not know where, and neither do you." He shook his head. "No. Not on that. Not with the four of us."

Thane did not argue. He had, Nageru thought, expected that answer, and had only wanted it said aloud.

"Then where is it?" said Korroc.

They all turned, after a moment, to the desk.

Aravashnial had not spoken since Nageru began. He had sat behind the desk with his hands flat on it and listened, and his eyes had never left Nageru's face. Now he looked down at the papers in front of him, and was quiet a moment, and then he shook his head.

"I do not know it," he said.

He said it simply, without embarrassment. "A fortress, among broken hills, with a bell. A shrine beneath one tower. Old, by the sound of it — older than the Wound, perhaps." He frowned. "It must be far to the west. Further than I have been. I have never seen such a place, and I have never read of one that matches it."

He got up — carefully, one hand on the desk — and went to the shelves, and began to take down maps.

He spread them on the desk over the papers and the collar. Old ones, most of them; Nageru recognized the brown soft-edged look of the survey that had shown them the passages under the citadel's west side, a lifetime ago. The elf went over them one after another with a long finger, slowly, his lips moving. He went over the west of each one twice.

At the end of it he straightened, and shook his head again.

"I could guess," he said. "I could give you six places it might be. I would not stake four lives on any one of them."

"Nageru could draw it," said Korroc. "What he saw. The walls and that. If he drew it, somebody might know it."

Nageru looked at his own hands.

"I could try," he said doubtfully. "I have never drawn anything."

"Nobody here has seen it to know it," said Caleth. "That is the difficulty. It would be a fine drawing of a place none of us can find."

He rubbed his eyes. "Horses," he said. "It will have to be horses. We go west and Nageru leads, and we find it the slow way."

Korroc made a sound of deep pain at the word *horses*. Then he stopped. Nageru saw a thought arrive on his face.

"My mother," he said.

They looked at him.

"She knew the waystation," said Korroc. "Didn't she. Knew it when none of us did, nor the elf, nor any map in this room. Knew it from seventy-five years gone." He was already moving for the door. "She knows this country. She and Helja. They knew it before any of us were born. Come on, cousin."

Thane went with him.

---

They were not gone long. When they came back they came to the command room, where the rest of them had gone with the maps, and Korroc's face was the face of a man who has gone to the well and come back with half a bucket.

"*That must be way to the west,*" he reported. He did his mother's voice without meaning to, Nageru thought — the dryness, the snap at the end. "*Past the Gray Road. The old river.*" He scratched his beard. "And then a great deal about the country out there, and what it was like when she was a girl, and where a fortress might've been, and where it'd make sense to put one. But she's not sure. She doesn't know it. She said there were a lot of old forts out west, once. Crusaders built one every time they took a hill." He shrugged. "She said she'd think on it."

So they went to the maps.

They spent the rest of the morning over them in the command room, with Irabeth looking in from time to time and Aron coming and going. They spread every map Drezen had across the long table and weighted them with daggers and inkwells, and went over the west of each one. Caleth read them. Aravashnial read them. Korroc read them and complained that half of them disagreed with the other half about where the Gray Road even was. Nageru stood at the end of the table and looked at the lines and the hatching and the little drawn hills, and tried to find in any of it the shape that was burned into him, and could not.

It was not a map. It was a place. He knew it the way he knew a room in the dark. He did not know how to make a line on a page say *here.*

Thane left a little before midday.

He did not say where he was going. He simply put down the corner of the map he had been holding, and stood looking at nothing for a moment with the expression he wore when something had occurred to him that he did not yet trust — and then went out.

He came back a good while later. He was not alone.

Two of Irabeth's guards came in first, and then Thane, and then, between two more guards, Joran Vhane.

The dwarf stopped just inside the door. He was in his forge leathers still, with soot on his forearms and a burn on the back of one hand. He looked at the room — at the long table, at the maps on it, at Irabeth, at Aravashnial — and his face did nothing, the way it always did nothing.

Thane did not explain. He only lifted a hand toward the table. *There.*

Nageru watched Joran look at Thane. He watched the two dwarves look at each other for a moment, the way men look at each other who have stood guard over each other at a forge for some weeks, and have not liked each other the whole time, and have come to a kind of arrangement about it.

Then Joran walked to the table.

He did not ask what they were looking for. Perhaps Thane had told him on the way. Perhaps he could see it, in the way they were all standing over the west of the map. He looked down at it for a while — at all of it, at the whole sprawl of the Wound and the Marchlands spread out in brown ink — and his eyes went slowly west.

Then he put out one hand and laid one finger on the map.

It was a long way west. A very long way. Past the long line of the Gray Road and the dead river beside it, and out beyond that into a part of the map where whoever had drawn it had grown less sure of themselves, and the hills were drawn smaller and further apart, and there was a great deal of nothing.

His finger did not shake. Nageru noticed that. The hands that had stopped being able to heal anything were perfectly steady on the paper.

"There," Joran said.

That was all he said.

Nobody asked him how he knew. Nageru saw Caleth open his mouth to, and close it again. He saw Irabeth look at the dwarf for a long moment with her commander's face, and then at Thane, and say nothing. He saw Thane not look at anybody.

Nageru looked at the place under the finger.

It was not on the map. There was nothing there, only a few small drawn hills. But he looked at it, and something in him that had been holding its breath since the tower room let it out.

*Yes.*

He did not know how he knew. He knew.

"Thank you," he said to Joran.

The dwarf looked at him. Then he nodded, very slightly, and took his finger off the map, and the guards took him out.

---

Caleth measured it with his fingers, against the scale at the bottom of the map.

"A week," he said. "At the least. If nothing gets in the way." He straightened. "Nothing will fail to get in the way."

"So, horses," said Korroc heavily.

"So, horses." Caleth looked at the map. "Or—"

He stopped. Nageru watched him look at the map again, at the long line west, and he watched the counting start behind his eyes.

"The alternative," Caleth said, "would be to fold to the temple of Irori. The fane. I have been there. I know it." He traced a line west with one finger. "And walk from there."

Thane leaned over and put his own finger on the place. Thirty miles west of Drezen. A small mark on the map that somebody — Aron, Nageru thought — had added in fresh ink since they came back from it.

"If we could at least fold to there," Thane said, "it'd save a couple of days."

Caleth nodded slowly. He was still counting.

"It would take something out of me," he said. "All of us, and the horses." He was quiet a moment. "Three castings. I could do it in three." He nodded again, more firmly, to himself. "I can do that."

"Not a horse for me," said Nageru.

Caleth glanced at him. "You will run a week?"

"I have run further," said Nageru. And then, because Korroc was looking at him with an expression of deep personal betrayal: "Brother. It is not a thing to envy."

"It is," said Korroc. "It very much is."

---

Aravashnial had been in the room all morning. He had sat at the far end of the long table with one of the old maps in front of him, and Nageru had glanced at him from time to time, and the elf had always been reading. He had not spoken since the study, except about the maps.

Now he stood up.

He did it carefully, one hand on the table, the way he had done everything for three days. And he came down the length of the table to where they stood over the map, and stopped, and looked at Caleth.

"May I go?" he said.

Caleth looked at him.

"I can still be useful," said Aravashnial. "I cannot cast. I know that. You need not be delicate about it." His voice was very level. "But I know a great deal. About the country out there, a little. About demons, a great deal more. About what hunts a demon, and why, and what it wants with her when it finds her." He paused. "That is worth something. Even without a spell in me."

Caleth frowned.

Nageru watched him frown. It was not the frown of a man who was going to say no. It was the frown of a man who had wanted to be asked, and had not wanted it to be like this.

"I'll agree," Caleth said at last. "If you will let me cast a spell on you."

Aravashnial paused. He looked, Nageru thought, a little surprised.

"I want to try," said Caleth, "to dispel what is on you."

The elf was silent for a moment. Then he nodded.

"I can agree to that," he said.

Caleth stepped back from him. Two paces. He set his feet. He drew in a breath, and Nageru saw his hand go — not to the rod at his belt, not to the Thighbone, but to nothing; he wanted it clean. And he began to speak.

It was a long working. Longer than most of what Caleth cast in a fight. Nageru felt it build in the room — the air going tight, the way the air goes before thunder — and Caleth's voice rising through it, steady, precise, and his hands shaping something between them that Nageru could not see and could feel, a thing like a blade made of unmaking. Whatever was on the elf, this would find it. Whatever had been done to him, this would take it apart.

Caleth let it go.

It went into Aravashnial.

Nageru saw it land. He saw the elf's eyes close, and his shoulders go back very slightly, the way a man's do when a wave breaks over him on a beach. He saw it go *in*. It landed solidly. It did not miss.

And then — nothing.

It did not catch. That was the only way Nageru could have said it. It went into the elf the way water goes into dry ground, looking for a root, and there was no root. It went over him and down him, searching, and found nothing to take hold of. Nothing to break. Nothing there.

It slid off him like rain off a slate roof, and was gone.

Aravashnial opened his eyes.

Caleth stood two paces off with his hands still raised.

Nageru looked at his face. He had seen it in a great many places in a year. On a ceiling in a cave. Over a broken staff. On a parapet with two letters in his hand. It had never once looked like this. It did not look like anything. It was as though everything in it had been set down at once, very carefully, the way Dagna had set down her mallet in the yard.

Disappointment. Nageru had no other word, and that word was much too small.

"Nothing," Caleth said.

He said it very quietly. Almost to himself. He shook his head.

Then he lowered his hands.

Nageru watched him do the next thing, and would remember it. He watched the half-elf stand there for one breath — one — with nothing at all in his face. And then he watched him put it away. All of it. Pick it up and fold it and put it somewhere inside his coat, with whatever else was there, and close the coat over it.

"Very well," Caleth said. His voice was perfectly level. "Be ready. We leave within the hour."

Aravashnial looked at him a moment longer.

"Thank you," he said.

Caleth nodded, once, and turned back to the map.

---

They gathered in the courtyard before the hour was out, with the sun near the top of the sky.

It was the same courtyard. The flagstones were still scarred where the glabrezu had stood, and where the drakes had fallen; somebody had scrubbed at the worst of it and given up. Dagna's new wall stood across the gap at the far end, raw-edged and pale against the old stone. It looked, Nageru thought, rather better than the old one.

Five people. Four horses.

Korroc approached his with the grave and wary courtesy of a man greeting a dangerous relative. Thane had chosen a smaller animal than last time, a dun mare with a short back and a look of great patience, and had clearly given the choice a good deal of thought. Caleth's was the tall gray he always rode. And the fourth was a quiet bay for Aravashnial, who stood beside it in a traveling cloak with nothing in his hands and nothing across his back — the staff had stayed on his desk, in its four pieces — and who looked at the horse the way he looked at anything new: with interest.

Irabeth came down to see them go. So did Sosiel, who did not say anything at all to Aravashnial, but looked at him, and then at Caleth, with an expression that conveyed with great economy what he thought of the whole enterprise and whose responsibility it would be.

Caleth stood in the middle of the courtyard, and they came in around him, the four of them and three horses, and he began to speak.

---

The world folded.

It came back as wind. A cold high wind, off the hills, and the smell of dry grass and old stone, and under it — so faint he could not have said whether it was there — something like incense.

Nageru opened his eyes, and was home.

No. Not home. He corrected himself at once, gently, the way his masters had corrected him as a boy when he said a thing that was nearly true. Not home. He had a home, and it was in the foothills to the north, with a waterwheel and a library and two old people in it. But he stood on the flat stone shelf before the cliff, and he looked up, and there it was, cut into the face of the rock: the fane.

It was whole.

He had not been sure it would be. He had left it whole, a month ago — more — with a hall full of monks who were not there, mending it with their hands while he knelt. But he had not been sure, in some small place, that it had not been a thing he had seen once and would not see again. That he would not come back and find Baphomet's face on the cliff, and the blood-runes, and the bone pit.

It was whole. The doors stood open. The long steps up to them were swept. Above the door, cut deep into the stone, were the old signs of his order that he had grown up under, clean-edged as the day they had been cut.

The horses shied and stamped. Korroc, holding his by both the reins and a fistful of mane, said something to it in Dwarven about the virtue of standing still. Caleth was already speaking again, eyes shut, and then he was gone, and the place where he had been standing was empty, and a moment later he was back with Thane and Aravashnial and the last horse, and he swayed.

Only a little. Nageru was beside him before it was more than a little. Caleth put a hand on his shoulder, and stood a moment with his eyes shut, and then took the hand away.

"Three," he said. "As promised."

"Sit down," said Nageru.

"In a moment." Caleth opened his eyes and looked up at the cliff. He looked at it a long time. "It's still here," he said.

"Yes," said Nageru.

They would not ride on today. Nobody had needed to say so. They would stay the rest of the day and the night under the cliff, and Caleth would sleep, and in the morning they would go west.

---

There were people.

He saw them when they came up the steps: a handful, no more. Five. A man and a woman of middle years, in the patched gray of crusade camp-followers, sitting on the top step with a pot over a small fire. An old man inside, by the door, asleep against the wall with a staff across his knees. And at the far end of the hall, at the feet of the statue, two young women kneeling side by side with their heads bowed.

They looked up when the party came in, and Nageru saw them see the swords and the plate and the half-elf with the bright sword at his hip, and go very still.

"Peace," said Nageru. "We are only passing."

The woman on the step looked at him — at his wraps, at his bare feet, at his face — for a long moment.

"We heard it was back," she said. "In the camps. Somebody said a temple had come back. Out here." She looked past him, at the hall. "We didn't believe it. We came to see." She looked back at him. "It is back."

"Yes," said Nageru.

She nodded slowly, as though he had confirmed something she had been told and had not quite let herself believe until a stranger said it, and went back to her pot.

Nageru went in.

The hall was as he had left it. The pillars. The high clean ceiling. The light coming down in long bars from the high windows cut into the rock. No dust on the floor; somebody — the five, perhaps — had been sweeping it. And at the far end, seated, with his hands open on his knees and his face turned a little upward, Irori.

He had kept his seat. They had carved a demon over him, and they had not been able to change how he sat.

The monks were not there.

Nageru had not expected them. He had told himself not to. He stood in the middle of the hall and looked, and the hall was only a hall: stone and light, a pair of kneeling women, a sleeping old man. No hum in the air. No voices remembered into it. No old man with amber eyes coming toward him across the floor to bow.

*You still have work to do. We will wait for you.*

He found that he did not mind. He found, standing there, that he was very slightly glad. It was a thing that had happened. It did not need to happen again for him to know it had.

He went to the foot of the statue, a little to one side of the two women, so as not to trouble them. He knelt. He sat back on his heels, and then folded his legs under him, and put his hands open on his knees.

He sat.

---

He sat the rest of that day.

He knew, distantly, that the others came and went. That Korroc brought the horses round into the shelter of the cliff and fed them and talked to them in a long low grumble that was very nearly affectionate. That Thane walked the shelf and the valley below it, both ways, twice, and came in and stood behind him for a while and went out again. That Caleth lay down on a bench along the wall with his coat over him and his hand on his chest and slept at last, properly, for hours, with the color slowly coming back into his face. That Aravashnial sat in a corner of the hall with a book and read, and turned the pages, and did not come near.

He knew it. He let it go by.

He did not try to understand anything. That was the first thing. He had come into the hall wanting to understand — the dream, the bell, the woman with the wings; what had answered her; what *work* meant, and *wait*, and *son* — and he set all of that down at the foot of the statue, one thing at a time, the way he would have set down his javelins at the door of the training hall.

Then he only sat. And breathed. And was still.

It was not quiet, in him. He had not expected it to be. But it was *his*, the not-quiet, and he could sit inside it and watch it without being moved by it, the way one watches weather through a window.

Some time in the afternoon one of the young women got up from her knees and went out, and the other stayed. She was praying under her breath, in a dialect of the north he half-knew from the monastery's kitchens. She was praying for her brother, who had gone to the crusade a long while ago and not written. Nageru did not listen. But he heard it, and when she finished, and went out, he found that he had said a word for the brother too, without deciding to, and did not take it back.

*A demon, asking a holy man for help.*

He had not thought about her all day. He thought about her now, without trying to.

*I asked for someone who might listen. Before deciding what I am.*

He looked up at the face of the statue above him. It was a calm face. It had been carved by someone who had loved it, and then a demon's face had been carved over it by someone who hated it, and then the demon's face had been taken away by people who were not there, and the calm face had come out from under it unchanged.

He had been told, in this room, what he was.

He had not asked to be. He had not asked anyone to decide it. An old man had walked across the floor and bowed to him and said a word, and the word had been true — he had felt it be true; it had gone into him and found its place and settled there as though there had always been a place cut for it. And he did not know what it meant. And everybody he loved had heard it and not one of them had spoken of it since, and he was grateful to them for that, in a way he did not know how to say.

He did not know what she was.

He did not know whether she was what she said. Caleth might be right. *A demon is a demon.* He had fought a great many of them, and killed a great many of them, and every one of them had been exactly what it was, all the way down.

But she had asked to be listened to before somebody decided.

He thought he could do that. He thought that, of all the things in the world, that was perhaps the one he had been made for.

*Expectation is a poor substitute for observation.*

He would go and observe.

*There is always another step.*

He sat there until the light in the high windows had gone from white to gold, and from gold to the long red of evening. Then he bowed to the statue, once, and unfolded himself, and got up, and went out to find his friends.

---

They had made a fire on the shelf before the doors, in the lee of the cliff, where the five pilgrims had made theirs. The pilgrims had moved down to the far end of the shelf to make room, and sat around their own small fire, talking low. The horses stood in a row against the rock with their heads down. The sky to the west was the color it always was out here, at sundown: a long smear of rust going to bruise, with the first stars coming through it.

Korroc had cooked. Nageru could tell, because it was very good and there was a great deal of it.

He sat down by the fire, and Korroc handed him a bowl without a word, and he ate.

Caleth was awake, and looked better. He sat with his back to the cliff and his coat around him and his hand where it always was. Thane sat cross-legged a little apart, with his knives laid out on a cloth in front of him in a row, going over each one with a whetstone. And across the fire from Nageru, on a folded blanket, sat Aravashnial, with his long hands around a cup of something hot and his face red-lit by the flames.

They talked about the road. About the Gray Road, and how to cross it, and whether there were fords, and what the country beyond it might be. Nageru told them what he could. He found he knew more than he had thought — not the road, but the end of it: the broken hills, the approaches through them, which way the ground ran. He drew some of it in the dirt by the fire with a stick, badly, and Caleth looked at it a long time and asked careful questions, and Nageru answered them.

And then he came, without meaning to, back to the dream.

To the hag.

He had not dwelt on her, in the telling. He had given her as he had seen her and gone on. But here, in the firelight, with the long dark of the Wound out past the shelf and a week of it between them and the bell, he found he wanted to say more of her. The way she had gone hunched beneath the arches. The way her head had turned. The claws catching the moonlight. And the smile.

"It went too far across her face," he said. "Much too far. From here" — he touched the corner of his mouth — "almost to here." He touched the hinge of his jaw, just under the ear.

Across the fire, Aravashnial set down his cup.

"An annis," he said.

They looked at him.

"An annis hag." He leaned forward a little, into the light. "That is what you saw. It can scarcely be anything else. The size of her — and the claws, and the teeth. They are iron, you know. Truly iron. Not a manner of speaking." He looked around the fire at them. "The greatest of the hags, and the cruelest, and very nearly the strongest thing that walks on two legs that is not a giant."

"Lovely," said Thane, without looking up from his whetstone.

"Staunton hired one," said Aravashnial.

Thane's whetstone stopped.

"It is in his journal," the elf said. "I read it — the whole of it, the week after you brought it up from below. Joran confirmed it to the council, after." He turned his cup in his hands. "When she escaped, Staunton sent hunters. They found traces and nothing else. And so he hired an annis hag, sworn to Sifkesh, to follow her. To follow, I believe his words were, *a trail made by betrayal and despair.*" He looked at Nageru. "I think you may have seen her."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Then she's still hunting," said Korroc slowly. "After all this. Staunton's dead, and she's still out there doing what he paid her for."

"Or someone is paying her now," said Caleth quietly. "For the same thing. With a thousand platinum."

*She does not want to kill me. She wants to take me back.*

"Back to what?" said Thane.

Nobody answered him.

Aravashnial went on.

He told them about hags. He told them a great deal, and all of it in order, and Nageru listened to all of it and kept what he could. That an annis would rather take hold of a man than strike him — that she would try to get her arms around you, and if she did, she would crush you against her, and rend you with the claws while she held you. That you must not let her get both arms around you. That she could wear another shape — smaller, older, kinder, a crone at a crossroads or a lost girl at the edge of a camp — and walk up to your fire in it and sit down. That she could call up fog. That a great deal of magic would not touch her as it should, and that it was better to trust a blade than a spell with her, if one had the choice. That she was clever, and patient, and cruel for the pleasure of it, and that if she was leading a hunt she would not be at the front of it, but somewhere behind, watching, letting the others find the quarry for her.

"And where there is one," he said, "look for three. They like to keep company. A coven, they call it."

"Oh, good," said Thane.

Nageru listened.

And somewhere in the middle of it — somewhere between the fog and the coven — he found that he had stopped hearing the words.

He did not know why. The words were good. They were exactly what was needed. They were clear and well-ordered and complete, and they were said in the elf's own voice, the dry rustling voice like pages turning that Nageru had heard lecture on vrocks and brimoraks and aurochs and the inner workings of a demon's forge. Every word of it was something Aravashnial would know, and every word of it was something Aravashnial would say.

And something was not right.

He could not have said what. He looked across the fire at the elf and could not have pointed to one thing. The hands moved as they always moved. The head tilted as it always tilted. The eyes went from one of them to the next, the way they always did when he was explaining, checking each face to see who had understood and who had not.

But Nageru had sat across a great many fires from a great many people, and he had learned, before he learned to read, to listen to more than words. To breath. To stillness. To the small motions of a body that tell you what it means before it does it.

He listened to that, now.

It was not wrong. He could not have said that it was wrong.

It was only not *his.*

Nageru sat very still, with his empty bowl in his hands, and did not move his eyes from the elf's face.

Then he moved them.

He looked at Korroc.

The dwarf was sitting on the far side of the fire with his elbows on his knees and Fiendsplitter laid across his boots, quiet for once. He had been listening to the elf the way he listened to everything Aravashnial said, with a kind of patient interested frown. And his eyes came up from the fire, and found Nageru's, and Nageru saw in them that Korroc had heard it too.

Whatever it was. He had heard it.

Korroc's frown did not change. He held Nageru's eyes for a long moment. Then, very slowly, he turned his head and looked at Caleth.

And Caleth was already looking at both of them.

He sat with his back against the cliff and his coat around him, very still. His hand was on the left side of his coat. His face was in shadow, and the firelight came and went over it, and Nageru could not read it at all — and then the fire leaped up, and for one moment he could.

Caleth looked at Nageru. At Korroc. And then, for a long moment, across the fire, at the elf who was still talking. At the face of the man he had sat on a floor beside all of one long afternoon, and had gone up a narrow stair to three times a day since, and had cast at, two paces off, with everything he had.

Caleth looked back at Nageru.

He did not say anything. Nobody said anything.

Thane was holding a knife up to the firelight, sighting along the edge. "These claws," he said. "If she gets one arm round you. Not two. One. What then?"

"Then you have a moment," said Aravashnial, "in which to make very sure she does not get the second one." He smiled slightly. "I should go for the elbow, myself. Even a hag needs an elbow."

Thane grunted, satisfied, and went back to his whetstone.

The fire burned. The wind came down off the hills and moved the grass at the edge of the shelf. Far out to the west, past the Gray Road and the dead river under it, in a fortress folded into broken hills, a bell hung in a tower that a hunt was afraid of, and a woman with wings sat beneath a window and waited for somebody to come.

And on the shelf below the fane of Irori, the elf went on explaining, and three of the four men around the fire listened to him, and did not look at each other again.
