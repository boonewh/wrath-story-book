Beauty Has Teeth
Chapter Fifteen — What Wore the Inheritor's Face

Caleth had learned to read a building the way his foster father had taught him to read a face — the bones under the paint, the intent in the composition, the small honest tells a maker leaves without meaning to. And Citadel Drezen, from the inside, had the face of a beautiful woman someone had spent seventy years teaching to lie.

It had been lovely once. He could see it under the ruin the way you can see a good drawing under a child's scribble: the vaulting had been raised by people who loved the reach of stone, the doorframes cut by hands that took pleasure in a true line. Then the demons had come, and the men who served them, and they had not simply broken the place. They had worked at it. They had spent care on its defilement the way its builders had spent care on its beauty, and that — Caleth thought, stepping over a fallen sconce shaped like an angel's cupped hands — was the part that turned his stomach. Not the destruction. The craft of it.

He put the thought away and did the other thing, the colder thing, the thing that ran under everything else in him like a second pulse.

Count.

They had folded onto the roof at first light. It was full morning now, grey and hard off the Ahari, and every corridor they cleared was a corridor the army below did not have to climb over its own dead to take. Somewhere under his boots the crusade was moving up the bridge — Irabeth's soldiers, the freed prisoners with borrowed steel, the Queen's colors climbing toward a gate that men had died in front of for three generations. Every room the four of them emptied up here was a handful of arrows that would never come down on those colors. That was the arithmetic. That was why they were four instead of two hundred, and why they moved fast, and why Caleth did not let himself stop at the angel's cupped hands and grieve.

He was already grieving. He simply did it while walking.

They found a room where the ceiling had come down entire, and two rooms empty as robbed graves, and then they turned north along the western wall, and Thane stopped at a door with his hand raised.

Caleth stopped. They all did. You learned to, with Thane — the dwarf's stillness was information.

Thane laid his ear a hand's width from the frame, and his weathered face did the small collapse it did when it read something. Voices, the flat of his hand said. Then the collapse went a shade colder, and he mouthed a word Caleth caught off his lips before he heard it in his own chest.

Bowstrings.

Caleth's arithmetic sharpened to a point. Arrow slits. A gallery along the wall, cultists at the slits, shooting down — down at the bridge, down at the colors, down at the men climbing toward the gate while these archers stood safe in the stone and picked them off at leisure. Every heartbeat he stood here reading the situation was a heartbeat one of those bowstrings sang again.

Thane's picks came into his hand. The lock gave with a small clean click, and the dwarf began, patiently, correctly, to ease the door open a finger at a time so he could see before he was seen.

Caleth did not have that kind of patience today. He had the other kind — the kind that had already finished the sum and did not want to watch the total climb.

He went through the door.

"*Stop shooting,*" he said — shouted it, the whole word, and part of him even meant it, part of him would have given a great deal for the archers to simply set down their bows and be men he did not have to kill — and he was already moving as he said it, Radiance leveled, the leaf-blade catching the grey light and throwing it, and he drove the glaive into the nearest bowman before the g of shooting had left his teeth.

Saint's mercy, he thought, pulling the blade free. I asked. I did ask.

Then there was no more room for asking.

The gallery ran long and narrow against the outer wall, slits down its length spilling thin blades of daylight, and it turned a corner and ran on north. Six of them, maybe more around the bend, faces turning, the nearest already folding around Radiance's steel. Thane came in a half-step behind Caleth, low and quick, daggers out. Nageru passed them both — the aasimar simply arrived where he had not been, bare feet silent on the bloody stone, and brought one foot across the man Caleth had already opened, and that one went down and stayed.

An archer spun and loosed at Nageru point-blank.

The arrow never landed. Nageru's hand came up and closed on the shaft in flight — plucked it out of the air the way another man catches a tossed coin — and in the same motion sent it back, and it took the archer through the shoulder and spun him into the wall.

Another dropped his bow and dragged out a scythe, swinging it at Caleth's head in a wild flat arc. Caleth read the arc before it committed — too high, too eager, a farmer's swing on a soldier's weapon — and simply was not there for it, a half-pace of Virtuous Bravo footwork that felt, from inside, less like dodging than like declining. He answered at reach. Radiance went into the scytheman's chest and out through his back, and Caleth felt the small honest resistance of a spine and then none, and drew the blade home as the man folded.

A third arrow found Nageru's arm. The aasimar took it without a sound, the way he took everything, and Caleth saw — in the corner of his attention, in the ledger he never stopped keeping — a thin thread of that hurt bleed sideways out of Nageru and be gone, drawn off across the room into Korroc, who had come in last and heavy and was already carrying what he could of everyone. The dwarf grunted, wore it, kept coming.

Thane's daggers found an archer. Nageru's foot found another, and the man dropped like a puppet with the strings gone. Two left. The one at the far end took one look at the narrow room filling with the four of them and made the only correct decision available to him: he threw down his bow and ran, north, around the bend.

Caleth let him go a heartbeat, calculating — runner, unarmed now, going to warn, but going away from the bridge, away from the slits, and every second here is a second not shooting our people — and then went after him because a warned enemy is a costed enemy, and reached the door the two fleeing men had used, and put his shoulder to it.

It did not move. Barred from the far side. He shoved again, felt the honest immovable weight of a beam in its brackets, and stepped back.

Nageru arrived beside him and hit the door with the flat of one foot, and the frame gave a sound like a struck bell and the door smashed inward off its bar.

Beyond it — nothing worth the running. An armory, wide and clean and empty, the two archers already through it and gone out some far exit into the citadel's guts. Racks of weapons. Good ones. Caleth's eye ran the room out of habit and found no threat and, a half-breath later, found something else.

Behind him, Thane's lock gave its small click, and the dwarf leaned into a side room stacked with arrows and spare bows — the gallery's magazine, the endless supply that had fed the endless shooting. Thane looked at it, looked at the slits, and his mouth went thin with something that was almost satisfaction. He kicked the nearest quiver over, and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say. The shooting had stopped. That was the sentence.

Caleth had walked to the weapon racks. His hand went to a battleaxe before his mind had finished telling him why — the pull of a well-made thing, the same pull the citadel's true bones gave him. The head was dark, near black, drinking the light instead of throwing it. He ran his thumb along the flat of it.

Korroc was suddenly at his shoulder. "Adamantine." The dwarf said it the way he said the names of the gods — with weight, with respect for the thing itself.

"And good work," Caleth said. "Look at the weld line. Whoever made this loved the metal."

Korroc grunted his agreement, and there was in it, briefly, the old ache Caleth had learned to hear in him — a smith standing in an enemy's fortress admiring an enemy's craft, and hating that he had to admire it. Caleth lifted the axe off the rack. It was too heavy for him and it was not his weapon and he had, at that moment, no earthly use for it. He slid it into the bag of holding anyway. Good steel was a resource, and Caleth did not leave resources on the wall for the enemy to find again. He would learn, before the day was out, that not every resource fits the hand that hoards it. But that lesson was still ahead of him, and Caleth Aranor spent most of his life a little ahead of himself, and so he did not feel it coming.

They checked their map — the one the cousins' parents had drawn, folded in Caleth's spellbook against his hip, the truest thing any of them carried — and moved on, and the map said the next great room was a chapel.

---

Nageru pushed the chapel doors open, and Caleth's painter's eye drank the room before his other eye, the colder one, caught up.

It had been a hall of the Inheritor. Two rows of stone benches faced a raised dais to the north, and on the dais stood a statue twelve feet of armored woman, sword and shield, the proportions grand and true and — even under the grime, even with the tapestries hanging in tatters to either side — genuinely beautiful. A larger hanging on the south wall showed the same armored woman kneeling before a man in green robes, and Caleth's eye read the composition and knew the story it told and filed it and moved on. Three smaller statues, human-sized, knights in armor with their longswords pointing down, stood in alcoves along the west. It was a place built to make a person feel small in a good way — small the way you feel small under a real sky.

There were four crusaders in the room, sitting on the benches. And a woman, standing before the dais, who turned as the doors opened, and smiled.

Beside Caleth, Korroc went very still, and his eyes grew wide, and the dwarf said the thing out loud because he was the only one who saw it whole in that first instant:

"That looks like Iomedae."

And it did.

That was the horror of it, and Caleth understood the horror before he understood anything else, because reading beauty was the one thing he had been doing since his foster father first put a brush in his hand. She had the face off the statue. The bearing off the tapestries. Everything a devout hand had spent seventy years ago carving into that twelve-foot figure of the goddess, this woman wore — the strong jaw, the level brow, the eyes that promised to see you and find you worthy. She was the icon come down off its pedestal to stand among the benches and love the men who'd waited for her.

"I am a vision from the Inheritor herself," she said, and her voice was warmth itself, the exact warmth Caleth had spent his life learning to render and never quite catching. "And I am here to bless you in your efforts."

Every trained thing in Caleth stood up at once and said no.

Not because the beauty was flawed. Because it wasn't. Because it was too whole — a real face has asymmetries, a real face has a history written in it, a real holiness announces itself the way his own goddess did, quietly, and lets the believer come to it. This came at them. This performed. This was a portrait painted from another portrait by someone who had never met the sitter, and Caleth had condemned a hundred forgeries for less.

"Yeah," he heard himself say, flat, before he had decided to speak. "Right."

Thane, beside him, shook his head once. "Nope."

Not one of them believed her. Caleth felt a cold thread of pride in that — good, they see it too — and it was the last uncomplicated thing he would feel for a while.

Nageru's quiet voice cut across the room. "If you are a vision of the goddess," the aasimar said, in the deliberate way of a man laying each word down like a stone in a wall, "why are these here?" He looked at the four crusaders on the benches. "They just — vision up, too?"

Caleth had already reached for the other sight. It came to him easy as breath, the way it always had, the sense he'd carried since a childhood spent learning to read what was under a face — and it lit her up like a lamp behind a shutter.

"She's evil," he said.

Thane nodded, reading his own version of the same truth off her the way he read a floor. "And chaos."

The woman's smile did not so much as flicker. That was almost the worst part. She had been named, in her own false temple, by two men who could see her, and she simply went on wearing the Inheritor's face, patient, certain, because she had done this before and it had worked before and there was no reason in the pit that made her to think it would not work again.

Caleth leveled Radiance.

"You are one of the great deceivers," he said, and his voice had gone to the place it went when the calculating and the sworn came together, the cold precise place his oath lived. "You are not of Iomedae. You wear her face and you shame it. And we will destroy you."

He moved.

There was a fold in the world — that small cool half-second of nowhere he hoarded like a miser and spent, now, without a flicker of regret — and his feet came down behind her, and Radiance was already rising. The blade flashed. It bit her twice, hard, holy light running white along the leaf-edge, and the false Inheritor's face finally broke its serenity and snarled.

Nageru was there in the same breath. Two fists, celestial fire waking along his knuckles, driving into her.

And Korroc — Korroc did the thing that was most himself, the thing Caleth loved him for and, that day, would have cause to bless him for. The dwarf did not go for the demon. He put his broad body between the fight and the four crusaders on the benches, and Caleth heard him send his senses out the way he did, reaching, testing — and heard him grunt with something like grief, because whatever Korroc found in those four men, it was not evil. He rooted himself, reaching down through the floor into the deep stone, and set his feet, and began to talk to them. Told them they were the good ones. Told them what she was. Tried, in the middle of a fight, with a demon two paces off, to break four charmed men gently out of a lie.

The four charmed men attacked him for it.

One came around Korroc and swung at Thane. One went the other way for Caleth. Two stayed on the dwarf. None of them landed a blow — they moved like men swimming in syrup, like men doing a thing their bodies knew and their souls were screaming against, and Caleth read that too, filed it: they don't want to. She's holding them. He did not kill the one who swung at him. He turned the blow with Radiance's haft and stepped past it and kept the demon his target, because the demon was the string, and you cut the string, not the puppet.

Then she clawed him.

He had let her get close — had put himself close, behind her, at reach, in the seam — and her hand came around faster than her broken beauty should have allowed, and her nails opened three cold lines across his forearm. Not deep. He'd taken worse and kept his arithmetic. He adjusted his grip, already deciding the next three strikes—

"We are of the same faith, my follower." Her voice, low now, only for him, and the room seemed to lean in around it. "Before you drive your glaive through me — at least allow me one embrace. And a kiss."

And the world went warm.

No, said the small true voice at the center of him. No, that is a compulsion, that is her, that is the lie reaching into your own skull and dressing itself in your own wanting, you KNOW what she is, you SAID what she is—

But the warmth was already his. That was the obscene craft of it, the thing worse than any forgery he had ever unmasked: it did not feel like being commanded. It felt like deciding. It felt like his own idea arriving fully formed and reasonable — of course, one embrace, what is the harm, she is beautiful and beauty is holy and you serve beauty, Caleth, you of all men understand that a thing can be broken and still be worth an open hand — and every rationalization she poured into him wore the shape of his own oath, his own creed, his own goddess turned inside out and used as a lever.

He watched himself lower Radiance.

He watched his own feet carry him the last half-step. From very far away, from behind a wall of glass, the true Caleth beat his fists and screamed the arithmetic — she drains, that is what she is, a kiss from that is a wound you may not survive, Korroc is busy, the count, the COUNT — and none of it reached his hands, and his hands set the glaive aside, and his arms went around her, and he leaned in, and he kissed the thing wearing the Inheritor's face.

His life went out of him like heat out of an opened door.

He felt it go. That was the cruelty piled on cruelty — the compulsion let him feel the theft, let him stand inside his own body while it was emptied, the warmth she'd poured in now running the other way and taking the real warmth with it, taking strength, taking years, the golden fire of the wardstone guttering low in him, his knees going soft, his sight silvering at the edges. And still, held in the lie, some part of him thought it was tenderness.

Then Nageru hit her.

The aasimar came in low and hard, both fists blazing, trying to break them apart the way you'd break a grip on a drowning man — and it hurt her, Caleth felt her flinch through the awful intimacy of the kiss, but she did not let go, and neither did he, because he could not, because his own arms were hers now.

And then Thane came in under all of it, low, mother's blade and father's knife thrusting up into her together, into the soft seam where the false armor met the false flesh.

She arched. She screamed — and the scream was not Iomedae's, the scream came out of a much older and much worse mouth — and her form let go of its lie all at once. The armor became a woman's shape and the woman's shape became something with bat-dark wings and skin the wrong color for anything born of a mother, and for one single unbearable heartbeat Caleth stood holding a succubus, her true face inches from his, still wet from the kiss.

He recoiled. He dropped her. His hands came off her as if she'd gone to coals.

And she was gone — banished, unmade back to whatever pit had sent her, snuffed out of the world's air the way all of them went when you finally got a blade far enough in, leaving only a smear of sulfur and the sudden ringing absence where a thing had been.

The four crusaders stopped where they stood. All at once. Weapons falling from hands that had been made to hold them, faces surfacing out of the syrup, four men blinking in a defiled chapel with no memory they'd trust of how they'd gotten there.

Caleth stood in the middle of it, swaying, wiping his mouth with the back of his hand — hard, once, twice, as if he could take the skin off with it — and did the only thing left in him that was his own. He turned it into work.

"What army do you come from?" His voice came out steadier than he had any right to. Arithmetic. Cold water on a burn. He asked, and each of the four gave a different answer, and he catalogued them without truly hearing them, and he said, "Go back out the way we came. There's a supply room — find rope. You can get down the outer wall. Go to the rear of the fighting and report to command." Mercy in the shape of instructions, because instructions were a thing his shaking hands could still deliver.

Then he turned to Korroc, and something in him gave, just slightly, just at the seam.

"I don't feel so great," he said.

Korroc did not say anything. Caleth would remember that. The dwarf did not ask, did not look at him with the question that would have cost more than the wound — what did she do to you, what was that, are you a fool, half-elf — none of it. Korroc simply stepped up and laid a hand on him and cast, and a wash of golden warmth rolled through Caleth where the cold had been, filling the emptied room the demon had drunk dry, and his sight cleared and his knees came back under him and the wardstone's fire, low and guttering, caught and rose.

"Thank you," Caleth said, and breathed, and stood.

No one spoke of the kiss.

Not Nageru, who had tried to break it and could not. Not Thane, who had ended it with two blades. Not Korroc, who had healed the harm of it and looked, once, at Caleth's mouth and then away. The four of them stood in the ruined chapel of a goddess not one of them served, and the thing that had happened sat among them unspoken, and by unspeaking it they agreed, all four, that it would keep.

Caleth was grateful, and ashamed of his gratitude, and filed even the shame.

Because here was the sum he could not stop working, the sum that would ride under his ribs down every corridor after: he had known. He had looked at her with the eye he trusted above every other thing about himself, the eye that had never once failed him, and he had named her — she's evil — and he had leveled his blade and called her deceiver, and none of it had mattered. She had reached past all of it. Past the training, past the oath, past the cold clean arithmetic he had built his whole self on, and she had taken his own hands and used them against him, and dressed the theft up in the very things he held holy.

A man who plans for every future, he thought, wiping his mouth one more useless time, and she made me want the one that emptied me.

Knowing had not been enough.

That was new. He turned it over the way he turned over everything, looking for the handle, and could not find one, and — because there was a war on and the army was climbing and the arithmetic did not stop for a man's wounded pride — he put it away unsolved, in the place where he kept the things too heavy to carry openly, and made himself useful again.

---

Nageru found the seam.

The aasimar had gone quiet along the western wall while the rest of them recovered, running one hand slow across the stone the way he ran it across everything, reading the world by touch as much as sight. "Thane," he said, and nothing more.

Thane went to him. Nageru's fingers stopped. "Look. A seam."

Thane bent, and studied, and did the grammar his mother had left him — listen, look, touch — and after a moment his hand found a place in the stone and pressed, and a door that had not been a door swung inward.

Beyond it, a room with two more statues, armored women with their swords raised, and the floor littered with smashed and empty chests. They stood in it a moment, all of them thinking the same puzzled thing — why guard an empty room — and then Thane and Korroc saw it together, in the same breath, the way the two cousins so often arrived at a thing at once.

"Another door," Thane said, and Korroc nodded, and Thane crossed to it and pressed the stone and opened the second hidden room.

And here, at last, was a treasury.

A metal rod stood raised in the hands of a northern statue, and from it hung a banner — a sword before a sunburst, worked in thread that had once been fine. South of the statue, a gleaming alabaster altar. Four more statues flanked the room in real suits of metal armor, and a tattered red-and-gold hanging covered the south wall. Caleth's eye went to the banner first, because the banner was the thing they had climbed a fortress to find — and his other sight went with it, reaching, testing — and came back flat.

"That armor is the only magic in the room," he said, nodding at one of the four flanking suits. "The banner is ordinary."

Korroc's shoulders dropped a fraction. "The captain we sent out told us as much," the dwarf said. "The one we're after was taken to the new rooms. Below." He said below the way a dwarf says a thing that troubles him — rooms under the floor of a place his own people had dreamed and drawn, rooms on no map, cut by no hand he'd trust. "This is a decoy. Or just what they left when they took the real one down."

So they had come all this way to the wrong banner. Caleth filed it — the true one is below, in the dark that isn't ours, keep that; the enemy hung a false sword-and-sun here the way the demon wore a false face, this whole citadel lies with craft — and moved to the one thing in the room that had answered his sight.

The magic suit stood on a statue near the front. He came close, and Korroc and Thane came with him, and Korroc leaned in and squinted and made a low sound in his chest.

"Is..." The dwarf's soot-stained fingers hovered over the breastplate. "Is that mithral?"

It was. Gold-plated over mithral, the two metals married by someone who had known exactly what they were doing, and worked into the breastplate — etched deep and clean and reverent — the sunburst sigil of Iomedae. Caleth's iconographer's eye read the work and found no forgery in it at all. This was the true thing. Whoever had made this had loved both the metal and the goddess, and had made a holy object, and it had ended up here, in a demon's treasury, a candle in a sewer.

He would have said so. He was already composing the observation.

Then the statue at the front of the room — the one holding the banner — smiled.

---

It smiled from its chest, which was the sort of detail Caleth's mind refused for a quarter-second before accepting, a mouth splitting open in the stone breast of an armored woman, full of teeth that were not carved. And in a flash a tongue shot out of it — long, wet, obscene against all that dry stone — and struck Nageru, who had drifted forward to look at the banner, and dug in, and held.

Across the room, Caleth felt Korroc flinch — the dwarf spun, feeling the bite land on Nageru as if it had landed on himself, the bond drawing the hurt across the open air.

Caleth's hand was already up, the other sight already spent, and it told him what the shape was before his mouth caught up.

"*Mimic!*" He threw two rays as he shouted it — fire, twin lances of it hammering into the false statue. "Don't touch it! It's a mimic!"

Nageru tried to twist free of the tongue. It held him like a hooked fish. And then the thing sprouted a limb from its chest beside the grinning mouth — a stone-colored pseudopod, thick as a man's thigh — and slammed it into the aasimar's ribs.

Thane's shortbow sang. The arrow skipped off the mimic's flank and clattered away; the creature's hide was as hard as the stone it wore.

And then the altar opened a mouth.

The gleaming alabaster altar, the beautiful holy-looking altar, the thing Caleth had catalogued and dismissed a moment ago — its top split into teeth, and it heaved itself up off the floor on limbs it had not had, and came at the group. Came, specifically, at Caleth.

He had a heartbeat to appreciate the joke the room was playing on him — you read the banner and the armor and the two false statues and you never once thought to read the altar you were standing three feet from — and then it was on him, slamming into his chest and attaching, its whole grasping mass fastening to him like a lamprey, cold and wrong and immensely heavy, dragging him half off his feet.

So this is how it goes, said the cold voice, unhurried even now. You have perhaps a breath before it does to you what the other statue is doing to Nageru. Fire is what you reached for and fire is slow. You need it off you now, not soon.

Across the room, Korroc took Caleth's advice a moment too late for himself and a moment right for Nageru — the dwarf's own two rays of fire leapt from his fingers into the mimic gripping the aasimar, buying him room.

And Caleth, pinned, half-lifted, a monster's teeth working against his chest, did the thing he had been quietly learning he could do — reached down, not into a spellbook or a memorized shape but into the well the wardstone's golden fire had left burning in him ever since the fragment shattered in his hands. He took hold of a spell of lightning and he took hold of the golden fire both, and he braided the one into the other, poured the borrowed strength of a broken god-stone into a working never meant to hold that much — and let it go.

Electricity roared out of him in a single white sheet.

The mimic on his chest convulsed. Every fiber of the false altar seized at once, the teeth spasming open, and it slid off him — poured off him, really, its grip failing all along its length — and slumped to the floor a smoking heap of something that had never been stone at all.

Caleth staggered back, breathing hard, the golden well in him a fraction lower and every nerve in his arm singing.

Nageru finished the other one. The aasimar's fists blazed up with holy light and he brought them both down together on the mimic that held him — and his knuckles stuck for half a heartbeat to its grasping hide, and then the sheer force of the blow tore him loose and drove the creature down, oozing, off the statue and onto the floor, dead.

Silence, but for four sets of lungs working.

Caleth looked at the ruin of the altar-thing at his feet, and then at the still-grinning wreck of the statue-thing across the room, and then — because it was either that or think about the other mouth he'd let get close to him today — he let one corner of his mouth go up.

"I catalogued the whole room," he said, to no one. "I catalogued the banner."

"You catalogued the wrong furniture," Thane said, wiping an arrow he hadn't even bloodied, because his hands wanted the ritual.

---

They took the armor off the fourth statue — the true one, the gold-and-mithral suit that had answered Caleth's sight and no one's teeth — and Korroc began, without discussion, to take off his own.

Caleth watched him do it, and something in his chest that had been braced all morning eased half a notch, because there was no better sight in a bad place than Korroc Stonevein deciding a thing was right.

"Are you sure about that?" Thane said, watching his cousin work the buckles of the dented plate he'd worn since Kenabres. "Could be cursed. It's sitting in a demon's trophy room, cousin. Everything in here's a mouth."

Korroc shook his head. He lifted the mithral breastplate, and turned it in the grey light, and his soot-dark face did a thing Caleth had learned to read on him over these long weeks — the look of a smith meeting a piece of work that spoke to him, that told him something in a language under words.

"It feels right," Korroc said. Simple as that. Then, slower, frowning at the sunburst etched deep in the metal: "There's something about this armor. Something I feel like I should know. And I can't — I can't quite get it."

Thane's dry voice: "Sounds cursed to me."

Korroc chuckled, low, and shook his head at his cousin, and went on putting it on. "I guess we'll see."

Caleth said nothing, and watched, and did his own quiet arithmetic on the thing — and came up, for once, with a sum he did not share.

Because he knew that feeling. He knew it better than any of them, and could never say so. The ache of a thing you knew-without-knowing. A mark on your skin you'd carried since before memory that meant something your bones understood and your mind could not reach. A dragon's eyes finding you in a crowd and a certainty settling in you that you had been recognized — recognized as what, by what right, toward what end, you could not say, only that the knowing sat in you real as a stone in a shoe. Caleth lived every day with a thing he felt he ought to understand and did not. He was, in a real sense, an expert in that particular ache.

So when Korroc said I feel like I should know this, Caleth believed him completely, and the belief came with a small cold prickle of kinship he would have found difficult to explain. He's carrying one too. His shaped like armor. Mine shaped like a scale I've never spent. He watched the gold-and-mithral suit settle onto the dwarf, piece by piece — and it did settle, Caleth saw it, saw the way each plate found its place as though the space had been waiting for it, as though the armor had been forged around a body it had never met and was only now, seventy years later, coming home to the right one. Every buckle Korroc closed sat true. The breastplate that had hung loose on a statue's shoulders fit the dwarf's barrel chest as if a master had measured him for it.

Forged precisely for him, Caleth thought, and did not say. Or for someone shaped exactly like him.

He filed it — deep, in the place where the heavy things went, next to a scale that whispered get them out — and said only, aloud, in the dry register the dwarves liked:

"If it is cursed, at least you'll match the décor."

Korroc laughed, a real one, and stamped once to settle the greaves, and looked for a moment less like a man in an enemy's fortress and more like a man who had found, in the last place he'd have looked, something of his own.

---

The map said the court was next, and the court was a wound.

It had been the citadel's heart — Caleth could read that off the bones of it even now, five decorative columns still holding a thirty-foot ceiling, the scale of it built to make you stop and look up. But the walls. The walls were murals, and the murals were the reason his eye kept trying to slide off them and his oath kept dragging it back to look. Angels and templars, rendered with the same loving craft as the true work everywhere else in this place, engaged in every foulness a diseased hand could imagine — coupling with beasts, feasting on the slain, holiness drawn defiling itself in careful, patient, skilled strokes.

Every face deserves to be seen, his creed said. Caleth made himself see. It was the discipline of it — a paladin of Shelyn did not get to look away from the ugly thing, because looking away was how the ugly thing won, was how it convinced you it was the only truth. He looked, and he grieved, and he moved. Collapsed stone barred the way north entirely; only rubble and the five columns and one long empty hall glimpsed through a gap where Thane put his eye.

Thane moved to a pile of rubble at the north and peered into it.

The room screamed.

It came out of the rubble-gap like a physical thing, a sound that got into the base of Caleth's skull and shook, and the world went white and loud and far away, his body locking rigid, Radiance suddenly a great weight in hands that would not answer. He knew what it was even inside the stun — he'd met it before, at the Ahari, in the Vescavor dark — and being able to name the thing that was killing him did not let him move a single muscle against it.

Vrock, he thought, from the white place. And I am standing here like a struck bell while it—

"*Vrock!*" Korroc bellowed it, and the dwarf was already moving.

It came out of the rubble fast — head like a vulture, reeking, its talons and claws finding Thane before anyone could close the gap, and Caleth heard rather than saw the wet sounds of it, blood on stone, his cousin-in-arms taking the demon's whole first fury while Caleth stood locked and useless and screaming behind glass for the second time that day.

He hated it. Even inside the stun, he hated it — the uselessness, the specific horror of a man who lives by acting reduced to a spectator in his own skin. He'd tasted it when the dragon's kin bit him down into dark at the bridge, and he'd tasted it an hour ago in a false chapel with a demon's mouth on his, and now here it was a third time, and Caleth Aranor, who had built a whole self on never being caught unready, was beginning to understand that the war had a lesson for him and was going to keep teaching it until he learned.

Nageru was across the court and on the vrock in a blink — and missed, the demon twisting aside from the aasimar's fist. Korroc reached Thane, and Caleth felt, dimly, through the fog, the golden thing happen: some of Thane's wounds bleeding sideways into the dwarf, Korroc grunting as he took them and then pressing his hand to his cousin and giving back health, the bond running both ways at once.

Caleth's own sense came back like feeling returning to a slept-on arm — all at once and painful. He looked down. Radiance was on the floor; he'd dropped it in the stun. He bent and took it up, and the leaf-blade woke warm in his hand, and he moved — behind Nageru, to the reach, to the seam, the arithmetic snapping back online. Two demons in a moment. Thane bleeding but standing. Korroc carrying. Nageru committed. Where's the gap.

The demon struck Thane again, claws and a bite. Nageru pivoted his whole body into a fist and hit it three times, hard, and the vrock screamed — and did not fall.

And then there was a brimorak in the room.

Caleth's stomach dropped. He knew these now too, knew them from the towers — goat-headed, hooves of fire, and immune to the two easiest tools in his kit. Smoke billowed off it at once, boiling up to fill the court, and the arithmetic re-cast itself in an instant: fire won't touch it, lightning won't touch it, cold is the key, and I burned the well low killing the altar — how much cold do I have left, and can I reach it before it breathes.

Korroc took another share of Thane's hurt and pressed his hand to him and healed almost all the rest of it, and Caleth heard his low growl, the one the dwarf got when a fight stopped being a fight and became simple stubborn work:

"I can do this all day, bitch."

Caleth almost smiled. He leveled Radiance at the brimorak instead — because Radiance was neither fire nor lightning, Radiance was holy steel and a demon minded holy steel whatever its hide shrugged off — and thrust twice, and both bit, white light flashing along the blade and the goat-thing shrieking.

The vrock went at Thane once more, but the dwarf was whole again now, and quick, and it caught him only glancingly. Nageru's fists came down on it in the same instant — and the vrock was gone, banished mid-shriek, unmade.

The brimorak filled its lungs and breathed a line of boiling blood across the court. Thane dropped under it clean. Caleth did not — it caught him full in the chest, a gout of scalding red that should, by every rule the towers had taught them, have dropped him screaming.

It barely marked him.

Fire had never found much purchase on Caleth, and never would. Some curtain his body drew against heat without asking him first, bred into the same part of him that dealt in flame — so that the burning thing spent itself against him and found little to catch, the way a hot coal set on wet stone only hisses. And there was the quieter thing beneath even that: the surplus his own destructive magic left wound about him after a hard casting, a shimmer of borrowed hardness that drank the first true bite of a blow before his skin ever felt it. The boiling blood broke over both and guttered to nothing. He grunted — more at the insult of it than any hurt — and did not so much as break his read of the fight.

Then he stepped in and put Radiance through the brimorak, holy fire running the length of the blade, and the goat-headed thing came apart and was gone.

Quiet. Cooling stone. Four sets of lungs, again.

Caleth looked at the murals one more time — made himself — and then away, north, to the next dark.

---

The next room was almost an insult.

Thane heard voices, picked the lock, and they went in on half-orcs — and it was over nearly before it started. Thane's daggers, and one fewer. Korroc's hammer, another. Caleth crossed to the far one and ended it with Radiance before it had fully turned. Nageru dropped the next with a single fist. Thane put a blade in the last, the one Korroc had already opened, and it fell.

Korroc stood in the sudden silence and frowned at the ease of it, and shook his head, and Caleth understood the frown. After a false goddess and two mouths made of stone and a vrock and a fire-hooved demon, four half-orcs who died in the time it took to draw breath felt less like mercy than like a joke told at the wrong funeral. You did not trust a fight this easy. You wondered what it was buying time for.

Caleth wondered. He filed the wondering. And they moved.

---

The last room was the other chapel.

Caleth knew what it was the moment the doors opened, and he knew what it would do to Korroc, and he watched the dwarf's face instead of the room, because the room he could read in a glance and his friend he loved.

A huge statue of a squat dwarf with a massive hammer and shield stood at the forefront — Torag, unmistakable, the Father, the great Smith of the dwarven gods. Twin tapestries of forges flanked him. Rows of benches. Three more dwarven sentinels in alcoves along the east. And across the back wall, a larger hanging that had once, before it was slashed to rags, commemorated the very building of this citadel — the raising of Drezen, its stand against the demons, dwarven hands and human hands together at the work.

Korroc's history, Caleth thought. Literally his. His father's dream and his father's stone and his father's god, all in one room.

And every surface of it had been defiled. Torn, smashed, scratched, fouled — and the three eastern statues worst of all, their whole surfaces etched over with demonic runes, so densely that the honest dwarven stonework beneath could barely be seen. Someone had spent a long time here. Someone had enjoyed this.

Korroc growled low in his chest — a sound Caleth had heard him make exactly once before, in the desecrated chapel of Shelyn on the cliff, the sound of a man of the Father standing in a fouled workshop. The dwarf moved into the room, and the rest of them with him.

One of the three etched statues stood up.

It stepped down out of its alcove, ran the demonic light along its rune-carved surface, and brought its hammer down hard — at Thane, who was not where it swung, having read the weight-shift and rolled clear. A second statue stood, stepped toward Caleth, then turned mid-stride and swung at Thane instead. Thane cursed in Dwarven and went backward out of reach.

Caleth read the pattern in two heartbeats. They're not choosing at random.

He reached into the bag of holding and pulled out the adamantine battleaxe — the good black axe, the resource he'd hoarded off the armory wall, and here, surely, was its moment: a heavy weapon against heavy stone. He set his feet and swung.

And the axe betrayed him utterly.

It was too heavy and it was the wrong length and it was balanced for a grip his hands had never trained, and the swing that should have bitten stone went wide and hauled him half off his center, and Caleth stumbled, actually stumbled, in the middle of a fight, glaring at the weapon in his hands as if it had lied to him — which, in a sense, it had. A resource that doesn't fit the hand is not a resource, said the cold voice, filing the lesson dryly. It's a wasted swing and a lost half-second — paid, of all the places to spend it, here.

He was not too proud to correct an error the instant he saw it. He turned and tossed the axe underhand to Nageru — "*Yours*" — and swept Radiance back off his shoulder into his own true grip, where the world went quiet and precise again.

Nageru caught the axe out of the air. And then did a thing Caleth had almost never seen: the aasimar who fought with nothing but his hands took up a weapon, and used it, and used it well. He turned into the first swing and the adamantine head bit deep into rune-carved stone and chips flew. In Nageru's hands the axe was suddenly exactly what it had failed to be in Caleth's.

A third statue stood, walked completely past Caleth without a glance, and slammed into Thane.

"They're going after dwarves," Caleth said, aloud, to the room — because it was information and information was to be shared. The runed statues wanted the two Stoneveins. They'd walk past a half-elf and an aasimar to reach a son of the mountain. Whatever hate had been etched into them had been etched specific.

At the front of the chapel, Korroc had been standing before the great statue of Torag, his hand half-lifted toward it — and Caleth saw the moment the dwarf felt Thane's wounds arrive in his own chest through the bond, saw him turn from his god's image to his cousin's need without a flicker of hesitation, the way he always did. Korroc reached down into the deep stone for its strength and brought the adamantine warhammer across the nearest statue's back, and stone chips flew, and the runed thing turned on him and slammed him twice — and Caleth saw the dwarf feel it, really feel it, a grimace cracking his soot-dark face. The evil worked into these statues bit at Korroc in a way it didn't bite the rest of them. It knew what he was. It hated him for it.

Caleth moved to flank. That was his work — not to be the wall, never the wall, but to be the second angle that turned a slugging match into an execution. He put himself where a statue would have to choose between him and Thane, and Thane read the geometry in an instant and tumbled through the reaching stone arms to fetch up beside Korroc, so that now the three had each of the statues flanked, each caught between two threats.

He thrust Radiance at his statue twice. The first bit; the second skated off the stone and did nothing, the blade finding an angle the rune-work turned aside, and Caleth adjusted his footing and did not waste breath on frustration. Beside him, Korroc healed himself with a quick wash of grace and struck the same statue twice more, patient as a man at a forge, chip after chip. Thane's daggers went in four times fast, finding the mortar-seams the way they found the seams in armor, and stone cracked and crumbled under them. Nageru pounded steadily with the adamantine axe, methodical, each blow a prayer with a bad word in it.

The statue Caleth had been working continued to ignore him entirely and swung at Thane, and missed, Thane flowing under it — and Caleth saw his opening the way he lived his whole life seeing openings, a half-second ahead of everyone else in the room. He stepped and thrust, and this time Radiance flashed white and went home, and the statue crumbled around the blade, collapsing into rubble that had, a moment before, been trying to kill a dwarf for the crime of being one.

Thane's statue tried to counter and missed a final time, and Thane's daggers answered, and it came apart. He turned in the same motion and drove a blade into the one Korroc had been grinding down, and that one crumbled too, and then it was only rubble and dust and the four of them breathing in the wreck of Torag's house.

Korroc finished his healing. And then, for a moment, the dwarf just stood in the ruin, looking at the great statue of the Father at the front — untouched by the fight, defiled by long patient hands, the runes crawling over the smaller sentinels' shattered remains.

Caleth watched him, and made a decision, and did the thing he was for.

He began to cast.

Nothing grand. He had no scroll of consecration here, no great working to reclaim the whole hall as he'd reclaimed Shelyn's chapel on the cliff. What he had were cantrips — the smallest magics, the first things any wizard learns, beneath the notice of anyone counting a mage's real power. He conjured water. Clean water, out of the air, a cupped rush of it, and he sent it across the nearest fouled surface, and where it ran the filth ran with it, and honest dwarven stone showed through underneath.

He did it again. And again. Small workings, one after another, washing what he could reach — the runes streaking and thinning, the muck of seventy years sluicing off the benches and the base of the great statue, the forge-tapestries' slashed threads at least made clean if they could not be made whole.

He was aware of the others watching. He did not stop. This was not for them.

Because here was the thing he had learned in a false chapel an hour ago, and was only now finding the handle of: a demon had worn a holy face and used his own creed against him, had taken the beautiful and made it a lie, had proven to him that knowing a thing's evil was not proof against it. And the answer to that — the only answer he had, the answer his goddess would have given — was not to stop trusting beauty. It was not to grow so cautious that he looked away from every holy face for fear of the mask. It was this. To find the true holy thing under the fouling, the broken thing that was still the holy thing, and to kneel to it, and to clean it, and to make it seen again. The demon had shown him a whole false face. Here was a true face defaced, and Caleth Aranor would spend his smallest magics restoring it, because that was the difference between them, and it was the whole difference.

He cleaned the base of Torag's statue until the Father's carved feet stood clear of filth.

Then he stood, and his water ran out, and he looked at Korroc.

The dwarf's eyes were bright. He said nothing — no more than he'd said over the kiss, no more than he'd said over the armor. But he crossed the ruined chapel, and he put one gauntleted hand on Caleth's shoulder, the gold-and-mithral gauntlet that had come home to him in a demon's treasury, and he squeezed once, hard, and let go. Two paladins of two gods in the wreck of one's house, and the whole conversation passing between them in a hand on a shoulder.

Then Korroc looked around the cleaned-as-much-as-it-could-be chapel, and out, and back, and the moment closed the way all their moments closed these days.

"The army's outside," the dwarf said. "Fighting. Right now."

"I know," Caleth said.

And they knew, all four of them, what that meant. It meant they did not get to stay and finish this. It meant there was no time to do it properly, no time to consecrate, no time to grieve the way the place deserved to be grieved. It meant a war was climbing a bridge below them and needed the four of them moving, always moving, toward the way down — toward the stair the Stoneveins' map did not show, into the new rooms no dwarf had ever drawn, where a true banner hung in a true dark, where a monster's ill-used brother might be a friend or a snare, where a spared captain's warnings waited to be proved, and where somewhere in the halls a second succubus still walked, patient, uncaught, waiting on a check-in that would never come.

Caleth wiped his mouth one last time — a small unconscious thing, and he caught himself doing it, and stopped.

He looked once more at the great statue of the Father, standing clean-footed now in his fouled house, and he thought about faces. About the false one that had worn a goddess and taken his hands. About the true one under seventy years of filth that a cup of conjured water could still, a little, restore. About the mask and the thing behind it, and which of them a man chose to believe, and what it cost either way.

Knowing wasn't enough, the wound in him said again. It would say it for a long time.

No, Caleth answered it, and turned toward the stair. But it's a place to start. And it's more than she had.

He settled Radiance across his back, and touched the spellbook at his hip, where the Stoneveins' map — the citadel their four parents had dreamed and drawn — lay folded against secrets of his own that he would carry to his grave, and he followed the two dwarves and the aasimar toward the dark that waited.

The war did not wait. It never had. That was the war.
