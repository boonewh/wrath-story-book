# Every Name But Two

*Chapter Seventeen — The Ledger and the Butterflies*

---

There was a desk against the wall, and the desk had a book on it, and the book had Droskar's mark on the cover, and Thane had flint and steel in his hands before he had finished deciding to.

It was a good desk. That was the first thing he'd noticed about it, which told him something about himself he didn't much want to know — that he could stand in a room with Staunton Vhane cooling on the flagstones and still catalogue the joinery. Dwarven work. Mortise and tenon, no nails, the grain run so the top would not cup in a hundred years of damp. Somebody's whole careful month, that desk. Somebody who had cared.

Whose desk it was, he couldn't have said. Whose book it was took him no time at all.

The mark on the cover was the same mark that was on the shield lying on the flagstones twenty feet away, next to the dwarf on his knees — almost Torag's hammer and anvil and not it, the same bones with something gone wrong in the making, the anvil crowded, the whole thing bent toward *toil* where the Father's bent toward *craft.* Thane had watched Korroc's face when he'd found that shield. He had a fair idea what it had cost his cousin to ask the question out loud.

So: a book of the Dark Smith, left open on a desk like a plate at a table, by a dwarf who had been standing right here when they came through that door.

He knelt and set the book on the bare stone floor — because he was not a fool, and because you do not start a fire on good wood — and struck the flint.

Behind him, Joran Vhane made a sound.

Thane had been half-listening to that dwarf since the fighting stopped. It was habit. A man on his knees in the middle of a room where his brother has just died is a man you keep in the corner of your eye, and Joran had knelt there through all of it, hands empty, hammer on the stone beside him, staring at Korroc with an expression Thane could not get the bottom of. Not fear. Not hate. Something closer to the way men looked at something they had heard about all their lives and never seen.

Now Joran was looking at the book.

"Wait, Thane."

Korroc's voice. Thane looked over, frowning, the steel still in his fist.

"It's a Droskar text," he said.

"I know." His cousin had not raised his voice at all. "But we're talking to Joran. Not trying to upset him."

*Talking.* Thane put the flint down slowly. Not because he agreed. Because there was no argument he could make in front of Joran Vhane that he would want Joran Vhane to hear.

Joran stood up.

He did it carefully — an old dwarf's care, all knees — and he came toward the book, and Thane was on his feet with mother's blade out and his body between the dwarf and the book before the second step landed.

"Hey." Caleth, from across the room, mild as a man commenting on the weather. "What are you doing?"

Joran stopped. He did not look at the dagger. He looked past it, at the book on the floor, the way a man looks at a coat he has left somewhere he cannot go back to.

"Please let me have the book."

"Why?"

"It's mine."

Caleth had come up on Thane's left, and Thane felt rather than saw the half-elf's attention go over the thing and turn it inside out in the space of a breath.

"The book is mundane," Caleth said. "No magic in it at all."

Which was not the same as *harmless*, and Thane noted that Caleth had not said *harmless*, and filed it.

Joran Vhane's eyes came up and found Thane's, and he asked the question flat, the way a man asks the price of something he has already decided he cannot afford.

"What are you going to do with me, if you aren't going to kill me?"

Thane looked at him.

He was aware, distantly, of the shape of his own mood — that he had asked for Staunton Vhane alive not two minutes ago and had been refused by his own blood, and that the refusal was still sitting in him unswallowed, and that every hard thing he said in this room for the next hour was going to have that in it whether he meant it or not. Knowing did not help. It rarely did.

"I didn't want your brother dead," he said. "Both of you have a lot to answer for."

And then, because the man had asked, he told him.

He told the story plain and short, the way his mother told things. Of two dwarves of the Stonevein line, brothers, Thorek and Borin. The Fourth Crusade. A city they had helped dream on paper twenty years before either of their sons was born — blueprints, drawn and signed and sent to a Queen, a citadel meant to stand at the edge of the wound in the world and *hold.* And then a night when the Sword of Valor went out of Drezen in a traitor's hands and the walls came apart, and the two of them died on this ground within a season of each other, and one of them was never found at all.

"They were in your brother's company," Thane said. "Or near enough that it makes no difference."

Joran Vhane shrugged.

Not cruelly. That was the worst of it — there was nothing in the shrug at all, no defiance, no dismissal. It was the shrug of a man being asked about a road he had never walked.

"I don't know who was in my brother's company."

And that was the first door of the day that Thane put his shoulder to and found nothing behind.

He stood with that for a moment. Seventy years, a fortress, a war, two dead fathers, and the one living man in the world who had stood at Staunton Vhane's elbow for all of it did not know their names.

"Then why are you here?"

"I listened to my brother." Joran said it to the floor. "He talked me into coming here. To work the forge."

"What do you make?"

"I don't make." Something crossed the old dwarf's face — the first thing that had. "I change."

Thane frowned so deeply he felt it in his jaw.

"I use the forges to change things," Joran said. "At the instruction of my brother. Or of Aponavicius."

Caleth stepped up. "What kind of things do you change?"

"Whatever they bring me. Weapons. Armor."

"How do you change them?"

"I can make a holy weapon unholy." A pause. "For example."

"So you corrupt them."

"I change them."

Thane watched the half-elf take that answer, and hold it, and set it down without arguing, and he understood exactly why. You do not argue with a man's word for the thing he does. You *note* it. The word a man chooses is the whole confession.

"Who is Aponavicius?" Caleth asked.

"That's the general to the south," Korroc said. "The one the Queen told us about. The Marilith."

Caleth nodded slowly, and his eyes had gone somewhere else and come back. "What will we find when we go to the rooms below?"

Joran thought about it. Genuinely thought — Thane could see the calculation, and it was not the calculation of a man deciding how much to give. It was a man trying to remember.

"That banner is down there."

"Why did they move it?"

"Aponavicius likes trophies." He said it without inflection. "Things she can show off and brag about." He paused. "The forge is also down there. And the cells."

Thane's turn. "Who's down there that's going to give us trouble?"

"Eustoyriax is the keeper of the banner."

"Tell me about Eustoyriax."

"I can't." And there was the first flinch of shame in it, small. "I've never met him. I've never been past the forge. There are many rooms down there I've never been in."

"Anything else we need to know?"

"Nul." Joran's mouth thinned. "He'll be down there before Eustoyriax. He's a vampire."

Thane nodded once and put the name where he put names.

"Anything else?"

Joran Vhane shook his head, and then he looked past all of them at the thing on the floor that had been his brother, and asked the only question he had actually come to ask.

"Can I at least take care of my brother's body?"

"I see nothing wrong with that," Caleth said.

"I do." Thane heard how it came out and did not soften it. "I might still want to ask Staunton questions."

Which was a bad answer, and a true one, and everyone in the room knew both.

Joran moved toward the book. Thane stepped in the way again.

"Just give him the book, Thane."

His cousin. Again.

Thane did not turn around. "That'll bite us in the ass."

"Yes it will."

That was not Korroc.

Nageru had come out of a side room with a book of his own — a great heavy thing open across both forearms, black leather gone to cracks, brass at the corners tarnished green — and he said it without heat, without looking up, in the tone of a man agreeing with a remark about rain. Then he kept reading.

Thane stood there a moment longer, because the shape of the room had changed and he wanted to be sure of it before he moved.

Then he stepped aside.

Joran Vhane slipped past him, picked up the Droskar text, carried it to his brother's body, and knelt.

And began, in a low cracked voice, to give the traitor of Drezen his last rites.

---

They gathered around the other book instead. All four of them, in a dead man's map room, while twenty feet away a priest of the Dark Smith prayed over a corpse.

*The Journal of Staunton Vhane.* No title page said so. It didn't need one.

The early pages were beautiful. Thane hated that immediately. A precise dwarven hand, letters standing like fence posts, each line the same distance off the one above it — the writing of a young dwarf who had been taught properly and was proud of it. Later the hand went cramped and struck-through and mean, and there were pages missing, torn out close to the spine so the stubs stood up like bad teeth.

*I dreamed of Jormurdun again.*

Thane's chest did something ugly and complicated.

Because he knew that sentence. He had grown up inside that sentence. It was not the same lost city — Thorek Stonevein had dreamed of Khar-Zadûn, the sky-hold, the towers that pierced cloud, the wind singing in runic channels — but it was the same *sentence*, the identical dwarven ache, another hold swallowed by the mountains and remembered only because dwarves are too stubborn to let a name go. Staunton Vhane's father had called it foolishness. Thorek had never called it anything but home.

Two dwarves. Two lost cities. Two roads north to a crusade that needed soldiers.

*I could not spend my life beneath the same mountain, hammering the same iron, waiting for the stone to close over me.*

"Saint's mercy," Caleth murmured, somewhere above his shoulder. "He was a person."

"He was always a person," Nageru said. "That is what makes it a betrayal, and not weather."

They read.

They read past the years of searching, the ruins and the dead ends and the maps copied from older maps copied from the memories of dead dwarves. They read the moment the crusade caught him and held him, and Thane thought: *that could have been my father's page.* Then they read the next one, and it stopped being anything like his father's page at all.

*I know what they say. Some traitor opened a gate. Some officer failed his duty. Some nameless coward doomed Drezen.*

*Let the scholars argue.*

*It was me.*

*I took the Sword of Valor from Drezen. I carried it from the city because I believed I could use it against our enemies.*

Korroc read it aloud and his voice went to gravel in the middle of it and he finished anyway.

*Minagho knew exactly what to say. By the time I understood what I had done, Drezen's protection was gone. The demons came. The walls broke. The streets filled with blood. Thousands died.*

*For years I told myself Minagho deceived me. That is true. It is also a coward's answer. She gave me the opportunity. I made the choice.*

Nobody said anything for a while.

Thane found he had to work at his own breathing. Seventy years the crusade had argued about how Drezen fell. Seventy years of scholars and inquisitors and old men in Kenabres taverns, and the answer had been sitting in this fortress the whole time in the traitor's own hand, and the answer was *he did it and he knew.*

It was the most honest thing Thane had ever read.

He hated it more than the lies would have deserved. A lie you can break. This was a man who had looked straight at the worst thing he had ever done and written it down correctly, and then gone on being that man for seventy years anyway. There was no door in that. Thane turned it over and over looking for the hinge and there was no hinge. Just a wall, built plumb and true, by someone who knew exactly what he was building.

Then Caleth turned the page, and they came to the list.

---

It ran for pages.

*Blade of Saint Edran — Iomedae — COMPLETE.*

*Shield of the Dawn Vigil — Sarenrae — COMPLETE.*

*Torag — forged breastplate — markings removed — COMPLETE.*

*Longsword, crusader officer — COMPLETE.*

*Warhammer, dwarven — COMPLETE.*

*Three Eagle Watch shields — COMPLETE.*

Thane stopped reading.

He was aware of Caleth's hand hovering to turn the page, and of himself not moving, and of the small stupid fact that he had put one finger on the parchment as though the line might get away from him.

Three Eagle Watch shields.

Eagle Watch. Three months before the world ended he had taken a contract from two grim women whose names he was never given, and he had worked the gutters and the warehouses of Kenabres for them, listening for the sound the Templars made when they thought no one was listening, and he had reported through a sigil-system to a courier he had never met. He had thought of it, at the time, as work. Honest coin for honest watching.

Three of their shields had come to this room, and gone down through that forge, and come back out as something that would make the man who carried it weep.

And two lines above: *Torag — forged breastplate — markings removed.*

Markings removed.

Korroc had gone very still beside him. Thane did not look at his cousin and his cousin did not look at him, and the two of them stood there over a ledger in a dead man's handwriting with the same thought standing between them, unspoken, because to speak it would be to ask a question neither of them could ever have answered.

Whose breastplate.

There was no way to know. There would never be a way to know. Torag's own were not rare in this war; a smith's god collects smiths, and the crusade had eaten dwarves by the hundred for seventy years, and *markings removed* was a line Staunton had not thought worth six more words on. He had written down the god and the item and the fact that he had filed a dead dwarf's family off the metal, and moved on, because the next entry mattered as much as this one, which was to say not at all.

*He wrote down every relic that they corrupted,* Thane thought. *Every single one. Pages of them. He kept a ledger like a merchant.*

*And he did not write down my father.*

He made himself finish the page. He made himself read the line at the bottom, in the cramped hand:

*They bring their relics to the Worldwound believing holiness makes them untouchable. Everything can be broken.*

"Thane." Caleth, quietly. "Do you want a moment."

"No." He took his finger off the parchment. "Turn it."

---

There was more, and most of it was worth having, and none of it was what he wanted.

The brother. Pages on the brother — Thane read those with his eyes going flat, aware of the low murmur of last rites still going twenty feet behind him. *Joran was at his prayers again tonight. Droskar. After everything we have seen. After everything we have become.* And then, further down, in the same paragraph, thrown away like a coin into a fountain: *I have brought cousins into the fold with less effort. Thurl needed only direction.*

Thane's eye caught on the word *cousins* for half a beat before his head supplied the obvious: *his.* Vhane blood, somewhere in the Five Kings Mountains, strangers. He let it go.

*He does not sleep unless exhaustion takes him at the table. He uses his magic to keep himself awake. Fool.*

*Finally he asked whether I ever thought of home. The Five Kings Mountains. After all these years. I laughed at him. I should not have.*

*His faith is failing him. I can see it even if he refuses to admit it. Perhaps Droskar has finally disappointed him. Perhaps Joran has finally disappointed Droskar. I do not particularly care which.*

*Months ago, I considered having him killed. There. I have written it.*

*But Joran is useful. No one else understands the corruption forge as he does. No one else has his hands. That is reason enough. It must be.*

*I have stationed three babaus with him. I told Joran they were there to protect the forge. He knew I was lying. I knew that he knew. Neither of us said anything.*

Korroc's jaw worked.

Then further in, and this one Thane felt Korroc read before he heard him read it, because his cousin's hand tightened on the edge of the desk hard enough that the good dwarven joinery creaked.

*The shadow arrived wearing a crusader named Maranse. An amusing disguise, though apparently not amusing enough to save Maranse once Kiranda received him.*

The room got quiet in a particular way.

Thane had known. That was the thing. He had known it in the garrison bunkroom with a rapier in his hand and a man screaming filth at him from the end of a chain, known it the way you know a floor is rotten before you have tested it, and he had been overruled, and an hour later there had been an ice blade in Caleth Aranor's spine.

*Being right is a cold room.* He had learned that in a war camp with Nurah's wrists in his hands. He had not expected to have to learn it twice in one day.

He said nothing at all. There was a version of this where he said something, and it would have been true, and it would have cost him his cousin for a week.

Korroc turned the page himself, hard.

The rest came fast, and it was the part an inquisitor would have traded a finger for.

A shadow demon called **Eustoyriax**, who had come up from something called the **Ivory Sanctum** bearing five vials of *distilled power* made from powdered **Nahyndrian crystal** — and Thane's whole spine went cold at that name, because he had heard it first in a letter in a tower in Kenabres and last from Irabeth's own mouth, and both times it had been about turning the wardstones into a weapon against everyone he had ever known. Staunton had drunk one. *Not borrowed. Not granted. Mine.* A dragon had died screaming of it. Something called a xacarba had lived somewhat longer.

*There are four elixirs remaining.*

**Xanthir Vang.** Master of the Templars of the Ivory Labyrinth in this region, leader of the Blackflame Adepts — and the worm that walks who had brought down the Blackwing's upper floors and taken something nobody could name. *I have stood beside demons while they butchered children,* Staunton had written. *Yet there is something about Vang that revolts me.*

**Irabeth Tirabade.** A page of her. Thane read it once and did not read it again and did not repeat any of it aloud, then or ever, because there are things you do not carry to a friend. *When Kenabres finally falls, I want her alive.*

And a succubus. Captured by Aponavicius herself, held below Drezen, awaiting transfer to an oubliette in the Rasping Rifts, and *escaped.* Hunters sent after her. An annis hag hired, devoted to Sifkesh, because if anyone can follow a trail made by betrayal and despair —

*Arueshalae. Succubus. Heretic.*

*Apparently even the Abyss has apostates.*

Thane read that line twice and could not work out why it snagged on him.

And then the last page, and the traitor had written it *to them*, and known he was doing it.

*Perhaps they will reach this room. Perhaps they will kill me. Perhaps they will even find this book. If so, let them read it. Let them read every name. Every relic broken upon our forge. Every crusader who screamed beneath these walls.*

*This is the legacy I leave them. I have no children. There will be no sons or daughters to carry the name of Staunton Vhane. My allies will compose no songs for me. So let this book serve instead.*

*And if the heroes of Kenabres are the ones reading these words —*

*Welcome to Drezen. Come and see what your victory has bought you.*

Caleth closed the book slowly.

"Every name," Thane said.

The others looked at him.

"He says *let them read every name.*" His voice came out level and he was distantly proud of that. "He wrote down a warhammer. He wrote down three shields. He wrote down a breastplate with the markings filed off." He nodded at the black leather. "Every name but two."

Nobody answered him, which was correct, because there was no answer.

Korroc picked the book up off the desk.

Thane watched his cousin carry it across the room to where Joran Vhane knelt over his brother's body, and crouch, and hold it out.

"Read what your brother thought of you."

Joran did not take it. He looked at the cover for a long moment, and at Korroc, and then away.

"I know he only kept me here because I can work the forge."

Korroc lowered the book.

"Can the things that have been corrupted be reversed?"

The old dwarf thought about that a long while. When he finally looked up his face had something in it that Thane, standing across a room full of dead demons, disliked intensely, because it looked like hope wearing a very old coat.

"I don't know."

Thane had heard enough.

"The first thing you're going to do," he said, "is come down with us and destroy the forge."

Caleth's head came up. "Not if we can use it."

"What?"

"If the corruption can be reversed —" the half-elf gestured at Joran, at the book, at the whole rotten fortress — "then that forge is the only thing in the world that can do it. Every relic on that list. Every shield. There are men still alive who would give an arm to have their family's steel back the way it was." He said it reasonably. He always said things reasonably. "We need it."

Thane's face clouded and he did not try to stop it.

"We don't need to use that forge." He heard the flint in his own voice and let it stay. "It will not lead to good things. It needs to be destroyed."

"We might—"

"No."

It was Joran Vhane who had injected the "no." He had stopped mid-rite.

He had been murmuring the whole time, steady, the old dwarven cadence going under the argument like water under a bridge — and he had stopped in the middle of a phrase, hand raised over his brother's chest, gone rigid.

Korroc was closest. "What?"

"Chorussina speaks through telepathy." Joran's voice had gone thin. "She just spoke to me. She's gone down into the rooms below and she demands I follow her." His eyes came up, and there was nothing in them now but plain animal fear. "She tried to reach Staunton. He didn't answer. She's assuming — correctly — that he's dead."

"And?" Thane said.

"She says she is going to begin a ritual that will destroy half of Drezen."

Thane felt his own eyes go wide and hated the tell. "Which half of the citadel?"

"No." Joran shook his head. "*Half of Drezen.* The city."

The city. Where Irabeth's army was, right now, coming up the Ahari and through the streets and winning.

"Chorussina is below?"

Joran nodded.

"If we find her, can we interrupt it?"

"I don't know."

Caleth looked at Korroc. "What do we do with Joran?"

Thane watched his cousin cross to the kneeling dwarf and go down to his level and look him full in the face, and he watched Korroc do the thing Korroc did, and for once he could not find it in himself to grudge him it.

"Joran. That book says you're losing your faith in Droskar — or Droskar is losing his faith in you, and either way it's the same thing." Korroc's voice was low and entirely without softness. "This is your chance. I truly want to see you redeem yourself and turn to Torag. He's waiting for you."

He let that sit exactly one heartbeat.

"Understand this. If we see you again and you are not in the Queen's custody, willingly facing her judgment — we *will* kill you. Do you understand?"

Joran Vhane nodded.

Korroc stood and turned and started walking.

"We have a job to do."

---

The map showed one stair down, and Thane went to it the way his mother had taught him to go to anything with a hinge.

*Listen first. Hinges and bolts speak.* He put his ear to the wood and gave it a slow count of ten and heard nothing but the fortress breathing. *Look second. Marks at the keyhole tell you who picks it most often.* Old marks, all of them, none fresh. *Touch third. The plate tells you if the lock's been forced before.* It hadn't.

He set two fingers against the doorframe for half a second — the small habit, the thing he had never once in his life named — and *click*, and pushed.

The stairwell went down into a dark his father had never drawn.

That was the thought that came with him down the steps and would not be put anywhere. He had studied his parents' blueprints until he could walk them blind, every corridor, every stair, every buttress in his father's careful hand and his mother's careful hand and ten other hands beside. And the parchment simply *stopped* here. Whatever was below had been cut into the stone of his family's citadel by something that had come after, and no dwarf had drawn it, and no dwarf had signed it, and there was no door in this dark whose builder Thane could name.

A doorkeeper wants to know who has business inside.

Down here, nobody did.

---

The first room was cages.

Narrow dark cells all down the walls, and around the main entrance an iron cage standing free with a cell door barring the vault behind. It had all been used, once, hard. It had not been used in a very long time. Whatever had been in the cages had died in them years ago, and someone had swept the worst of it out and never come back.

They moved through and Thane read the floor as he went. Dust broken in one line, boot-width, going the way they were going. Recent. *Somebody came through here today.*

The next set of double doors gave onto a passage — long, prison cells down both sides, ending in a barred chamber to the east, an alcove south with iron doors, a barred gate north.

Thane's hand went up.

So did Korroc's, at the exact same instant, and the two of them looked at each other and nodded, and it was the closest thing to conversation either of them had managed since the map room.

"Noise," Thane breathed.

Middle of the hall. North turn.

They went slow. The cells were empty — he checked every one anyway, one flick of the eyes each, because a doorkeeper who assumes is a doorkeeper who dies — and the emptiness had a texture to it that he did not like. Cells that have been empty a long time smell like dust. These smelled like a room somebody had left.

At the end the passage was barred across, a cell door standing to the left. Thane went the last twenty feet on the outside edges of his boots and put one eye to the gap.

Two men stood in the chamber beyond.

Human. Armored, or what was left of armored. Paladins, by the make of the plate — and Thane knew at a glance what the plate had cost, because he had spent a season at a forge beside his cousin and he knew good work when he saw it wrecked.

They were standing. That was the trouble. Their legs were broken. Both of them, and one had an arm that ended wrong, and a jaw hanging in a way a jaw does not hang on anything that draws breath, and they were *standing*, upright, with nothing holding them up.

He waved the others forward.

The four of them came up together, and the two things in the cell turned their heads.

"Specters," Caleth said, and cursed.

They came through the bars.

Not between. *Through* — the iron simply not mattering, the way a thought does not care about a wall — and one was on Korroc and the other was on Thane before any of them had finished drawing.

A hand went into his chest.

There was no impact. That was the horror of it; Thane had been hit by nearly everything the Worldwound had and every single one of them had at least had the decency to *hit*. This just arrived — a cold that was not temperature, going in under the ribs and taking hold of something that was not organ or blood, and *pulling*, the way you would pull a nail with your fingers to see if it was loose.

Something in Thane said *no.*

Not bravery. Nothing so grand. It was the same flat refusal he had put in front of Nurah's spell in a war camp, the same one that had shut the thoxels out of his head on the citadel's back wall — the doorkeeper's answer, given without thought and without ceremony. *You have no business in there.* The cold scrabbled once at the latch and found it held, and slid off him.

Beside him Korroc grunted like a man taking a step in deep water. The stone in his blood, doing the same job by a different road.

Nageru moved first and got nothing for it. His fists went into the thing and *through* it, and Thane saw the aasimar's face register that — not surprise, exactly; recalculation — before he had even finished the follow-through.

Thane took one step forward, and the daggers came up.

Mother's blade leading. Father's knife reversed along the forearm. He did not know how to fight a ghost. He knew how to fight a *thing in a doorway*, and that was what this was: a trespasser standing in the frame between the world and whatever came after, half in and half out, and everything half in a door has a seam.

He found it on the second thrust.

Mother's blade went into the place where the dead paladin's chest was thinnest — not thinnest in the flesh, there was no flesh, thinnest in the *sense* of it, the place where the shape flickered — and the specter came apart like breath on a cold morning and was gone.

Radiance took the other one a half-second later, Caleth stepping in with the leaf-blade blazing. Korroc's hammer swept through where it had been and found something on the way, a resistance like swinging through wet sand, and then Nageru shifted — one step that covered eight feet — and hit it twice, and Thane put both blades into the seam, and the second one went out.

Silence. Four sets of lungs. A cell with two piles of ruined armor in it and no bodies at all.

Thane pulled out his cloth and wiped mother's blade, and then father's knife, slowly, both sides.

There was nothing on either of them. That was not the point and had never been the point.

He looked at the plate on the floor. Iomedaean, both suits. Someone had broken their legs first. He thought about how long you have to hold a paladin of the Inheritor in a cell in the dark before he becomes the thing that stands in the cell in the dark, and then he stopped thinking about it, and stood up.

"They were ours," Korroc said.

"Aye."

That was all either of them had.

---

The whispering started as they left the room.

It came up out of nothing, everywhere at once — low, continuous, more than one voice or maybe a great many, running under the air like water under floorboards. Thane stopped dead and so did everybody else. He turned a slow circle with a dagger low and found no direction in it at all. No wall it came from. No door.

"Anyone make out words?" Caleth murmured.

Nobody could. It was pitched under speech, the way a cellar is under a house.

They waited. Nothing else came.

*The ritual,* Thane thought, and started walking again, and felt the tension in his shoulders slacken by exactly the amount that a named thing is less frightening than an unnamed one.

He would be thinking about that, later. About how quickly he had taken the first explanation offered, because it fit, and because it let him keep moving.

---

Through the doors, and into a room built for questions.

Two large cells flanked an interrogation chamber. Sigils were scribed into the floor at both cell mouths, up the lintels, across the barred doors themselves — layer on layer of the things, patient work, months of it. The western cell's sigils glowed a low red. The eastern cell's did not, and its door hung wide.

Caleth went in first and the rest of them let him, because this was his.

Thane watched him.

He watched because that was his job and because something about the half-elf's walk had changed the moment he crossed the threshold. Caleth Aranor moved through most rooms the way a man moves through a gallery. In this one he went carefully, and he crouched at the dead sigils, and Thane saw his hand hover over them without touching.

"Antimagic field," Caleth said, and frowned.

He rose and crossed to the other cell. The one still lit.

Thane saw it happen from ten feet away. The half-elf walked toward the red-glowing door and simply — stopped, four paces short, like a man walking into a rope in the dark. And Radiance, in his hand, *dimmed*. The steady hearth-light that had been in that blade since the day Thane carried it out of a ritual chamber wrapped in cloth went down like a lamp with the wick screwed low.

Caleth stood very still. Then he backed up. Three steps, four, not turning his back on it, shaking his head slowly.

He went the long way around the room after that, keeping the width of the chamber between himself and that door, and Thane watched him do it and thought: *he's giving it a berth like it's a snake.*

Thane had never once seen the half-elf afraid of a room.

He filed it. He did not ask. There were a great many things in Caleth Aranor that Thane had learned to file and not ask about, and he had a working theory that every one of them would come out eventually, in the man's own time, and that hurrying it would cost more than waiting.

Caleth went back into the open cell instead, and stopped.

"Thane," he said. "Come look at this."

---

The walls were covered in butterflies.

Hundreds of them. Thousands, maybe — Thane's eye started to count and gave up in the first square foot. They were scratched into the stone, every one, some the length of his thumb and some no bigger than a fingernail, layered over each other where the space had run out, going up the walls as high as an arm could reach and then higher, where somebody had stood on something. They had been made with no tool at all. He knelt and looked close and saw the truth of it in the shallow ragged grooves.

*Fingernails.* Years of fingernails.

And in the middle of the western wall, cut deeper and larger than all the rest, words.

*HEAR MY PRAYER, LADY LUCK. FOR IT IS LUCK I NEED IN ESCAPING THIS CELL. NOT JUST THE LIFE BEHIND THESE BARS, BUT AN ETERNITY SPENT SHACKLED TO THE ABYSS ITSELF. IF YOU TRULY ARE THE TENDER OF DREAMS, THEN TENDER UNTO ME A NEW EXISTENCE. LET ME SING THE SONG OF THE SPHERES, SEE THE STARS WITH NEW EYES, AND ESCAPE THE PIT INTO WHICH MY SOUL WAS CAST. BRING ME GOOD FORTUNE, MY LADY. BRING ME A NEW SPIRIT MADE CLEAN. AND I WILL FOREVER BE YOURS, IN LIFE, AND IN FAITH. I AM READY. FINALLY READY.*

Nobody spoke for a while.

Butterflies. Desna. The Song of the Spheres, the Tender of Dreams — Thane knew her the way a dwarf knows the gods that are not his, which is to say by their signs and not their doctrine. Wanderers' goddess. Stars and luck and dreams.

Nageru said it first.

"The succubus. From the journal." He was looking at the wall with his hands loose at his sides. "It said they had one locked up here."

Korroc's eyes came up, and he nodded slowly. "Aye. Jailed, and escaped. That would explain *shackled to the Abyss.*"

"Arueshalae," Caleth said.

Thane stayed crouched.

He was thinking, in the flat procedural way he thought about everything, about the *work* of it. About how long it takes to carve a thousand butterflies into granite with your hands. About a thing out of the pit sitting in a stone box inside an antimagic field, in the deepest hole of a demon fortress, and reaching — not for the door. For a goddess of stars, on the far side of everything, who had no reason at all to listen.

And the door hung open.

He looked at it. Sigils dead, iron swung wide.

Somebody had gotten out of here. The one prisoner Aponavicius had ordered specifically not to lose. A demon had prayed to be made *new*, for years, with her fingernails, and then walked out of a room that magic could not work in, and Staunton Vhane's own hunters had never found her.

Thane stood up.

He was aware that he did not know how to hold this. He had spent his whole life understanding demons perfectly: thieves at a door that should never have opened, trespassers to a man, every one of them, no exceptions and no complications. It was the cleanest thing he owned. His father had died to it.

And here was a cell with a thousand butterflies in it.

*It could be a lie,* he thought. *A play. Something carved to be found.*

He looked at the height of the highest butterflies and the depth of the lowest and the way they overlapped where the space ran out, and he knew, with the same certainty that told him a floor was rotten, that nobody carves a lie for ten years.

"Well," he said, and heard how flat it came out. "Something happened in here."

He went out past the dead sigils and did not look at the other cell.

---

At the end of the hall the passage opened into a larger chamber, barred across, and six things hit the bars at a dead run.

Thane had his blades up before his mind caught up. They came fast and hard, six of them, arms through the gaps to the shoulder, fingers hooking, faces mashing against the iron with their mouths open. Fangs. Not the crude broken teeth of ghouls — long clean ones, and eyes that had something behind them.

"Vampire," said Caleth and Nageru together.

Caleth again, a beat later. "Spawn. But still."

Thane looked at their clothes and something in him went very cold and very quiet.

Rags. But rags with a shape to them — a surcoat's cut here, a sword-belt's wear-line there, and on one of them a pauldron strap still buckled over a shoulder with nothing left to hold up. Crusader kit. All six.

He did not know their names. He was standing in a fortress where the ledger of everything worth writing down had been kept for seventy years and their names were not in it either.

"We need to put them down," Caleth said.

Korroc frowned. "We've a more pressing issue to take care of."

"We're on a timetable," Thane heard himself say.

And there it was. Six men who had come north to hold the line, hanging on the bars of a cage in the dark under Drezen with their hands out, and Thane Stonevein — who had stood threshold-watch over two murdered knights in the Gray Garrison because a door closed proper is not nothing — turned around and started walking.

"This is a later issue," Korroc said, to nobody, in a voice like a man signing something.

They walked away. The hissing followed them a long way down the passage, and then the bars were too far, and it was only the whispering again.

*Later,* Thane told himself. *You come back for them later.*

He noted, distantly, that this was exactly the kind of promise a man makes so he can keep his feet moving, and he made it anyway.

---

The double doors south smelled of fresh blood before he had them halfway open.

He knew the room from the smell alone — that sharp copper tang with nothing rotten in it yet, meaning *hours* — and when the gap widened enough to see, the shape of it confirmed everything. A restraining table with straps. Implements racked on the walls in an order somebody had thought about. Tongs. Hooks. A brazier gone cold.

And two babau, unhurried, cleaning the equipment.

They had a bucket between them and they were working a set of pincers with a rag, and they were *talking*, in that clicking bubbling language of theirs, in the exact tone of two men closing up a shop. Not one of them was looking at the door.

Thane looked at that for one full second longer than he should have.

Then he went in.

He crossed the flagstones with the whole weight of himself in his boots and mother's blade went into the nearest one's flank — and the demon *saw* him, at the last instant, a stupid piece of luck, and rolled with it so the blade went in shallow and skidded on rib. It sprang back, shrieking, oily hide rippling.

Caleth was a stride behind him and thrust and missed clean.

Nageru did not miss. The aasimar had come through the door and past both of them and was simply *there*, opposite Thane, on the far side of the demon, and his fists went in twice with the flat wet sound of a mallet on meat.

Korroc pushed past Caleth and put himself in the second demon's path, shield up, and the second demon obliged him.

They were fast, both of them. The one on Nageru got in twice, claws finding the aasimar's guard and getting through it, and the one on Korroc raked at him twice and Thane heard his cousin's plate ring and saw the stone-hard skin under it turn most of what got past.

Then it was their round, and Thane had a flank.

That was all he had ever needed. *Don't try to hit the chest. Hit the joints.* The demon was turned three-quarters away from him, dealing with Nageru, and Thane put mother's blade in under the arm where the hide gathered, and father's knife into the hollow behind the knee, and mother's blade again up under the jaw, three in the time it takes to say it, and the babau screamed and folded and was gone — banished, snapped back to whatever pit had lent it out.

Radiance came in on the survivor. Nageru, with no target left, crossed the room without seeming to hurry and arrived behind the second demon and came down on it with both fists.

Korroc's hammer took it twice and it went the way of the first.

An empty torture room, and four of them breathing.

Korroc closed his eyes a moment, and Thane watched the ruin come out of Nageru's arm and go into his cousin along the thread, and then watched Korroc mend himself. He never got used to seeing it. It was the least dwarven thing about the most dwarven man he knew.

Thane wiped both blades and went to look at the table.

Straps still buckled. Blood on the leather, tacky, not dry. He put two fingers near it without touching.

"Somebody was on this," he said. "Today."

---

They took a different door out, and the whispers came with them, and Thane knelt at the next lock in the passage and started listening.

He got about four seconds into it.

"What was that?"

Caleth's voice, from across the room. Thane looked up. The half-elf had stopped short to give him working room the way he always did, and then had turned around entirely and was facing the other way, chin up, *sniffing.*

"Something's going on over here." He was already moving. "A puff of white smoke. Under that door. But no *smell* of smoke."

Thane finished his door because a door half-read is worse than a door unread. He pushed it open and left it standing open and went after them.

Nageru reached the far door first and stopped and listened, head tilted, in that way he had of listening with his whole body.

"Chains," he said. "Dragging across a floor."

Caleth arrived, leaned in, and pushed the door open.

Bookshelves — well-stocked, floor to ceiling, all four walls. A plush carpet, deep and dark and expensive, on the floor of a dungeon under a demon fortress. A reading room. Somebody's *study*.

A vampire spawn stood across the room.

And in the middle of the carpet, a half-orc in good clothes stood with a shackled crusader held up against his chest, one hand fisted in the man's hair to bare the throat, and his mouth on the vein.

He pulled his fangs out with the unhurried irritation of a man interrupted at supper, and hissed.

Thane spoke the cadence before anyone moved — the low Dwarven words settling down over all four of them like a hand on a shoulder, the doorkeeper's blessing, offered fast and without ceremony because there was no time for the other kind.

Nageru went across the room.

Thane had seen the aasimar do this a hundred times and it still did something to the back of his neck. The vampire hauled the crusader in front of himself as a shield, which was the correct move and would have worked on anybody else — and Nageru simply *tumbled*, low and impossibly economical, under the man's dangling feet and up behind, and his fists came down on the back of the half-orc's skull with everything in him. Thane heard the crack of it. Clean, the kind of blow that ends most arguments.

The vampire dropped the crusader, who folded to the carpet like laundry.

Then it turned its head and looked Thane straight in the eyes.

The will came at him like a hand on the front of his coat. Not cold, this time. *Warm.* It was reasonable, and it was patient, and it knew his name — or it knew the shape of the place where his name went, and it reached for the latch with the perfect confidence of something that had done this ten thousand times.

*No business here.*

Thane shook it off so hard his teeth clicked.

The half-orc growled, gave up on him, and turned on Nageru with a dagger — good steel, and fast, and it went into the aasimar's side before he could roll clear.

Korroc moved past all of it and put himself down beside the crusader on the floor, opposite Nageru, and the room was a box with a vampire in the middle of it. Thane came up on the third side. Caleth took the spawn.

Nageru hit the half-orc again, and then his hands went cold-gold with holy light, and hit it twice more.

Radiance went into the spawn twice and the thing shrieked as the light entered it and came apart on the carpet.

Then the vampire sensed Korroc at its back and spun, and cast — something aimed at the hammer-and-anvil on Korroc's shield, a black clutching thing that broke on the sigil and did nothing at all. Korroc swung twice and hit air both times; the half-orc moved like water going downhill. It slipped Thane's thrust too, and Thane swore in Dwarven.

Nageru's hands were still glowing. He hit it twice, hard.

And Caleth crossed the room and made the fourth corner of the box and drove Radiance in.

The vampire came apart into smoke.

"*No—*" Thane started.

The smoke rolled — not up, along, low across the carpet like spilled milk running downhill, and it reached the wall, and it went into a crack.

Nageru was after it. Thane was two steps behind him and already reading the wall as he ran, and he had it before he arrived.

"Secret door." He got a thumb into the seam and felt for the plate. "And locked."

He knelt. Listened. Looked. Touched. Then the picks came out, and the lock was an honest one, and it gave in under twenty seconds. *Click.* The stone swung.

A small chamber. A coffin.

Nageru walked in and pushed the lid back with one hand.

The half-orc lay in it, reformed, eyes shut, waiting out the hours it needed. There was a moment where Thane thought about what it would be like to wake up from that.

Nageru did not give it the hours.

He drove a stake through its chest first, leaning his whole weight onto it with the deliberation of a man setting a post. Then he unslung the adamantine battleaxe he had been carrying since the map room — the monk who used no weapons, carrying a weapon since morning because that had been the right tool once and he had not put it down — and brought it down across the neck, and the head came off clean.

Then he uncorked a flask of holy water and poured it slow and thorough over the stump and over the head both, and stood there while it steamed, and made sure.

"Nul," Thane said.

Nageru nodded, and did not look up until the steaming had stopped.

*Well,* Thane thought. *One of the two we were warned about.*

---

Korroc was on his knees on the carpet with the crusader's head in his lap.

Thane came back out to find the man's eyes fluttering open, and his cousin's broad hand flat on the man's chest, holding him down as gently as a man holds a bird.

"What's your name, crusader?"

"Idrian." A dry crack of a voice. "Idrian Vosner."

"Slow, now." Korroc got a potion out and propped him up and tipped it in a swallow at a time.

Thane and Nageru went through the shelves while that was happening, and the shelves were the strangest thing Thane had seen all day.

Histories. Real ones — the crusades, all of them, going back to the First. Chronicles and campaign records and three separate accounts of the founding of Drezen, and a shelf of scroll-cases with tags in a crabbed hand. Somebody down here had *read.* Somebody had sat on that carpet with a lamp, for years, working steadily through the recorded history of the people it was eating.

They took what they could carry. Thane went along the tags with his eyes and took the four oldest and left the rest, and thought: *Aravashnial's going to want a week in this room.*

They came out with their arms full to find Korroc looking up at him.

"Can you take this man's shackles off?"

Thane looked at his cousin. Looked at the adamantine warhammer lying on the carpet next to his cousin's knee.

"You have a hammer."

Korroc chuckled — the first sound like that either of them had made since the map room — and let it go, and Thane knelt and had the shackles off in ten seconds, and the fact that neither of them said anything else about it was, Thane thought, most of an apology in both directions.

Idrian Vosner could not stand. That much was obvious the moment they tried it. He could not have walked twenty feet, let alone out of a citadel with a battle in it.

So they carried him into the vampire's own chamber, past the beheaded thing in the coffin, and set him against the wall with a waterskin and his own recovered dagger in his hand, and Thane pulled the secret door shut on him.

"Stay here," Korroc said through the gap. "We'll be back."

The stone closed. Thane stood with his hand on it for a second.

The safest room in this dungeon was a vampire's bedroom, because it was the only room down here that locked from the inside and nothing knew about. He turned that over and found it did not get less ugly with handling.

---

They were checking their kit to move on when they heard boots in the passage.

Every one of them stopped dead. Thane went to the doorframe with both blades out, low, and Korroc set his shield, and Nageru's hands came up.

Joran Vhane walked through the door.

He looked at the four of them — at the dead spawn, at the blood on the carpet, at Nageru's axe — and Thane watched Korroc's shoulders go tight and watched him not swing.

Joran spoke fast.

"You've gone the wrong way."

He turned around and started back out. "You need to follow me."

Thane found his voice. "What?"

Joran stopped in the doorway and looked back at them with an expression of pure exhausted impatience, the look of a foreman watching apprentices carry a beam the long way around a yard.

"You're going the wrong way. You have to go through the forge to get there."

And he walked out into the passage.

The four of them looked at each other.

Thane's mouth was dry. He ran the numbers the way he always ran them, fast and cold: the man had been left alone in a room with his brother's body and a clear route to a fortress full of allies and every reason in the world to run, and instead he had come down a stair into a dungeon after four people who had promised to kill him, in order to tell them they had taken a wrong turn.

*That'll bite us in the ass,* he had said, this morning.

He still thought it might. That was the trouble with being right often: it stopped being a thing you trusted and started being a thing you *carried*, and it weighed the same whether you were right or not.

They followed him.

---

Joran led them back to the stair-room, and south of it, and behind the entrance of a chamber that ended in a blank wall.

He walked up to the blank wall and pushed a particular place on it.

A section slid aside and the heat came out over them like a hand.

Thane had grown up around forges. He had spent a season working beside Korroc at one. He knew forge-heat, and this was not forge-heat. This was heat with an *intention* in it.

Beyond the short hall the room opened up enormous. A ten-foot cage of a lift stood on an elevated terrace at the top, chains and pulleys running away above it on a rail — down into the chamber, or across it to a set of double doors on the far side. Below the terrace the walls glowed dull red, actually glowed, and vapor came off the floor in slow wisps, and the temperature up on the terrace was somehow almost cool.

And down in the middle of it, squatting on the floor of the pit, a forge of black and red metal.

Thane looked at it a long time.

It was well made. That was the thing that got into him. Whoever had built the thing had known what they were doing — the proportions right, the throat of it correct, the bellows-housing set exactly where a working smith would want it. It was the work of somebody with hands.

*No one else understands the corruption forge as he does. No one else has his hands.*

Joran stepped onto the lift.

Caleth stared at him for a moment, measuring — Thane could see the half-elf doing arithmetic on the old dwarf, the same arithmetic Thane had been doing since the doorway — and then stepped on. Korroc went next. Nageru after him. Thane last, with his back to the cage door and one hand on it.

Joran worked the controls with the fluency of forty years.

The cage went out along the top rail, over the pit, and the heat came up through the floor of it. Thane looked down through the bars. Down in the red, things moved among the coals — long, glistening, coiled, tending the fires with a slow attentiveness that had no urgency in it at all. Salamanders. Outsiders who had been keeping this fire lit for longer than Thane had been alive, and would keep it lit after.

Beside him, all three of the others had gone rigid in the same instant.

He glanced at them. Caleth's jaw. Nageru's hands. Korroc's whole face.

*The paladins.* Whatever the three of them could feel rolling off that thing, Thane got only the edge of it — the way you feel a fever in someone else's hand. He was glad of it. Looking at their faces was enough.

The cage came down at the far side and stopped in front of two doors.

"I don't have keys to these," Joran said. "I've never been allowed through."

Thane knelt.

Listen. Nothing. Look — and here the marks told him something, which was that these doors were opened rarely and by someone with a key. Touch: never been forced.

He got his picks out.

It fought him. He got two of the four pins and lost them both, and reset, and got three and lost all three, and swore steadily in Dwarven under his breath the way he always did, and on the fourth attempt something inside the mechanism shifted wrong and the pick *snapped.*

Thane stood up slowly with half a pick in his hand.

He looked at it.

It was an entirely ordinary thing to happen. Picks broke. This one had been in his kit since Anevia's storeroom and had opened a hundred doors. There was no reason at all for the small cold weight that arrived under his ribs.

Except that he was a doorkeeper standing in front of a door in his father's citadel, and the door had beaten him.

He put the pieces away, and took out the chime.

He had carried it since the Gray Garrison, and he did not like using it, and he had never entirely worked out why. He knew why. It was because a chime does not care about hinges or plates or marks at the keyhole. It does not ask who has business inside. It simply overrules the question.

He struck it once.

The doors shuddered and gave, and Thane pushed, and they swung open easy.

---

The chamber beyond was odd-shaped. A small dais of steps went down into it and more stairs led away northwest and southeast. Lit candles stood in a ring on the floor around a circle of arcane purpose scribed in something dark. The ceiling went up nearly forty feet, and hanging from it on a heavy chain, turning very slightly, was a crystal the size of a horse.

It was purple. It pulsed.

Thane had seen a crystal that color exactly once, in a vision that came out of a shattering wardstone, in the arms of the witch who had opened the Worldwound.

Four figures stood beneath it. Three were schir — hairy, horned, goat-legged, thick through the chest, holding their halberds like men who had used them. The fourth was a tiefling woman with her hands up and her voice going in a low continuous cadence that fit itself into the whispering that had been under everything for an hour, and did not stop when the doors opened.

"Chorussina?" Caleth said.

Joran nodded.

They spent one moment on themselves, because they were not fools and the ritual was still going and a moment was what they could afford. Thane put the blessing over all four of them. Caleth threw up a ward against chaos that settled on Thane's skin like a coat out of a cold room. Korroc raised his faith around himself. Nageru breathed out and became fractionally harder to touch.

Then Nageru went, and there was no more waiting.

He crossed the floor and hit the nearest schir twice, and the second blow was one of those the aasimar landed perhaps once a fight — everything aligned, all the weight through, the whole still-water discipline going out through four inches of knuckle. The demon came off its hooves. It was dead before it finished traveling, and it traveled a long way: the body went back and up and *into* the great crystal with a crack that Thane felt in his back teeth, and slid down it, and lay still.

The crystal swung on its chain. Kept pulsing.

Nobody looked at it. There was no reason to.

The other two schir came at Nageru together, and one of them opened him from hip to ribs with the halberd's spike.

Then the world folded, and Korroc and Caleth were simply *gone* from beside Thane and standing behind the tiefling forty feet away.

Chorussina turned inside her own ritual and spat a word, and a cloud boiled up around the two of them, thick and yellow-green and stinking of something dead in standing water. Thane saw his cousin's head go back and his throat work, and saw Caleth press the back of his wrist to his mouth — and both of them held, and did not double over, and Korroc swung.

He swung through her.

Thane saw it and understood it instantly: *displaced.* The image was standing a half-pace off the woman. Korroc's second swing corrected for it and connected — and as the hammer struck, fire ran off of her, up the haft and jumped onto him, and Thane heard his cousin grunt.

Thane ran.

He ran the whole length of that chamber flat out, which was not his preference and not his craft, and he did it because Nageru was alone in a corner with two schir on him and there was no clever way to be there in time. He came in on the far side of the nearest demon, and there was the flank, and there were the joints.

Nageru's fists took the first one three times and it was gone.

Caleth thrust twice with Radiance and both went home, and the tiefling staggered.

The last schir hit Nageru again. Thane got in under its guard and mother's blade went into the armpit and father's knife found the inside of the thigh where the great vessel runs, and the third strike ended it, and the last demon in the room dropped and vanished.

Chorussina took one step back — and Thane saw two auras drag at her as she moved, Korroc's and Caleth's both, the anchoring weight that had shut more than one demons' escape-roads that day.

It did not hold her.

She went out of the world and came back into it forty feet away, standing directly behind Joran Vhane.

Korroc took one step toward Nageru instead of toward her, because he was feeling the aasimar's wounds through the thread and could not do everything, and sent a wave of gold rolling out over all of them. Thane felt the ragged places along his ribs close.

Nageru was across the chamber before the gold had finished. He reached the tiefling and hit her once.

Then Caleth reached out and put a hand on Korroc, and the two of them were gone and standing beside her, and Thane saw the half-elf's face as he arrived and knew what was coming, because he had seen it at the Tower of Estrod and at the Ahari and in a room with a false goddess in it.

Caleth reached down into the golden place the wardstone had left in all of them, and reached again, twice in the same breath, spending like a man who has decided the account will not be needed after today.

He put his hand on Chorussina and let it go.

The lightning did not crack. It *tore* — one white sheet of it, so bright the candle-ring went invisible, so loud Thane's ears rang flat. The tiefling arched, every limb straightening at once, and when Caleth took his hand away she went down onto the stone and did not move again.

---

Silence. Or what passed for it.

Because the whispering was still going.

Thane heard it in the ringing aftermath and did not, at first, understand what he was hearing. He stood in the middle of the chamber with both daggers out and turned in a slow circle looking for the thing that had died and had not stopped.

Joran Vhane took two steps back from the group and went down on his knees.

There was a curved bone spike standing out of his back.

Thane had not seen it happen. He put that together a half-second later — the tiefling's last act, the thing in her hand as Nageru reached her, some pale claw off an insect the length of a forearm — and by then Korroc was already there.

Joran tried to heal himself. Thane watched him do it: the hand going to the wound, the old words starting, the whole practiced motion of a man who has done this ten thousand times.

Nothing happened.

Korroc carefully pulled the claw out of him, and Thane saw his cousin's face change as he touched it — saw him drop the thing on the flagstones like a hot coal — and then Korroc's hand was on Joran's back and the gold went in and the wound closed.

"Korroc," Thane said. "Don't touch that again."

"Aye. It's hungry."

Then, above them, the crystal made a sound.

Thane looked up.

He was standing directly beneath it. He had crossed under it at a run and stopped under it to kill a demon and had never once given it a thought, and now he looked up forty feet into a mass of purple the size of a horse and saw that its pulsing had gone wrong — not the slow tidal glow of an hour ago but a stutter, arrhythmic, panicked — and that a crack had opened from one end of it to the other, and that the crack was branching.

It came off the chain.

Thane went sideways and down and rolled, hard, without any grace at all, and the world came apart above him. The crystal hit the flagstones and burst, and the sound was enormous, and shards went out across the chamber in a flat sheet, and a great slow bloom of purple smoke rolled up out of the wreck and hung there and thinned and was gone.

Thane came up on one knee brushing crystal out of his beard.

The candles had all gone out.

"Everyone standing?" Caleth, hoarse.

They were. Somehow.

Thane got up and looked at the wreckage of the thing, and then, slowly, tipped his head.

The whispering was still going.

He watched Korroc realize it at the same moment. His cousin's brow came down and he turned his head one way and then the other, and Thane saw the exact instant the wrong conclusion came out from under him.

"I thought that was the ritual," Korroc muttered, and shook his head.

So did Thane. He had thought it too, an hour ago, in a corridor by two dead paladins, and he had let himself stop looking for the source because he had a name for it.

The ritualist was dead. Her demons were dead. The crystal was in pieces on the floor and the candles were out and the circle was broken.

And something in this dark was still talking.

*You took the first answer that fit,* he told himself, *and you walked six rooms on it.*

He put a hand on the doorframe of the next door without thinking about it at all.

---

Joran Vhane was staring at his hands.

"It failed," he said.

He said it quietly, to nobody. He turned the hands over and looked at the palms, and then the backs again, the way a man checks a tool that has stopped working.

Korroc crouched next to him and put a hand on his shoulder.

"Listen. It can be all right. Torag will take you in."

Joran looked at him.

That was all. He did not answer and he did not argue and he did not weep. He looked at Korroc for a while, with an old dwarf's face gone completely blank, and then went back to looking at his hands.

Thane, watching from the doorway with the whisper going in the dark behind him, thought: *there it is.*

Because he had spent the whole morning being told there was a road out of this for Joran Vhane. Korroc had offered it in the map room with a death sentence tied to it, and offered it again over a book, and offered it again now, and Thane had listened to every offer with the flat certainty of a man watching someone try to bail a boat with a colander.

And it turned out he had been wrong about the shape of the thing.

Joran had not refused. Joran had *turned around* — had walked down a stair into a dungeon full of people who wanted him dead, to tell them they were going the wrong way. Whatever that was, it was a step, and he had taken it on his own.

And somewhere between that step and this room, the hands had stopped working.

Thane did not know what had done it. The claw, maybe. The ritual. The crystal coming apart. Or a god — the Dark Smith noticing at last that his priest had begun to look at a door, and closing it. There was no way to tell from here and there might never be.

But he knew what it looked like, from the doorway, and what it looked like was this: a man had reached for something better and been charged the price before he ever got there.

Thane had opinions about gods who did that.

He kept them, and went to check the next door.

---

Beyond it, the hall was thirty feet high and lit red.

Swaths of lichen grew in broad sheets across the walls, pulsing faintly, throwing everything in the color of a banked fire. Alcoves lined both sides, and in each alcove a mural, elaborate and colorful and lovingly done, with an inscription chiseled into the stone beneath.

Thane looked at the first one and stopped walking.

Deskari. The Lord of the Locust Host, the thing that had torn the Worldwound open — insectoid, immense, rendered with a care that made Thane's skin crawl — and under his blades, a woman in a familiar sunburst tabard, being opened from throat to hip.

The next alcove: the same demon lord, and Sarenrae.

The next: Torag. Thane heard Korroc's breathing change beside him and did not look at his cousin.

Then Desna. Then a figure Thane did not recognize until Nageru went very still, and then he did, and it was Irori. Then Shelyn.

Six gods, six deaths, six patient beautiful paintings, and one alcove larger than the rest holding a shrine to Deskari with a statue in it so lifelike that Thane checked it twice for breathing.

He walked the hall reading them, because looking away is how a thing like this wins — he had learned that from a half-elf in a great court one floor up — and somewhere around the fourth alcove he noticed the thing that he would keep and not say.

Alseta was not on the wall.

Of course she wasn't. She was a minor goddess; doors and thresholds and the turning of the year, a two-headed mask, a mantra a mother teaches a son over a lock. Not worth the paint. The enemy had gone to enormous trouble to defile six gods in this hallway and had not thought of his at all.

He found, somewhat to his own surprise, that he was not insulted.

*You don't paint a picture of the door you're going to walk through,* he thought. *You just walk through it. And she notices.*

At the far end of the hall, on a massive iron framework, hung a banner.

Red. And on it, in gold, the sunburst of Iomedae.

Nobody ran. That was one small mercy Thane would let them all have afterward — that after seventy years and three hundred miles and a fortress full of dead, not one of the four of them ran the last thirty feet like a fool.

They walked. And ten paces out, Thane threw out both arms.

"*Wait!*"

Everyone stopped dead.

Thane stood there and looked.

"What is it?" Caleth said.

He did not answer right away, because he was still working. Something in the room was wrong and had been wrong since the door, and he had been reading it under the murals the whole time without letting it up to the front of his mind: the lichen thinning in a line across the floor eight feet out from the banner. The wear on the flagstones. The way the alcoves were spaced — evenly, exactly, too exactly, like the ports on a wall.

"A trap," he said.

"Where?"

He turned in a slow circle, and he heard the frustration come into his own voice and could not keep it out.

"The whole damn room." He gestured at all of it, both walls, thirty feet of ceiling. "The whole room's the trap. But I can't tell where the trigger is."

He went at it anyway.

He got down on the flagstones and worked forward on his knees, running his fingertips along the joints, checking each stone for the half-hair of movement that means a plate. He found nothing. He worked the base of the iron framework — nothing. He worked the nearest alcove's lip, and the inscription channel, and the seam where the lichen met the stone, and there was *nothing*, no plate, no wire, no lever, no seam that did not belong, and the whole time the small cold thing sat under his ribs and grew.

*Listen. Look. Touch.*

He listened, and looked, and touched, and his mother's grammar told him there was a door here and did not tell him where.

He reached in with a pick to try the one place left he had any faith in.

The room went off.

Fire came out of the murals. All of them at once — out of every alcove on both walls, out of the painted mouths and blades and wounds, and it did not spread like fire. It *gathered*, drawing itself up into six burning shapes of the same insectoid horror painted behind it, and the six of them came off the walls together and slammed inward.

Thane went flat and rolled and it went over the top of him without touching him at all, and he came up in the middle of a hall full of burning demon-shapes with his heart going like a hammer.

The other three took it. Thane saw Korroc set his feet and wear it, saw Caleth's strange curtain turn most of it aside, saw Nageru breathe out through the heat — and all three of them came through, scorched and swearing, and it hurt them, and they stood.

The fire went out.

And the banner changed.

Thane saw it happen from thirty feet, and it was the quietest thing in the room. The red went out of it like blood out of a face. The cloth turned black. And the gold sunburst of the Inheritor twisted, ran, reformed, and became the locust-blade mark of Deskari, and hung there in the red lichen-light, entirely still.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Thane cursed. One word, ugly, the worst one dwarves had.

*A decoy.*

He walked up to it — carefully, checking every flagstone, and finding nothing, and knowing now exactly how much that was worth — and stood under a black banner in an iron frame at the end of a hall full of murdered gods.

It was good cloth. Somebody had taken trouble. Somebody had hung a false Sword of Valor in the deepest hall of this fortress and rigged a room around it so that when the crusaders finally came, all this way, through all of that, up all those stairs and down all those stairs, the last thing they would get was the sight of their own symbol turning into the enemy's while the walls burned them.

*Aponavicius likes trophies,* Joran had said. *Things she can show off and brag about.*

"It's not the banner," Thane said. "It was never the banner."

"No." Caleth's voice, behind him, and there was something in it that was not disappointment. It was closer to professional respect, which Thane thought was probably the most Caleth thing about the whole day. "It's a joke. It's an extremely expensive joke."

Korroc came up beside Thane and looked at the black cloth for a long moment.

"Then it's still down here somewhere," he said. "Fine. We keep looking."

---

They went back through it. All of it. Every alcove, every inscription, every seam of the shrine, working the hall from the far end back with four sets of eyes, because Thane had made a hard little decision on the walk back which was that this room was not going to beat him twice.

It was Nageru who found it.

He was walking the northern wall behind the Deskari statue with one hand out, not quite touching the stone — Thane had watched him do that before, once, in a treasury, and had learned to pay attention when he did it — and he stopped, and tipped his head back.

"Is that a door?"

Twenty feet up the wall behind the statue, a seam.

Caleth squinted at it. Thane came around the base of the shrine and got the angle and saw it too: a line in the stonework that ran too straight and had two friends, a rectangle where a rectangle should not be, high up in a wall with nothing beneath it. No stair. No ladder. No handhold. Nothing at all in this hall to say the thing existed except a joint a fraction wider than the joints around it.

*Nobody was ever meant to find that.*

"I can climb the statue," Nageru said. "I can reach it from the shoulder."

Caleth shook his head and unslung his bow. "Hold on."

He got a piton out of his pack — the ranged one, the odd magical thing he had been carrying since Kenabres and had used perhaps twice — and set it and drew and put it into the wall three feet above the seam, where it bit into demon-cut stone and held like it had grown there.

They rigged the rope, and made a sling of it, and hoisted Thane.

Because of course it was Thane. It was always going to be Thane. He went up the wall of a hall full of defiled gods with his boots walking the stone and his cousin's fists on the rope below him, and thought about the fact that there was not one soul in the world he would rather have holding that rope, and did not say it, because you do not say that to Korroc Stonevein in a working corridor.

He came level with the seam and got both hands on it.

*Listen.* He put his ear to the stone and there was nothing on the other side but the whispering, which was here too, which was everywhere.

*Look.* The joint was clean. Nobody had opened this in a long time.

*Touch.*

His fingers walked the seam, and found a place two-thirds along the left edge where the stone was worn very slightly smooth, and pressed.

The door swung inward.

Thane got a knee onto the ledge and hauled himself out of the sling and stood up on a lip of stone twenty feet above the floor of the hall, in the mouth of a passage nobody had drawn on any map in the world.

Behind him and below, three men and a lit banner black as pitch.

Ahead of him, a short dark passage.

And at the end of it, unmistakable in the light coming up from behind him, another secret door.

Thane crouched at the top of the ledge and looked at it for a moment before he called down.

Two doors, one behind the other, twenty feet up a wall in a hall built to make crusaders despair. Nobody builds that to protect a storeroom. You put a door behind a door when the thing on the other side is the last thing you have.

Somewhere in this dark, a shadow demon was keeping a banner.

And somewhere in this dark, something that had not stopped for the ritualist's death, or the crystal's breaking, or the candles going out, was still whispering.

*Every name but two,* he thought, and put his hand flat on the frame.

"There's another one," he called down. "Bring the rope."
