# The Shadow That Cast None

*Chapter Twenty-Two — What the Water Showed*

---

The silence in the command room went on a little too long, and Caleth was the one who broke it, because he had found a handhold and a man who is falling takes the handhold that is there.

"What color was this creature?"

His own voice surprised him. It came out level. It came out in the register he used for asking a quartermaster about grain, and some small clean part of him stood off to one side and noted the achievement with distaste.

Aron Kir looked up from the table. He had been looking at the broken staff. Everyone had.

"Red," Aron said.

Red. Caleth turned that over. Red was a thing with a shape to it. Red was a category, and categories had properties, and properties could be prepared against, and preparation was a thing a person could *do* while standing in a room where there was otherwise nothing whatsoever to do.

"Did it use a breath weapon?"

Aron nodded. Then he did something Caleth had not expected: he lifted his head and looked Caleth directly in the eyes, and held it, the way a man does when he has decided you are going to argue with him and he would rather get it over with.

"Acid," Aron said.

The room did not change. Caleth felt it change anyway, the way a floor changes when you learn the beam under it is cracked.

"Acid?" Korroc's head came up. "What the —"

"I know." Aron put both hands flat on the table beside the two pieces of the staff. "I know what it sounds like. I can only tell you what I saw. It came in three times and it was not fire."

*It was not fire.*

Caleth stood very still, and in the space behind his eyes the arithmetic he had been running since he walked through the gate came apart and reassembled around a new and considerably worse premise.

A red dragon breathes fire. That was not a rule of thumb or a scholar's convention; it was nearer to a law of the material world than most laws of the material world. The color was the fire. The scale grew the way it grew because of what lived behind it. You could no more have a red that breathed acid than you could have a river that ran uphill out of modesty.

Unless something had been done to it.

Six legs, he thought. Six.

He had let that detail go by in the first minute, because Aron had been saying *carried them off* and there had been no room in his head for anything else, and because the mind is generous to itself in the first minute and files the impossible things under *the man was upset, he miscounted.*

Aron Kir did not miscount. Aron Kir had built a bridge across a gorge in the Worldwound with nine casualties and had been flatly embarrassed about the nine.

Six legs. Red scale. Acid.

That was not a dragon. That was something that had been a dragon and had been *worked on*, and the only workshop within four hundred miles that did that kind of work was the one whose master's name Caleth had watched an elf write down and underline twice, four months ago, with his knuckles going white on the edge of a table.

He had a great deal more to say. He had perhaps six more questions, and every one of them was a good question, and asking them would take the better part of a quarter hour and produce information.

He discovered he could not ask any of them.

He turned and walked out of the command room.

He was aware, distantly, of the quality of the quiet he left behind him — the particular quiet of three people looking at a man's back and then looking at each other. He was aware that Nageru held out both hands in the small flat gesture that meant *I will go*, and that nobody argued.

He did not slow down.

---

Aravashnial's study was on the second floor of the west range, in a room that had been a steward's office before Drezen fell and had been a place where demons kept nothing at all for seventy-five years afterward, and had been, for something under four months, the untidiest room in the citadel.

Caleth had been in it perhaps forty times. He knew it the way you know a room you have stood in without being invited to sit down.

He knew that the stacks on the long table were not stacks; they were an argument, laid out left to right, and the elf could put a hand into the middle of the third one without looking and come out with what he wanted. He knew that the chair was pulled out at an angle because Aravashnial did not sit in it so much as lean over it. He knew there was a cold cup on the sill with a skin on it, because there was always a cold cup on the sill with a skin on it, and that once — once — he had noticed Caleth noticing it and had said, with genuine irritation, *I drink it, I simply do not drink it promptly.*

Caleth went through the door and did not look at the cup, and began pulling the argument apart with both hands.

He heard Nageru come in behind him. He did not look up.

Paper. Ledger. Paper. A survey with soft brown edges. A page in the elf's hand, that dense sloped script that ran downhill across a page as if the writer were leaning into a wind. Notes on the citadel's wards. Notes on the eastern bridge. A list of names in a column with checks beside eleven of them and nothing beside the rest.

Not that. Not that. Not that.

Nageru worked the far end of the table. He did not ask what they were looking for. He had not asked anything since the command room, and would not, and it occurred to Caleth in a detached way that he had never once had to explain himself to Nageru in advance, and that he had gotten used to that without noticing, and that this was probably the definition of a friend.

A page came into his field of view, held out at the end of a bronze arm.

He took it and read it.

It was a full page, and most of it was other people's words.

Accounts. Four of them, perhaps five, copied out one beneath the other in that dense sloped hand, each headed with a place and a year and either a name or a description standing in for one. The first of them said only *a drover*, and named a river crossing, and left it at that. All of them were short, because not one had been written by anybody who stayed to watch. Caleth read them the way he read everything, which was fast, and they did not agree with one another about very much.

They agreed about three things.

Too many legs. The color was wrong for the breath. And every man who had seen one had reached, in his own words and out of his own vocabulary, for the same idea — that the thing had been *built.* *Wrongly put together,* said the drover. *Like a horse made out of two horses,* said somebody in Nerosyan, in a year Caleth's eye went past and then came back to, because it was further from here than he would have liked.

And then, at the foot of the page, under a ruled line, in the same hand and pressed harder into the paper:

*Woundworm. A dragon mutated by abyssal energy.*

Caleth read it again, in case it improved.

That was the whole of the conclusion. A page of frightened testimony gathered across decades and three hundred miles, copied out and set in order — and one line of verdict at the end of it, of the kind a scholar writes when he has gone precisely as far as his sources will carry him and will not take one step past that on nothing.

Which, in a man Caleth had watched refuse to guess about a wand in front of witnesses, was not a failure. It was the most honest sentence on the page.

It was also useless.

"That's it?"

Nageru nodded.

They went back to searching.

He found two more mentions in the next quarter hour, both of them marginal, both of them the kind of note a scholar makes to himself in the certainty that he will remember the rest. *W-worm — Sarkoris survivals? cf. the Kenabres bestiary, ii.* And, in a different ink and a harder hand: *not a breed. A result.*

*A result.* Something had been the cause. Something had put its hands on a living dragon and come away with a thing that had six legs and the wrong breath, and had then sent it four hundred miles to a citadel with a banner on it and told it to keep going for the courtyard until somebody came out to stop it.

He was reaching for the next page when his hand went through the stack and came out with a sheet that did not match.

It did not match because the others were working notes and this was a fair copy. The lines were even. The hand had slowed down. Somebody had sat, and formed the letters, and meant it to be read.

XANTHIR VANG

Blackfire Adept.

Servant of Deskari.

Leader of the Templars of the Ivory Labyrinth.

Responsible for the destruction of the Blackwing Library. He somehow commanded the demons that attacked it.

Records place him in the Third Crusade, then still a man of flesh and blood. That was over seventy years ago.

Operates from the Ivory Sanctum.

Connected to the distribution of Nahyndrian elixirs.

Ivory Sanctum location: unknown.

VANG IS EXTREMELY DANGEROUS.

Caleth read it the way he read everything, which was fast, and had already begun to file it — *a dossier, prepared for someone, eleven lines and no speculation, that is a briefing and briefings get handed across tables* — when his eye reached the bottom of the sheet and stopped.

There was a gap of three fingers' width below the last line.

And then three more lines, in the same hand, in a different ink.

*Caleth—*

*He is the shadow that wore a man's shape*
*but cast none of its own.*

*He is the thing that killed your parents.*

Caleth Aranor stood in a room whose owner had not come back to it, with a page in his hand, and discovered that the floor had been taken out from under a part of him that he had not known was standing on anything.

He let go of everything else. He heard the papers hit the boards, a soft sequence of slaps, one two three, and did not look down. He got the chair with the back of one leg and sat in it harder than he meant to.

The ink was different.

That was the thing his mind fastened on, because his mind would fasten on anything rather than the rest of it. The dossier was one ink and the three lines were another. Which meant Aravashnial had written the dossier first — written it out carefully, evenly, as a thing to be handed over — and then set it down. And then, at some other hour, on some other day, had picked it up again and written the three lines at the bottom and set it down again.

And never handed it over.

How long. How long had this been lying in the third stack from the left, under a survey and a bridge and a column of names, four flights and a courtyard away from where Caleth slept.

Weeks, was the answer. It could have been weeks.

*You had it. You wrote it down. You knew before you wrote it down, which means you knew when we were standing in the Blackwing with the ceiling on the floor. You knew on the road. You knew at the forge with your sleeves rolled up, with your hands on my sword, and I stood there at your elbow and did not ask, because I had decided that asking again would cost more than waiting.*

*I was so proud of the waiting.*

He looked at the middle line.

*He is the shadow that wore a man's shape but cast none of its own.*

And a cold went through him that had nothing to do with grief at all.

Because those were not Aravashnial's words. They were not any scholar's words. They had no business in a document that said *Ivory Sanctum location: unknown.*

They were the Lantern Seer's.

He could hear them in the old voice, in a back room in Kenabres that had smelled of dust and lamp oil and cats, spoken to a boy with green eyes who had been told three things and had carried all three of them for nineteen years in the place where he did not put anything else. *Three rivers in the dark, child.* A city burning under a bleeding sky — that had come at noon on Armasse. A blade wreathed in radiance — that was across his back right now, and it had a rag of his own tabard still knotted at the crossbar from Eagle Rock. And the third.

The third was a shadow that wore a man's shape and cast none of its own.

He had never said those words aloud.

Not to Korroc, who would have taken it seriously and told him it was a warning from somewhere good. Not to Thane, who would have wanted a description and a last known location. Not to Nageru. Not in a confession, not in a fever, not in the underdark when he had been certain he was going to die in it and had been briefly and unforgivably talkative about other things.

Not to Aravashnial.

He was as certain of that as he was of anything. A card revealed is a card spent; he had been counting his cards since he was eleven years old and he knew the number.

So the man had never been told, and had written it down anyway, word for word, and put it under a survey and a bridge and a column of names.

There were four or five ways that could be true. Caleth ran all of them in the space of about two seconds, because that was what he was for, and every one of them was a door into a corridor he had no light for, and at the end of every corridor was the same locked room: *ask him.*

He could not ask him.

That, in the end, was the shape of the whole afternoon. That was the shape of the last hour and of the next year. There had always been a conversation waiting at the end of all this — a real one, a long one, in a quiet room, the one where a Riftwarden finally set something down and said *now I will tell you about your mother and your father* — and Caleth had permitted himself, in the small hours, to plan it. Not to look forward to it. He did not look forward to things. He had simply established that it existed, the way a man on a long march establishes that there is a town at the far end.

The town had been carried off the top of a tower.

He sat with the page in both hands.

Somewhere on the edge of his attention Nageru had stopped searching and was standing at the end of the table, not close, not watching him exactly — watching the room he was in, the way a man stands with a horse that has been frightened.

Caleth heard himself speak, and it came out of him almost too quietly to be sound at all.

"Xanthir Vang must die."

It was not a vow. Caleth Aranor did not make vows in rooms; he had a precise and slightly contemptuous understanding of what vows made in rooms were worth. It was a finding. It was the last line of a calculation that had begun nineteen years ago with a word — *lost* — that he had never for one hour believed.

*Lost* implies chance.

He had known, at nine, at fourteen, at twenty, with an instinct he could never make anybody else respect: nobody was *lost.* Something ended them. And somewhere past the horizon of his own small life, that ending was still walking around.

Now it had a name, and a rank, and an employer, and a habit of sending mutilated dragons at fortresses, and it had taken the only other person alive who knew what Caleth's mother's face had looked like.

He folded the page once, along the gap between the two inks, and put it inside his coat.

Not in the spellbook. The spellbook held the blueprints, and the blueprints were Thane's and Korroc's and were a thing owed to other people.

He put it in the innermost pocket, where the small heavy vial already was, over his heart, and felt the two of them settle against each other — the one undo any of them would ever get, and the name of a man who was going to die.

Then he stood up, and his face did what it always did, which was nothing, and he walked out. Behind him, after a moment, Nageru followed.

---

They were where he had left them.

Korroc had not moved from the end of the table. Thane had; Thane had drifted to the wall by the door with his shoulder against the stone and his arms folded, which was where Thane went when a room had too many people in it and none of them were saying anything useful. Irabeth and Aron and Anevia had made a knot of three by the window and were talking low and fast about stretchers and masonry and where to put forty people whose barracks was under a tower.

Caleth stopped in the middle of the floor and waited the four seconds it took for Nageru to come up on his left, because the thing he was about to say should be said to all three of them at once and not to two of them and then a third.

"I think we need to rescue the five who were taken," he said.

Korroc shrugged. It was the smallest motion in the room, one gauntlet up and down, a shift of plate.

"Of course," he said.

And Caleth, who had walked the length of two corridors and a stair assembling an argument — *we have a name now; the tower is going to take a month either way; four soldiers and a scholar are worth the risk; and if none of that moves you, then hear this, which I will only say once* —

— had to stand there for a moment and put all of it away unused.

He did not trust his face for the length of one breath. So he nodded, and turned it into looking at the table.

"We can try to track it," he said. "Or I have a way that I think can put us directly on top of it. It isn't without risk."

Everyone nodded. Nobody asked what the risk was. He noted that too, and put it in the same place.

Thane came off the wall.

"Tracking a flying creature is near impossible," he said. "Even with magic. There's nothing to read. And it could be a long trip — days of long. Whatever it did after it left here, it didn't do it locally, or we'd have found five bodies in a field."

"No," Caleth agreed. "We would have."

He turned toward the window, where the three of them were talking, and walked over, and stood until Irabeth looked up.

"I need a place to cast a spell," he said. "It needs to be private."

She did not ask him what spell. She did not ask him whether it could wait, or what it would cost, or whether he had eaten anything since a valley thirty miles west.

She straightened off the sill, and turned her head, and said one word to the room in the voice she used perhaps six times a year, and the room emptied. Men with slates and men with tallies and a paladin with his arm in a sling went out of the doors in under twenty seconds without a single one of them asking why either.

Irabeth Tirabade turned back to him.

"What do you need?"

"A kettle of water," Caleth said. "And some time."

---

They brought in a kettle from the kitchens, a black iron thing with a scorched belly and a bail handle, and set it on the great table where the maps went, and filled it, and left.

Irabeth stayed. Aron stayed. Anevia stayed, and leaned against the shut door with her arms folded, hanging back at the edge of the room like the other two — three people making themselves small in a space they commanded, out of an instinct Caleth recognized and was obscurely grateful for.

Korroc and Thane and Nageru came in close and then stopped, all three at once, at about the same distance, as though a line had been chalked on the floor.

Caleth pushed his left sleeve up above the elbow.

"I'm looking for Aravashnial," he said softly. It was not an announcement. It was the way the thing was done — you said the name into the work, you gave the water a shape to be, and then you spent an hour making the water listen.

He began.

It was not a spell like the others were spells. A fireball was a decision; you built it and you let go of it and it went where you had thrown it, and the whole of it was over in a second and a half. This was labor. This was standing over a kettle with both hands on the table and building a bridge out of nothing toward a place you could not see, one length at a time, and every length of it had to be true or the whole span came down and you started again at the bank.

Twice it came down.

He built it again both times.

He was aware, in the long middle of it, of the room around him in fragments: the quality of the light going over from afternoon toward evening; the sound of men running in the corridor, which did not stop the whole time; Korroc sitting down on the edge of a bench at some point with his forearms on his knees; Thane not sitting down at all. At one point somebody came to the door and Anevia opened it four inches and said something short and closed it again.

*Stop noticing. You cannot afford the attention.*

He stopped noticing.

And then, the way it always happened — not gradually, never gradually — the surface of the water went from being water to being a window.

For a moment he could make nothing of it at all. It was dark, and the dark had texture, and the texture was moving.

Then the image found its feet.

A cavern. An enormous one, natural, unworked — rough stone going away and away into a blackness that had no far wall in it anywhere the eye could reach. Light came from torches, scattered, too few and badly spaced, their flames all leaning the same way and then leaning back. A draft. Something in that place was moving air, which meant it was very large or it was open to somewhere.

And a long way off, small, at the edge of what the water would give him, a slow pulse of purple light.

Caleth's whole chest closed.

He had seen that light once before, and he had stood eleven feet from it, and he had started walking toward it before he had finished deciding to, because the thing about a rift is that every second it is open is a second something is deciding whether to use it.

*Abyssal rift.* Aravashnial had said it that day in the Ahari, flat and certain and unsurprised, the way a man names a thing he has seen enough times to have stopped being impressed by it. *We need to close this,* Caleth had said, and had gone forward reading it, and a thing crossed of man and vulture had come out of it while he was still reading.

And then the elf had shut it. Not fast. Not easily. **Three castings**, one after another, while three vrocks tore at the four of them and the tear fought him the whole way and drew down grudgingly, inch by inch, until it finally snapped closed on a demon like a door on a hand.

That had taken everything a Riftwarden elder had, in a cave forty miles from home, with the whole company covering him.

There was a rift burning at the far end of this cavern.

And there was exactly one person in the world who could put it out, and he was standing in the middle of the frame with his hands tied behind his back.

Caleth made himself move past it. The spell had not settled on the purple and was not going to; it went where it had been told to go, and he had told it a name.

Then it settled.

Behind him somebody drew breath.

Aravashnial was standing.

His hands were bound behind his back. A strip of cloth had been hauled between his teeth and knotted hard enough to pull the corners of his mouth back. His coat was torn open along one side and there was blood dried down it in a fan, and blood in the white hair, and he was filthy in the specific way of a man who has been dragged over ground.

He was conscious. He was on his feet. He was standing the way he stood at a table.

Beside him were four crusaders with their hands bound in front of them. All four were hurt. One was leaning most of his weight against another, and the other was taking it without complaint, and neither of them was looking at anything.

*Alive,* Caleth thought, and something enormous and undignified went through him and was ruthlessly put down. *All five. Alive.*

Then he made himself count the armed men.

He got to twelve and lost the thread of it in the dark past the torches, and the twelve he had counted were enough to tell him what they were. Plate, mail, mismatched, well kept. A surcoat on one of them with a device he had learned to read in a cellar in Kenabres a year ago.

Templars.

"That's a dozen," Thane said, very quietly, behind his shoulder. "At least."

Then something moved behind the prisoners, and every head in the command room came up.

It was only a part of a shape, sliding through the edge of the torchlight and out again — a curve of something going past, at a height that meant nothing until the eye found a man standing beneath it and understood the proportion.

Korroc said something in Dwarven. Caleth understood every word of it, and privately agreed with all of it.

A woman's voice came out of the dark, from somewhere the water would not go.

*"What did she bring me?"*

One of the templars answered, in the flat carrying tone of a man giving a report he has rehearsed.

*"Four soldiers and a wizard."*

A silence. It went on just long enough to be a comment.

*"How disappointing,"* the woman said. *"I was hoping for at least one of the knights."*

Nobody in the command room moved.

*"Here is his spellbook,"* the templar said.

There was a rustle of pages. It was a small, dry, domestic sound, and it was the worst thing Caleth had heard all day, and he could not have explained to anyone in the room why.

Several seconds went by.

Then: *"Oh. Now this is interesting."*

Two templars stepped forward and took Aravashnial by the arms, one on each side, and pulled him clear of the other four.

Then one of them reached behind Aravashnial's head, worked the knot loose with his thumbs, and pulled the gag down off his mouth to hang around his neck.

*So she wants him able to answer,* Caleth thought. *She has not even looked at him yet and she has already decided he is worth talking to.*

Aravashnial worked his jaw once, and said nothing.

Footsteps. And then a woman came into the frame of the water.

She was an elf.

Caleth had never seen her before. He would have remembered. She was beautiful in the deliberate, constructed way of a portrait rather than a face — every proportion doing what it ought, the bones of her arranged with the sort of care that in Caleth's experience people did not get for free. She was dressed well. Not armored, not robed for work; *dressed*, in a place with no floor and no chairs.

And she was at ease.

That was the detail his eye kept coming back to, because his eye had been trained on iconography and posture from the day a woman in Kenabres had first put a brush in his hand. The armed men around her did not concern her. The vast thing shifting in the dark behind her did not concern her. She stood in the middle of twelve killers and a monster with her weight on one leg, the way a person stands in their own hall.

She was holding Aravashnial's spellbook open in one hand, and she was reading it while she walked.

She stopped in front of him.

*"Your name."*

Aravashnial looked at her.

When he answered, his voice was completely flat, and Caleth — who had heard that exact flatness applied to a student who had wasted his afternoon, and once to a Riftwarden who had wasted his life — felt his throat close.

*"You must be the famous Jerribeth I've heard about."*

"*Damn*," Thane said, on an exhale.

The woman studied him for a moment. Then she smiled, and raised the open book a little, and tapped one of the pages with a fingertip.

*"This notation isn't standard."*

She looked back up at him.

*"But I have seen it before."*

Aravashnial said nothing.

Her smile deepened.

*"A Riftwarden."*

She closed the book.

*"Take this one."*

The two templars tightened their grip. One of the others gestured at the four bound crusaders.

*"And them?"*

The woman did not take her eyes off Aravashnial.

*"Leave them."*

Then she turned away, and said, over her shoulder, in the tone of a woman telling a servant that the scraps could go to the dogs:

*"Scorizscar can have them."*

And for the first time in the whole vision, Aravashnial reacted.

Not to the binding. Not to the gag, or the dragging, or the woman going through his life's work with one finger. His head came around and his eyes went to the four soldiers, and everything that had been held off his face for the last minute arrived on it at once.

One templar caught him by the jaw. Another forced the gag back between his teeth and knotted it behind his head, short and hard.

The four soldiers had understood as well. One of them began to fight the ropes on his wrists in a way that was not going to accomplish anything. Another shouted — the water carried the shape of it but not the words.

Behind them, the enormous shape in the dark moved again.

Closer, this time.

They dragged Aravashnial away after the woman. He twisted once in their hands, trying to look back at the four men he was leaving.

Then he was out of the frame.

For a few seconds more the water tried to follow him, and gave Caleth stone, and torchlight, and one more glimpse of that far-off mauve.

Then darkness, and the surface of the kettle was the surface of a kettle, and Caleth was standing in a room in Drezen with his hands braced on a table and his heart going like a hammer.

He straightened up.

Behind him the room was completely silent. Irabeth had come off the wall. Aron had a hand over his mouth. Anevia's face had gone hard in a way Caleth had seen exactly twice.

*He looked at the soldiers.*

That was the thing that was going to stay. Not the cage of men, not the woman, not the shape in the dark. Of all the things in that cavern to be afraid of, the only one Aravashnial had been afraid of was what was going to happen to four crusaders whose names he almost certainly did not know.

*I have been in a room with that man perhaps two hundred times and I never once caught him being kind where anyone could see it.*

Caleth turned around.

"Ready to go?" he said.

They were all nodding before he finished the sentence.

---

It took three minutes, and they were the best three minutes of work the four of them had ever done together, and not one of them said a word that was not necessary.

Caleth laid the first thing on Nageru, because Nageru would be inside the thing's reach before any of them and would stay there — a hardening that went over the aasimar's skin and settled, and left the bronze looking faintly like something quarried.

Korroc came and put a hand flat on Caleth's breastbone and spoke over it in Dwarven, low and fast, and the link between them went from a thread to a cable. Caleth felt it take. He had felt it before and had never gotten used to it: the sensation of a door being opened in your own body that you had not opened, with a dwarf standing on the other side of it saying *give it here.*

"Half," Korroc said.

"I know what it is."

"I know you know. I said it for me."

Caleth cast the ward against acid next, over all of them at once — and made himself take the extra three seconds to widen it properly rather than laying it fast and thin, because *you had the half-second and you spent it being fast* was a sentence he had written about himself in a place where he could not stop reading it.

Thane put Alseta's blessing over the group with his eyes shut and his father's knife held upright in his fist.

He did not announce whose it was, and did not need to. In a year of this nobody had ever sat down and gone around the fire naming their gods, and all three of the others could have answered for one another without stopping to think. The Welcomer got no ceremony out of Thane Stonevein. Caleth had worked out a long time ago that this was the devotion and not the absence of one.

It came down over the four of them like a hand laid flat against a door.

Then he opened his eyes and laid a second working over Caleth alone, a closing-up, a shoring — the doorkeeper's instinct, applied to a man's ribs.

*And they could all three answer for one another,* Caleth thought, *and I am not certain what any of them would say about me, and I have never once asked.*

"You'll be the one she wants," Thane said.

"Yes," Caleth said. "I imagine I will be."

Korroc turned and did the same thing to Thane that he had done to Caleth, and Thane suffered it with the expression of a man being fitted for a coat he had not asked for.

Nageru simply stood for a moment with his hands at his sides, and then was not standing the same way. Caleth could not have said what changed. Something arrived in him, and the floorboards under the aasimar's bare feet did not creak differently, and everything else about the room did.

And then Irabeth Tirabade walked forward and put four swords into Caleth's arms.

He looked down at them. Four plain arming swords, garrison issue, good steel, nothing on them.

"Their hands are tied," Irabeth said. "Somebody has to cut them loose, and then they're four unarmed men in a cave."

Caleth looked at the swords, and then at her.

He had run that engagement eleven times on the way up from the kettle. He had accounted for a dragon, a spellcaster, twelve armed men, a ceiling of unknown height, an unknown draft source, and an open rift at the far end of it.

He had not thought about the swords.

"Thank you," he said.

"Go and get him," said Irabeth.

Caleth shifted the bundle into the crook of his left arm, took Radiance off his back with his right, and folded the world.

---

It opened again in the dark, and the dark smelled of wet rock and old fire and something underneath both that Caleth's body recognized before his mind did and disliked intensely.

Torchlight. A floor of broken stone going away in every direction. The draft, moving across his face from the left, cold.

He had put them down precisely where he had been standing in the water, which is to say in the exact middle of it.

*Everyone in the room at once. That is the trade. Take it.*

The four swords went out of his arm and onto the stone in a clatter he had not been able to avoid, and he had a dagger out of his wrist-sheath and into the nearest bound soldier's hands before the man had finished flinching.

"Cut yourself free," Caleth said.

Then he let go of the spell he had been carrying since the command room, and it went out over all of them like a held breath, and the world slowed down by about a fifth.

Korroc came around on his heel with his shield already up.

"Everyone's present," he said.

He was right. The templars — Caleth counted eleven in the torchlight and did not bother to look for the twelfth, because it did not change anything. The elven woman, thirty feet away and turning, her face going through surprise on the way to something else. And beyond her, coming up off the stone into the light the way a barge comes up out of fog, red and enormous and wrong.

Six legs.

And away down the long axis of the cavern, where he had spent an hour looking at a slow purple pulse in a kettle of water, there was nothing at all.

His eye went to it before he told it to, the way the eye goes to a doorway it has memorized, and found unbroken dark where the color had been — not dimmed, not screened, *gone* — and he had about a quarter of a second to feel the floor move under that before the world took the thought away from him and did not give it back for some time.

Caleth saw it entire for the first time, and the artist in him, who never shut up and never had the decency to be quiet at a useful moment, made its report: *the proportions have been forced. That is not a build. That is a repair by somebody who had never seen the original.*

Aravashnial was ten feet from the woman, on his knees, roped — not tied now but *roped*, wound at the elbows and the wrists both — and he had already begun to fight it, and he was not going to get out of it.

His head came up and he saw them.

Caleth would think about that later, for some time. The elf's face, in a cave, with his life's work in somebody else's hand, seeing four idiots arrive in the middle of a dozen swords.

Then Nageru moved.

He took one step. It did not look fast. It never looked fast. Something about the way Nageru crossed distance made the distance seem like it had been a courtesy in the first place — and then his fists came into the nearest templar's chest, once, twice, with a sound like a mallet on a side of beef, and the man went down without having gotten his sword off his shoulder.

Nageru turned on his heel and hit the next one twice, and the second one folded on top of the first.

Four seconds. Two men.

Then the dragon roared, and the whole cavern leaned.

It came around and its head dropped and its jaw opened and Caleth was already moving, throwing himself into the roll he had been practicing since a valley thirty miles west of Drezen, and the acid came out of it in a sheet.

Thane went one way. Nageru went the other, flat, under it.

Caleth got most of the way clear and the trailing edge of it came across his left side, and the ward he had spent the extra three seconds on took the worst of it and let the rest through, and the rest was enough to make him bite down.

Korroc did not move at all.

Korroc had planted his feet with four soldiers behind him and had made the arithmetic in about a third of a second, and the acid hit him square and hung on him and *ate*, and the sound he made was not a scream, it was the noise of a man setting down something heavy.

Behind him, two of the four crusaders got clipped by the edge of it anyway. One of them grabbed his comrade under the arms and hauled him backward out of the open, behind a spur of rock, leaving a smear.

And thirty feet away, the elven woman's eyebrows went up.

*"Oh,"* she said, delighted. *"That was easy. The knights did come."*

She looked over the four of them the way a buyer looks over stock.

*"I want one alive."*

And then she did nothing at all — simply stood there, in the middle of a fight, with her hands empty and her weight on one leg — and Caleth understood with perfect clarity that she was letting other people find out what the four of them were made of before she spent anything of her own on it.

*She is not in a hurry, and she should be. Why is she not in a hurry?*

Two templars broke for the dwarves, one at Thane, one at Korroc.

Thane went under the first cut without appearing to have noticed it was coming, took one step sideways that put his man's shoulder against a pillar of rock, and came back off the wall with both hands working. The templar got out about half a sound. Then Thane stepped clear of him and let him fall.

Korroc caught the first sword on the shield and the second went in over the rim, inside his guard, and grated on something.

He reached down through his own boots into the floor of the cavern.

Caleth had watched him do it perhaps thirty times and had never once been able to see it happen. There was no light and no sound. There was simply a moment when Korroc Stonevein was a dwarf standing on stone and the next moment when he was a part of the stone that happened to be standing up, and nothing in the world was going to move him off that square of ground.

Then the dwarf put a hand over his own breastplate and hauled — and Caleth felt the cable between them go hot for a half second — and Korroc came up out of it whole. Not patched. Whole. Acid-scoured plate and a ruined surcoat and no wound under any of it.

He had spent something deep to do it. Caleth saw the cost go across his face and vanish.

"Fine," Korroc said, to nobody. "That's fine."

One of the templars in the second rank lifted both hands and let go of something that was not light.

It came out over all of them in a slow spreading wave, and it felt — Caleth would have no better description for it afterward — like being looked at by a room. It went through him, and through Nageru, and through the dwarves, and through the four crusaders on the floor, and every one of them shook it off.

It still touched them. That was the nature of the thing. You could refuse it and still be marked by having been offered it.

Korroc grunted as though he had been shoved in the back. Half of Caleth's, half of Thane's, all of his own, arriving at once through a door he had opened on purpose.

Then the dragon came for Caleth.

It crossed twenty feet in a single surge, and it did not bother with the claws; the head came down and the jaws opened and closed and Caleth saw, from thirty feet away and slightly to the left, his own body come apart into nothing in the middle of that mouth.

The mythic trick. The thing he had learned to do below the Gray Garrison and had never once been able to explain to Thane. You put a *you* where you were, and then you were not there, and for one second the world was wrong about you.

The dragon's head came up with nothing in it, and turned, and found Korroc.

The tail came around first and took the dwarf across the side and made a noise on the plate like a bell in a sack. Then both wings came down on him at once, the leading edges hammering.

Then the claws.

Six of them. Six, because it had six legs, and it had six legs because something had done that to it, and Caleth watched the geometry of it happen and it was like watching a man try to block rain.

Korroc got the shield onto three. Three.

The other three went in.

He stayed standing because he was rooted in the floor of the world and because he had decided to, and blood came out from under the Armor of the Pious in three places and ran down over Torag's hammer and anvil where a sunburst used to be.

A templar arrived on Caleth's flank.

He caught the first cut on Radiance's haft — the shock of it went up both arms — and slipped the second and did not slip it far enough, and steel opened his shoulder through the tabard.

*Enough.*

He took one step.

Not toward the templar. The templar was a soldier doing a job and could be killed in a minute by anyone. Caleth took a step to his left, and the step put him inside the reach of the elven woman, who was still standing there with her weight on one leg, amused.

Holy fire ran up the shaft of Radiance and out along the leaf of the blade, white and sudden, and lit the whole cavern floor for thirty feet.

Her features blurred.

It was beautifully done. Caleth had time to admire it even as he was moving: the outline of her went soft at the edges and doubled and slid a handspan out of true, so that the woman his eye insisted was there was not the woman who was there.

He had fenced against that before. You do not aim at what you see. You aim at where the floor says the weight is.

Radiance went into her.

And it *bit.*

That was the only word for what came back up the haft. The blade did not merely go in; it took hold, the way it had taken hold of a vrock at the Ahari and a succubus in a treasury and a thing wearing a saint's face. The holy fire on it did not wash over her — it went *through*, and found purchase, and burned.

Blood hit the stone.

"*She's a demon*," Caleth said, loud, over his shoulder, in the flat carrying voice of a man reading out a number. "Radiance has her. She is a demon."

"Of course she is," said Thane, from somewhere behind him, killing something.

And Caleth — with the blade in her and holy fire going down it — let go of the thing he had been carrying in the steel since a forge floor in a half-ruined fortress.

He had kept it loaded for months, the way you load a crossbow you do not expect to fire. He had touched the flat of this blade to a barbarian's shoulder in a windowless hall and deliberately let it pass, because the offer had been the point. He had never spent one. Not once.

He spent it now.

The working came out of Radiance the way he had built it — lightning turned inside out on the way, the old Exploiter's inversion he had done so often it was nearer a reflex than a decision, so that what should have arrived as a white crack of current arrived instead as a killing cold, hard enough to frost the blood on the blade.

It hit her full, at a distance of no distance at all.

Nothing happened.

Her expression did not change. Her posture did not change. The cold rolled off her and spent itself on the air and was simply, comprehensively, *not relevant*, and Caleth Aranor — who had spent four months keeping a spell in a glaive against the day he would need it most — got to watch the day arrive and the spell do nothing.

*Noted,* he thought, with a savagery that surprised him. *Cold is not the road.*

He tore Radiance out and put it back in before she could recover the distance, and the second one was as good as the first, and she made a sound this time.

The third, she was not there for. She moved a handspan and a half — an elegant, economical, *practiced* movement — and the leaf of the glaive went past her ribs and through the fold of a very expensive sleeve.

She was bleeding freely now, down one side, running to the wrist. She was still standing.

Across the cavern, Nageru left the templar he had been about to kill.

Caleth saw it out of the corner of his eye and understood it instantly. The aasimar simply turned his back on a live man with a sword and walked away from him toward the dragon that was standing on Korroc, and there was an expression on his face that Caleth had seen on him exactly once before, in a mausoleum, and had hoped not to see again.

The dragon saw him coming and swept the tail at him.

Nageru went under it — not over, *under*, dropping into the roll at the last possible instant with the thing passing a handspan above his back — and came up out of the roll already swinging.

He pulled from the deep well of his order, the patient thing he had been filling since he was a boy, and from the other thing, the newer thing, that none of them ever spoke about.

The strike landed on the dragon's shoulder and the whole animal staggered sideways.

It forgot Korroc entirely.

The bite came in low and hard and found Nageru and shook him. The wings came over and missed. The tail caught him across the hip.

And then the claws, and again the awful arithmetic of six: Nageru read three of them out of the air and was simply not where any of the three arrived, and the other three found him, and one of them opened him from collarbone to sternum.

He did not go down.

The elven woman lifted one bloody hand.

Caleth had half a second to recognize the shape of the casting and no time at all to do anything about it, and then the space that held him and the roped, kneeling Aravashnial detonated into color.

There is no describing it honestly. It was not light. It was every color there was, arriving all at once and none of them agreeing about where anything was, ricocheting off the stone and off each other and off the inside of his skull, and for about a second and a half Caleth had no body and no floor and no opinions.

He got a hand up. He got the discipline in front of it — the old cold trick of *I am a man standing on stone, and this is happening to my eyes, and my eyes are not the argument* — and most of it broke on that.

Most.

Down the cable, Korroc took half of what got through, and Caleth heard him swear.

And ten feet away, Aravashnial's eyes rolled up in his head and he went over sideways onto the stone and did not move.

"*No* —"

The woman's hand was already moving again.

She did not cast at the dragon fight. She did not cast at the dwarves. She cast at Caleth, precisely, with the air of a woman correcting an error in a ledger.

And the floor let go of him.

Down became up between one heartbeat and the next. Caleth's stomach went one way and the rest of him went the other, and the world rotated, and he fell thirty-five feet into the roof of the cavern and hit it flat on his back with his arms out, and lay there against the stone with the whole of the fight spread out below him like a diagram.

He could not get down.

That was the first and most important fact and his mind produced it immediately and without mercy. He was on the ceiling of a cave, pinned by his own weight going the wrong direction, thirty-five feet of empty air between him and the floor, with Radiance in one hand and every spell he had ever learned in his head and *no way to make any of it matter for four seconds.*

He could see everything.

He could see Thane put two strikes into a templar and drop him and turn and put two more into another. He could see Korroc, bleeding from six places, most of them nobody's wounds, take his hand off his shield long enough to fix himself again and then speak a word over his own breastplate — and the Armor of the Pious answered him, the way it had answered him since it decided whose it was, and something settled over the dwarf that Caleth's sight registered as a thickening of the air along every surface of him.

He could see Nageru, opened to the bone, twist aside from a templar's cut while the dragon came back at him. He could see the bite miss. He could see the wing land. He could see the aasimar read four claws out of six and get clipped by the fifth and taken hard by the sixth.

And he could see, at the edge of it all, a templar walk unhurriedly to where Aravashnial lay unconscious on the stone, and crouch, and put one hand on his shoulder.

"*NO!*"

Caleth heard his own voice come out of him at a pitch he did not recognize, from the ceiling of a cave, and it did not matter at all, because the templar and the elf were already gone.

There was not even a sound. There was a man crouching with his hand on a shoulder, and then there was bare stone with a coil of rope on it.

Two more templars in the second rank folded their hands through the same figure and were also gone.

Caleth lay on the roof of the world with his heart going and looked down at the empty floor where the only person alive who knew his mother's face had been lying eleven seconds ago.

He had crossed four hundred miles in a step. He had put four fighting men into the exact middle of a cavern he had seen for eight seconds in a kettle. He had planned for a dragon and twelve men and an unknown ceiling and he had planned correctly, and the plan had worked, and he had been thirty-five feet away with a blade in his hand and full knowledge of exactly what was happening.

*And knowing wasn't enough.*

He did not let himself finish the thought. He never let himself finish that thought. He put it into the ledger and he folded the world.

The cavern did that small cool nothing it always did — the half-second of being nowhere at all — and his feet came down on the stone floor behind the elven woman, and gravity remembered him, and he was already moving when he arrived.

She was fast. She was very fast. She was not fast enough for a man who had arrived from behind her with the whole thrust already begun before he existed at that spot.

Radiance took her under the shoulder blade.

The holy fire came up the blade and out of it and she *screamed* — a real one, torn out of her, nothing constructed about it at all — and her knees went, and she collapsed.

And vanished, eight inches above the stone, before she hit it.

Caleth stood over the place where she had not landed with the blade still out and burning and blood running off the leaf of it onto the rock, and said something in Elven that his foster mother would have washed his mouth for.

*Contingency.* Laid in advance, keyed to the body going down, to carry her somewhere else the instant the fight was decided against her. He had read about them. He had considered building one, years ago, and had decided the cost was disproportionate.

The cost was not disproportionate. He would be revising that.

Then Nageru killed the dragon.

Caleth turned in time to see the end of it, and afterward he was glad he had, because it was one of the more frightening things he had ever watched a friend do.

The aasimar had gone somewhere. Not physically — he had not moved more than eight feet in a minute — but the man inside the body had reached down past the patient thing and past the newer thing and had got hold of something that lived under both of them, and his fists were sheathed in a pale flame that did not flicker.

He hit it five times.

Not a flurry. Five separate decisions, each one complete, each one landing in the same place on the ruined red hide, and between the second and the third the animal began to try to get away from him and discovered that it could not, because Nageru was inside its reach and its reach was the only weapon it had ever needed.

Blood came off it in sheets and slapped the stone.

On the fifth, the legs — all six of them — stopped being legs and became six things that happened to be attached, and the Woundworm went down on its chest with a boom that Caleth felt through his boots, and its head came down last, and its eye went flat.

A templar stepped in and put a sword into Nageru's back while he was still standing over it.

Nageru's whole front ran red.

Thane killed a man and turned and stabbed another one and did not stop moving.

And Korroc let go of the floor of the world.

Caleth saw the dwarf come unrooted — saw the shoulders sag as the stone gave him back to himself, saw the gray bone-deep fatigue land on him all at once like a dropped cloak — and saw him take one step toward Nageru anyway, and put his own hand on his own chest, and burn the fatigue off with a mercy he had been given for other people.

Then Korroc reached Nageru and put both hands on him, and the ruin closed.

"That's *enough* of that," Korroc said.

After that it was work.

A templar came at Caleth from the right and Caleth turned the cut on the haft and came back over the top of it in the same motion, Radiance sweeping across the man's chest and opening the surcoat and everything under it — and the man's face did the thing Caleth had seen a dozen times and had never grown comfortable with, the pure astonishment of a professional who has just discovered that the scholar is not the easy one.

Caleth stepped back, and hit him three times, and that was that.

Nageru hit his man twice and the man went down. Thane went through two more the way a man goes through a hedge he is annoyed with.

The last one was near Caleth, backing up, and had stopped being a soldier some seconds ago and had become a person in a cave with four knights in it. He got his sword up.

Caleth put Radiance through his chest, once, and he sat down and stopped.

The cavern was quiet except for the draft.

Korroc was already walking, letting the healing go out of him in long slow waves that washed over all of them and did not stop at the four of them — it went past, and reached the four crusaders behind their spur of rock, and one of them made a noise like a man surfacing.

Then the dwarf came to each of them in turn: Caleth, and Nageru, and himself last, which Caleth noticed and filed and did not mention, because it was the eleventh time and he had stopped mentioning it around the fourth.

---

There were four soldiers alive on the floor of a cave four hundred miles from anywhere, and a dead dragon, and eleven dead templars, and no Aravashnial.

Caleth got the crusaders on their feet. Two could walk. One could walk with help. The fourth could not, and Korroc put a hand on him until he could.

They were young. That was the thing his eye insisted on telling him, uselessly. Two of them were under twenty-five and one of them had a Kenabres accent, and all four of them were looking at the four knights who had come out of thin air with the specific expression of men who had spent a day and a night arriving at a decision about their own deaths and were now being asked to take it back.

"You're going home," Caleth told them. "Stand close. Closer than that."

He sent them.

That was the harder way to do it and he did it anyway, because the easy way was to go with them and the easy way put him in a courtyard in Drezen with his friends four hundred miles behind him in a cave. So he built it to go without him — took the four of them in one hand, so to speak, and put them down in the courtyard of Citadel Drezen in front of a great many extremely startled people, and stayed exactly where he was.

It cost him considerably more than carrying them would have. He stood for a moment afterward with one hand on a rock, waiting for the floor to stop being interesting.

Then he turned around. The other three had already spread out and started working.

"Go," he said. "I'll take the water."

He had not slept. He had not eaten since a camp thirty miles west of Drezen, some indeterminate number of hours ago. It did not seem relevant. He found a flat rock and sat down on it, and pulled out a flask and emptied it into a fold of rock that would hold it.

He could not look for Aravashnial. He had already spent him — you get one reach at a given soul in a day and the water will not be argued with about it, and he had used his that afternoon on a cavern with a dozen templars in it, which had been the correct use and was no comfort at all now.

So he went after the templar instead.

The one who had walked over and crouched and put a hand on Aravashnial's shoulder. Caleth had watched him do it from the ceiling of this cave with nothing in his hands but a glaive he could not reach anybody with, and he had spent the intervening hour discovering that he could not produce the man's face.

That was the difficulty, and he knew it going in. You do not reach for a soul by pointing at a place; you reach for it by *knowing* it, and the more of a person you have, the shorter the bridge. He had known Aravashnial for a year. He had known that templar for four seconds, upside down, at thirty-five feet, in torchlight, from behind.

He built it anyway, one true length at a time, out over a gap he could not see the other side of.

The other three searched the cavern.

It took them the better part of an hour, and he was aware of them at the edges of it — Thane's lamp going away and coming back, Korroc's voice at a distance, once, saying something with satisfaction in it.

Between the second attempt and the third, he lifted his head and looked down the long axis of the cavern again.

There was nothing there.

He got up off his rock and walked out past the torches until the dark was complete and his own light was the only light in the world, and stood, and gave it a long time. He let his eyes go. He looked slightly to one side of where it had been, the way you do for a faint star.

Nothing. Not a glow, not a bloom on the rock, not a seam. Stone going away into stone.

He had watched it for the better part of an hour that afternoon while a bridge of his own making held on by its fingernails. It had pulsed the entire time, as steady as a heartbeat, and he had known what it was on sight, and had spent two seconds of a spell he could not afford refusing to look at it.

It had been there. Now it was not.

He stood in the dark and worked it, because working it was the only thing he had left to do with it.

A rift does not go out. That was the whole difficulty. A rift is not a fire and not a lamp; it is a place where the world has been holed, and holes do not close because the evening has gotten on. Aravashnial had spent three castings and every bit of what he was on one in the Ahari, and it had fought him the entire way down.

And Caleth had no idea whatsoever how large this one was. He had seen it from the far end of a cavern that had no far wall in it, which told him precisely one thing, which was that it had been bright enough to see from there.

So either something had closed it — which meant there was a second person in this business who could do what Aravashnial could do, and Caleth would very much like to know who — or something had come through and shut it behind itself, the way you shut a door you are finished with.

Or it had never been in one place at all, and *that* was a thought he found he could not follow to the end of on four hours of sleep, in a cave, with his hands still shaking.

Three worlds. No way at all to learn which one he was standing in.

*Four injured men. Three exhausted friends. And nothing between here and it but a mile of broken stone in the dark.*

He stood in the dark a little longer than the arithmetic justified.

Then he went back to the rock and wrote it into the ledger, in the long column of things he could do nothing about, which now ran to several pages, and began building the bridge again.

When he stood up at last, the three of them were over by the dead dragon with a cache open between them — coin, and a scatter of small goods, and something long wrapped in oilcloth — the way even the best men in the world will end up standing over a hoard.

They looked up as he came.

He shook his head.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Nothing?" said Korroc.

"Nothing." Caleth heard how it came out and did not try to fix it. "I don't have enough of him. That is all it is. I never saw his face — I have a man crouching in bad light and the back of a head and one hand on a shoulder, and you cannot find a soul with that. The spell goes out over the edge and there is nothing on the other side to catch on."

He stopped, because the next sentence was going to be about whose fault that was, and he had heard himself give that speech before and knew exactly how useful it was.

"Nobody got his face," said Thane.

He said it flatly, with no comfort anywhere in it, which was the only reason it worked as comfort.

"I've been at it since the fight ended," Thane said. "I do faces. It's most of what I am. He was one of twelve, he did the only thing in that room that mattered, and I could not pick him out of a line of three." He turned a coin over and put it in the sack. "So it isn't the spell and it isn't you. There wasn't anything to have."

Caleth nodded. He looked down at the cache and did not see it.

"I could try the woman," he said. "Do we want to try it?"

Korroc shrugged, and the shrug was tired.

"She'll be hard to find," he said. "But I don't know where else we go."

"No," said Caleth. "Neither do I."

He had already turned away when Thane's voice came from behind him, flat and unhurried, in the tone of a man who has noticed a thing on a floor.

"You still have her blood on your blade?"

Caleth stopped.

He looked down at Radiance. He had not wiped it. In a year of walking into the dark he had wiped that blade after every single kill, because the sword had belonged to Yaniel and Yaniel had died at these gates and there were standards.

He had not wiped it, because he had been standing over the place where a demon had failed to hit the floor, swearing in Elven.

The blood on the leaf had gone dark and tacky and was still, unmistakably, hers.

"I do," he said.

He looked at it a moment longer.

"That will help," he said. "That will help a great deal."

Thane nodded once, and went back to counting coins, and did not make anything of it, which was the entire and considerable art of him.

It took another hour.

At the end of the hour Caleth came back to them shaking his head again.

"Nothing," he said. "She's gone too. Or she's behind the same wall he is." He rubbed his face with both hands, which was a thing he did not do in front of people. "Let's go home."

"Right," said Thane. "I know where we are, by the way."

Caleth turned around.

Thane was standing at the mouth of a side passage with his lamp held low, looking out at something — and Caleth went and stood beside him, and saw.

The cavern opened. Fifty yards on, the roof fell away into a long split in the rock, and past it the world outside was the color of a banked coal.

It was night out there, and it was not dark. A long way below and away, a river of something that had no business being a river ran from the north-east to the south-west across the whole visible width of the country, and it was not water. It was moving, slowly, and it was the dull orange of a forge that has been shut up for the evening, and where it touched the banks there was a rind of black crust with cracks of light in it, and the air above it went up in columns that bent.

"The Molten Scar," said Thane. "That's it. That's the Molten Scar or I'm a human."

"You're sure."

"I'm sure of the Scar. And if that's the Scar, then that —" the dwarf lifted the lamp and pointed with his chin at a long pale line running along the near bank, straight in the way that only two things in the world are straight, "— is the Gray Road. Which used to be a river. One of the big ones, before all this. Now there's more lava in the bed than there ever was water, and there's a track along the top of the old levee that the caravans used, back when there were caravans."

He lowered the lamp.

"Four days from Drezen," he said. "On foot. If nothing eats you."

Caleth stood at the mouth of the cave and looked out over a river of fire in the middle of a country that had been farmland once, and did the sum.

Four days. Four days of walking, each way, along an open levee under an orange sky, through a stretch of the Wounded Lands no crusader had set foot in for seventy-five years. That was the distance from this floor, where Aravashnial had been kneeling, to the nearest door he could call home.

That was how far a wounded dragon had carried five men in a night.

"All the more reason to teleport back to Drezen," he said.

"I was going to suggest it," said Thane.

They gathered the cache and the four swords and the pieces of eleven dead men's business, and stood close, and Caleth folded the world one more time, and the light of the Molten Scar went out behind them.

---

They reported to Irabeth.

It took twenty minutes and Caleth gave it to her cleanly, in order, with the cavern's dimensions and the templars' count and the woman's manner of speech, and left out nothing whatsoever except one folded page in an inner pocket.

At the end of it she said, "The four are in the infirmary. Two of them are going to be fine by the end of the week."

"And the other two?"

"Also fine. Just slower." Irabeth looked at the four of them — the acid-scoured plate, the blood dried in Nageru's collar, the way Korroc was standing. "You got four men back from a place nobody could find."

"Yes," said Caleth.

"Say it like it's worth something."

"It is worth something." He heard how flat it came out. "It is worth four men. I am aware of the arithmetic, Commander. I am simply aware of the rest of it too."

Irabeth let that go, which was a kindness.

"The water won't give you the same man twice in a day," she said. It was not a question; she had commanded wizards for a long time.

"No. One reach at a soul, and then it has to settle." Caleth looked at the kettle, which somebody had taken off the table and set on the floor by the wall, and which had been sitting there cooling for six hours while a dragon died. "I have spent three today and none of them on anyone I could afford to spend. Him, this afternoon. Then a templar I had never laid eyes on, and then her." He heard the tally come out of his mouth and disliked it. "Aravashnial again in the morning. That is the whole of what I have."

"Then go to sleep."

He went to his room and lay on the bed in his clothes with Radiance across the chair and did not sleep for a long time, and when he did, he dreamed about nothing at all, which was worse.

---

In the morning they came in without being asked.

Caleth had expected to do it alone. He had come down before dawn precisely so that he could do it alone, and had found Korroc already sitting on the bench along the wall with his hands between his knees, and Thane in the corner where the two walls met, and Nageru barefoot by the door.

Nobody explained themselves. Caleth discovered he did not want them to.

Irabeth came, and Anevia, and Aron, and stood along the back the way they had before.

He filled the kettle. He pushed his sleeve up. He put both hands on the table.

"Aravashnial," he said.

And began.

It took longer this time. He did not know why and he made himself not care why, and he built the bridge four times and lost it twice, and the second collapse came near enough to the far bank that it put him on the edge of the table with his jaw clenched.

The fifth time, it took.

He felt it hook, and hold, and go taut — *found* — and the relief went through him so hard it nearly broke his concentration.

Then the water cleared, and there was nothing in it.

Not darkness as an image. Darkness as a *fact*. No torch, no candle, no grate, no seam of light under a door, nothing by which the eye could construct even the shape of a room.

"Is it working?" someone whispered, behind him.

"It's working," said Caleth.

Because it was. He could feel the spell holding, patient and true, exactly where it had been told to be. It had found him. There was simply nothing there to see.

They waited.

Seconds went past, and then more of them. The absolute black sat in the kettle and did not change.

And then, out of it:

*clink.*

Metal, shifting somewhere. A pause. Then again — *clink... scrape.* A weight moving over stone and a chain going with it.

Then breathing.

Slow. Deliberately slow. The breathing of a man who has worked out that panic costs more than it buys and has made a decision about it.

Caleth's eyes closed for one second.

*He's awake.*

They waited a long time.

There was nothing to see and the four of them watched it anyway, the way you watch a door. Occasionally the chain moved. Once there was a sound that might have been a cough.

Then, without any warning at all — *light.*

A door opened somewhere beyond him, and after the black it was like being struck. Caleth's whole body flinched back from the kettle. Behind him somebody swore.

It lasted perhaps two seconds.

In those two seconds they saw him.

Aravashnial was inside an iron cage.

The cage was small. That was the first thing, and it was the thing that went into Caleth like a nail, because his eye did what it always did and took the proportions before he could stop it: the bars, the floor, the height. The height was wrong. It was wrong by more than a foot.

He could not stand up in it. He was sitting half-slumped against the bars with one leg drawn up under him because there was not room to put it anywhere else, and his neck was bent where the top of the cage made him bend it.

His wrists were shackled.

His clothes were filthy and torn through in three places. The white hair hung loose around his face, out of its tie.

And one side of his face was swollen — the whole cheek and the eye above it, shut, the color already gone purple-black at the edge.

Somebody had hit him. Somebody had hit him hard, with a closed fist, more than once.

The door closed.

Black.

And Caleth stood in a room in Drezen with his hands flat on a table, and understood in a calm detached way that whoever had put a fist into that face was going to die badly and that he was going to be there.

Then, out of the darkness: fabric.

The soft continuous rustle of robes moving across a room.

They waited for footsteps.

There were no footsteps.

Caleth's skin crawled up the back of his neck in a way it had not done since a corridor below Drezen where he had walked toward a warded door and stopped four paces short of it like a man hitting a rope in the dark.

And underneath the fabric, there was another sound.

It was very faint. It was wet. It was many, many tiny movements happening at once and not quite in time with one another, and the mind refused to build a picture for it and kept trying anyway.

Then a voice came from somewhere beyond the bars.

It sounded male. It sounded educated. It sounded, very nearly, human.

*"So. I have a Riftwarden."*

Aravashnial did not answer.

The voice moved. It crossed the room — Caleth could hear it cross the room, could hear it change its bearing relative to the cage — and there were still no footsteps.

*"I admit, I believed I had been rather thorough."*

Silence.

*"Blackwing was such an unpleasant little place. So many books. So many secrets."* A quiet chuckle, warm and companionable. *"So many people convinced that knowledge made them dangerous."*

Behind Caleth, Korroc had stood up off the bench.

*"Destroying the library was simple enough,"* the voice said. *"Your organization proved somewhat more troublesome."*

And then Aravashnial spoke, quiet and controlled and absolutely dry.

*"You'll forgive me if I don't congratulate you."*

The unseen man laughed out loud, delighted.

*"Oh, good. Jerribeth suggested you might be entertaining."*

Thane made a small noise in the corner. Caleth did not turn around.

There was a sound of something being opened. Leather — a clasp, a cover laid back — and then pages.

And Aravashnial went completely silent.

Not quiet. *Silent.* The chain stopped. The breathing stopped being audible.

*"I've been reading,"* the man said.

A page turned.

*"You have an interesting hand."*

Another.

*"And a familiar mark."*

Nothing from the cage.

*"I've seen this arcane mark before."*

A pause. Then, almost gently:

*"I have another book in my collection bearing precisely the same mark."*

The faintest movement of chain. That was all. It was a fraction of a sound — a man shifting a wrist perhaps an inch.

The voice caught it.

*"Ah. You know which book I mean."*

*"No,"* said Aravashnial.

*"Don't you?"*

*"No."*

*"I've had it for years."* A page turned. *"I took it from another elf."*

Silence.

*"Another Riftwarden."*

And this time Aravashnial reacted, and they could not see it, and they heard it anyway: his breathing stopped. Just for a second. One held beat where there had been a rhythm.

Then his voice, very level:

*"What was his name?"*

The answer came back with a shrug in it.

*"I don't remember."* A pause. *"Should I?"*

Aravashnial did not answer.

*"One kills so many people."*

The chains pulled suddenly taut — a hard, fast, whole-body sound of a man coming up off the floor of a cage to the limit of what held him.

Then stopping.

The man gave a quiet laugh.

*"Ah. Rather determined fellow, as I recall."*

Nothing.

*"Very uncooperative. He provided remarkably little useful information."*

And then Aravashnial said, and Caleth could hear the whole of him in it, the same voice that had asked a question about a wand across a forge floor:

*"Where did you get the book?"*

*"You already know."*

*"Where?"*

There was a pause. It was not a hesitation; it was a man choosing the moment.

*"I killed him."*

Nothing came out of the cage.

No chain. No movement. Not even breathing.

And in a command room four hundred miles away, Caleth Aranor stood with both hands on a table and did not breathe either, because he had heard exactly what had gone into that silence, and he had never in his life heard Aravashnial make that sound, and the sound was no sound at all.

*"I remember him now,"* the man said. *"A little."*

Nothing.

*"He resisted."*

Nothing.

*"Much as you're doing now, actually."*

And Aravashnial's voice came through the dark, and it had almost nothing left in it.

*"Stop."*

The man did not stop.

*"I remember being disappointed by him."*

*"Stop."*

*"He knew remarkably little that was useful to me."*

The chains began to move.

*"He was stubborn."*

The chains pulled.

*"I suppose that runs in the family."*

The cage erupted.

It was a sound like a smith's shop falling downstairs — metal on metal on stone, a body thrown bodily against iron, again, and again, shackles hammering on bars, a chain snapping out to its limit and hauling back and snapping out again — and over all of it, for the first and only time since Caleth had known him, Aravashnial completely and entirely lost control.

***"YOU MURDERED HIM!"***

The kettle rang faintly on the table.

Nobody in the command room moved. Nobody in the command room breathed.

Silence came back.

And then the man laughed. Quietly. Warmly. With real pleasure.

*"There you are."*

Caleth's hands had closed on the edge of the table hard enough that he would find the marks in his palms an hour later.

*Family.*

He turned the word over in a mind that had gone very cold and very precise. *Family. Not an elder saying it of a junior. Not a Riftwarden speaking of an order. The thing he said before that was he was stubborn, and then he said it runs in the family, and a man who has never raised his voice in my hearing threw himself at a cage.*

In eleven months and four hundred conversations, Aravashnial had never once mentioned having anyone.

Caleth had assumed. He had assumed the way everyone assumes about elves and scholars and men who are rude about interruptions: that there had been people, once, a long time ago, and that they were a closed subject.

He had never asked.

*Neither of us ever asked the other one a single question that mattered. We were so careful. We were so extraordinarily careful with each other, for nearly a year, and we have both run out of time to stop being careful.*

In the dark, Aravashnial was breathing hard.

When he spoke again his voice was shaking, and it was not fear.

*"You should have made certain you killed all of us."*

And the unseen man sounded genuinely entertained.

*"Your rescuers?"*

Aravashnial went silent.

*"They were rather impressive."*

A pause. And then, in exactly the same easy conversational tone:

*"Especially the young half-elf."*

Caleth felt it go into him like cold water.

And in the cage, Aravashnial stopped moving.

Completely. Instantly. Between one instant and the next, every sound in that cell ceased — no chain, no shift of weight, no breath. A man who had been fighting iron four seconds ago went absolutely and utterly still.

It was the loudest thing Caleth had ever heard.

*Oh no,* he thought. *Oh, don't. Don't do that. Breathe. Say something stupid. Say anything.*

*Don't tell him.*

The man noticed.

Of course he noticed. He had been listening to an inch of chain move and building a conversation out of it for ten minutes; he had not missed a single thing all night.

*"Interesting,"* he said.

Aravashnial said nothing.

*"Very interesting."*

And Caleth — who had spent his whole life watching people give themselves away, who had learned before he was ten that the tell is never the thing the person says — stood over a kettle of water and watched the man he was trying to save hand over the one piece of information he had been holding, by doing absolutely nothing at all, out of the purest and most protective instinct a person can have.

*He went still because it was about me.*

*And that is why it was worth something.*

Then the other sound came back. The one underneath the robes. Wet, soft, hundreds of tiny movements overlapping.

Closer, now. Much closer.

The chains moved suddenly. Something struck the cage, and Aravashnial grunted in pain.

Another impact. The shackles slammed against the bars — and it was wrong, it was all wrong, because there had been no footsteps and no voices and nobody had opened the door, and something in that room had just picked a grown man up off the floor and put him against the iron.

*"What is that?"* said Aravashnial.

The man's answer was perfectly calm.

*"A precaution."*

Metal clicked.

*"No,"* said Aravashnial. It came out differently than anything he had said all night. *"No."*

*"You're a conjurer."*

The chains rattled violently.

*"No —"*

*"A Riftwarden."*

*"No!"*

*"And therefore an exceptionally inconvenient prisoner."*

Another metallic click, close and small and final, like a lock on a box.

And then the voice came very close to him.

*"You may experience some discomfort."*

Aravashnial screamed.

And green light flooded the water.

It came up out of the kettle and filled the whole command room, and for one terrible second they could all see.

Aravashnial was pinned against the bars of the cage from the inside.

There were no men holding him. Things had pushed themselves in through the gaps between the bars — pale, ropy, writhing columns as thick as a man's arm, wrapped around his forearms, around his shoulders, holding him flat against the iron.

And they were made of worms.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each column was a rope of individual bodies turning over one another, holding their shape by nothing but the will of the thing they belonged to, glistening in the green light.

The eye followed them back, because the eye always follows, and the eye found the figure standing outside the cage behind him.

The robes were there.

And the person inside them was not a person.

The shape of a man was coming apart as they watched. It was not dissolving; it was *revealing.* A sleeve was a sleeve and then it was a column of moving bodies in the shape of a sleeve. A shoulder held its line and then did not. A hood framed something that had the proportions of a face and none of the properties of one, and in one of those impossible limbs it held a man against a cage.

Around Aravashnial's neck was a black metal collar.

At its center was a faceted green crystal, and it was brightening.

Blood was running down his neck from under the rim of it, in four separate lines, and Caleth's eye — his eye, his trained, cursed, unsparing eye — measured the spacing and understood.

There were needles inside it.

They had driven into him.

The crystal grew brighter. Aravashnial screamed again, and it was a different scream from the first one.

And then his expression changed.

It was not pain any more. Caleth had seen him in pain — in a tunnel under Kenabres with his eyes burned out, holding perfectly still while a stranger poured a restoration into him. This was not that.

For the first time in the entire length of their acquaintance, Caleth Aranor looked at Aravashnial's face and saw genuine, undisguised panic.

The green light caught in his eyes and filled them.

He knew what it was. Whatever the collar was doing, or was about to do, *he knew what it was*, and he had one half-second of knowing it before it happened, and it terrified him.

Then the crystal flared —

— and the spell was ripped away.

Not dismissed. Not dispelled. Caleth had been dispelled before; dispelling is a hand closing a book. This was a wire under tension being cut with a chisel. The bridge did not come down length by length; the whole span was *severed*, out past the middle, with him still standing on his end of it.

**Severed.**

The image collapsed. The water went white and then went to water. Caleth's connection to Aravashnial ceased to exist between one instant and the next, and everything that had been running along it came back up the line into him at once and threw him bodily backward off the table.

He hit the floor.

Somebody got to him. Hands under his arms — Korroc's, plate-gauntleted, and Nageru's on the other side, and they had him up and sitting on the bench before he had entirely established where the ceiling was.

The command room was in uproar. Aron had his hands on his head. Anevia had come off the door and was halfway across the floor. Irabeth was saying his name.

Caleth could not hear any of it properly. His ears were ringing and there was a taste in his mouth like a struck flint.

He sat on the bench in Citadel Drezen with Korroc's hand on his shoulder and Thane crouched in front of him saying something, and he did not answer, because he was not in the room.

He was running it.

*Worms.*

*A man made of worms, in robes, moving without footsteps, who destroyed the Blackwing Library and commanded the demons that did it. Who was a man of flesh and blood in the Third Crusade, seventy years ago, and is not one now. Who holds the Ivory Sanctum. Who collects books with Riftwarden marks in them, and knows the marks on sight, and has had one of them for years because he took it off the body of somebody Aravashnial loved.*

*Who is extremely dangerous.*

*Who wears a man's shape.*

*And casts none of his own.*

He reached inside his coat, past the small heavy vial, and his fingers found the folded page, and he did not take it out.

He did not need to take it out. He knew what was written on it. He had known since a room upstairs, yesterday, with paper on the floor around his feet.

Everything he had been given in nineteen years had been given to him in pieces, by people who then left: a spiral on his shoulder blade he could not see without a mirror, three sentences from an old fraud in a back room, a word — *lost* — from somebody in an office who had not even looked up. He had built a whole life around refusing to accept the last of those, and he had never once had anything to put in its place.

Now he had something to put in its place, and the man who gave it to him was in an iron cage in the dark with needles in his neck, and had gone still at exactly the wrong moment, on purpose, for him.

*I will find you.*

It was not a vow. Caleth Aranor did not make vows in rooms.

It was a finding.

He became aware that the room had gone quiet, and that everyone in it was looking at him and waiting, and that he had been sitting on a bench staring at a kettle for some considerable time.

He looked at the kettle. There was nothing in it but water, going still.

"Caleth," said Korroc, gently, for what was evidently not the first time. "Lad. What was it?"

And Caleth told them.

"Xanthir Vang."
