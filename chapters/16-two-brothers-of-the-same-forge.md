# Two Brothers of the Same Forge

*Chapter Sixteen — The Price of Working with Demons*

---

The armor did not creak.

Korroc noticed it the way you notice a sound stopping — not at once, but in the space where the sound should have been. Sixty paces of walking out of Torag's fouled chapel and down a corridor his people had cut, and the plate on him made no complaint. No grind at the shoulder where his old pauldron had worn its bed. No tick from the left greave that had ticked since Kenabres. Just weight, distributed the way a good load is distributed, sitting on him as though the smith who set the rivets had known the exact slope of his shoulders and the exact barrel of his chest and had built out from there.

*Seventy years in a demon's hoard,* he thought, *and it fits me like it's been waiting.*

He put the thought down. He'd been picking it up and putting it down since the treasury, and it never got lighter and it never came apart, and there was work.

Behind him the chapel stood a little cleaner than it had. Not clean. He was not fool enough to call it clean — a man does not undo seventy years of patient defilement with a cup of conjured water and half an hour. But the Father's carved feet stood clear of filth, and the base of the great statue showed honest dwarven granite, and Caleth Aranor had knelt in a house that was not his and spent the smallest magic in his book washing a god he did not serve. Korroc had put a hand on the half-elf's shoulder and had not trusted himself past that. Some things you did not put words to. Words would have made it smaller.

They came from the west, so Thane took the east.

His cousin went along the wall trying doors the way he always tried doors, which was patiently and in an order — listen, look, touch, and only then the latch. The first two gave onto rooms that had gone unused so long the dust lay unbroken, and Thane's mouth twitched at that, at a fortress with more rooms than it had garrison to fill.

The third gave onto ruin.

"Armory," Thane said. "Was."

The upper parapets had come down through the roof of it at some point in the long years, and the fall had made a ramp of itself, a rise of broken stone and shattered beam sloping up and out to open sky. Korroc looked up through the hole and saw grey morning, and heard, thin and far, the noise of the war.

Caleth heard it too, and his head came around, and Korroc watched the arithmetic happen behind the half-elf's green eyes.

"Thane." Caleth didn't look away from the gap. "Could you go up there and make sure there aren't any archers?"

*Aye,* Korroc thought. *That's the question, isn't it.* Because that was what the whole morning had been — every gallery they'd emptied, every slit they'd taken away from a bowman, was arrows that never came down on the men climbing the bridge. Korroc could not see the crusade from in here. He could feel it the way he felt weather coming. Somewhere below and south, Irabeth's soldiers and two hundred freed men in their own recovered steel were walking uphill into a fortress, and every stone Korroc's people had ever cut was between them and it.

Thane went up the rubble like water going the wrong way, quick and quiet, and stopped just under the lip, and did not look over. He listened first. He always listened first.

Then he raised his head a hand's breadth, and froze — and Korroc had known his cousin fifty-one years and could read that freeze at forty feet in bad light. *Something's there. Something hasn't seen me.*

Thane held still long enough that Korroc's own breathing started to bother him. Then his cousin's hand came off the stone and gave them three fingers, and then two fingers curling up beside his temple, unmistakable.

Horns.

Caleth's chin lifted a fraction. "Let's kill them."

Korroc hefted the shield and rolled the adamantine hammer once in his fist, and beside him Nageru inclined his head, and that was the whole council of war.

---

Caleth went up first, and he went up *fast*, past Thane without a word or a glance, and Korroc — who had never in his life gotten anywhere first — understood why a half-breath later. The half-elf was talking as he climbed, low and quick, and a shape gathered out of nothing at the top of the ramp and went out ahead of him: a glaive of pale light, hanging in the air, moving on no arm at all. It swept at something Korroc couldn't see yet. Korroc heard a screech and the scrabble of a hoofed thing throwing itself backward.

Then Thane was over the lip, and the parapet answered.

Korroc came up last. He always came up last; he had made his peace with it about the time he'd made his peace with rain. By the time his boots found the broken siege-engine litter of the northern parapet, the morning had gone loud.

Brimoraks. He knew them now — goat-headed, squat, their eyes lit from behind like coals in a grate, and where they stood the stone smoked under hooves that were not hooves but small standing fires. Three of them, as Thane had counted. And a fourth, which Thane had not, standing behind Thane and Caleth both, on ground the two of them had already walked past.

The nearest filled its lungs and breathed.

Korroc had seen it on the towers and it still turned something over in him. Not flame. *Blood* — a gout of it, boiling, thrown out of a demon's chest in a wide scalding fan, and it took Thane and Caleth both in its path. Thane dropped under it clean, flat to the stone. Caleth did not, and it caught him full, and Korroc's chest tightened —

— and the half-elf came through it grimacing, steam rolling off his shoulders, hurt but standing. That old strangeness of him, the curtain his body drew against heat without asking his permission. It had saved him twice now that Korroc could count. Korroc thanked the Father for it in three flat words and kept moving.

Nageru went over the parapet's litter and did not stop at the first demon or the second. He rolled *past* them both, low and impossibly economical, and came up behind the pair that had breathed, and his fists were already working before his feet had settled.

The fourth demon — the one nobody had counted — kicked out at Caleth with a burning hoof, and missed, and Korroc's short legs finally brought him where they were going.

He came in behind it. It never turned. He set his boots and swung the way his father had taught him to swing, from the hip and through the target, and the adamantine head took the thing across the spine with the flat honest sound of a forge-blow landing true.

It staggered. It did not fall. *Tough,* he thought, and set again.

Across the parapet, the demon Caleth's floating glaive had been harrying finally lost patience with it and rounded on the thing — and found what any creature finds when it swings at a spell: nothing to hit, nothing to hurt, nothing to make stop. It clawed the pale blade and its talons passed through the light, and the light kept coming, dull and patient and unkillable, an annoyance that could not be answered.

Korroc almost laughed. There was a lesson in it he'd have liked to hand to certain apprentices. *You can't hammer a thing that isn't there. Turn around and mind the man behind you.*

It turned around too late. The air by its shoulder shivered — Korroc felt it more than saw it, a wrongness like a door trying to open in a wall that had no door — and then the shiver simply *stopped*, folded up, went nowhere. Caleth had walked onto this parapet and the whole of it had quietly become ground a demon could not step off of. That was the working the half-elf carried with him now, the one he never spoke about; Korroc had watched it fail an enemy three times since Kenabres and had never once heard Caleth mention it. Whatever pit-road the brimorak had reached for had a hand on the latch, and the hand was Caleth's.

The demon spun, baffled, and Radiance took it in the chest. White light ran the length of the leaf-blade and the goat-thing screamed and came apart into nothing at all, sent home in pieces.

Thane stabbed at the one in front of him and got a dagger in. It answered with a longsword wrapped in fire, a hard sweeping cut across his cousin's ribs, and Korroc felt it arrive in his own chest a half-beat later — the thread carrying the hurt up the bar the way heat comes to the hand. He grunted and wore it and did not stop swinging.

Nageru turned on that one and unloaded. Four blows, five; Korroc lost the count. The demon was gone before the last of them landed.

Then the one in front of Korroc came awake to what it was fighting.

It swung three times, fast — faster than a thing that size had any business being. The first hit the shield and the shield turned it. The second got through and bit into the plate at his side, and the gold-and-mithral took it better than his old steel ever had; he felt the impact and not much of the edge. The third he caught on the haft of the hammer, steel on steel with a ring that went up his arms and into his teeth.

Then it was his turn.

He brought the hammer round into the demon's chest and heard something structural give, and brought it back up and down on the skull, and the brimorak folded in on itself and was unmade.

*Three.* And the last one, all the way down the parapet, took in air.

"*Down!*" he bellowed.

The line of boiling blood came the length of the walk and caught them all. Thane was out of it before Korroc finished the word. The rest of them got mostly clear and wore the splash, and Korroc took his share along the arm and swore in Dwarven at the sting of it.

The pale glaive found the demon while its attention was elsewhere. It struck hard this time, real damage, and the brimorak reeled — and Thane was already charging, dagger coming up under, and the thing slipped sideways from it faster than a wounded creature should have. Then a shadow crossed the grey sky above it. Nageru had gone *up*, an impossible unhurried leap off the broken frame of a catapult, and came down heel-first, and the last brimorak stopped being anything.

Silence, and the fires guttering out of the stone where hooves had stood, and four sets of lungs.

Korroc got the wand out and went to Caleth first — the steam-scald across his chest and shoulders — and then stood a moment with his eyes half-shut, drawing what he could off the thread out of Thane's ribs and Nageru's clawed arm and into himself, taking the strain the way you take heat off a bar so the metal comes off the anvil true. Then he wanded his own arm.

"That one had four," Thane said, wiping both blades. "I gave you three."

"You gave me the three that were standing where a man could see them." Korroc put the wand away. "The fourth one wanted to be behind us. That's not your failing, cousin. That's just what they are."

---

They went back inside, and the citadel gave them a stretch of nothing.

South along the hallway they came into storerooms — real ones, stacked with foodstuffs, enough for a garrison and then some. Korroc read the stores with a quartermaster's cold eye and thought of Horgus and thought: *ours, if we live to want it.* Past the stores, a long room that had been a dining hall once, and had not been anything for a long time.

They came out the far side and stood on the citadel's opposite face from where they'd folded in that morning, at the foot of a stair going up, and the doors at the bottom of the stairs stood ajar.

Thane looked through the gap for a long time.

"Beds turned over," he said at last, very low. "Furniture broken. Bodies." His weathered face did the small collapse it did when it read something it did not like. "Dwarves and tieflings. Cultists, by the look. And they've been — " he stopped, and started again, flatter — "chewed."

He pushed the doors wider.

Two dwarves stood among the wreckage. They were not well. That was the kindest way Korroc's mind could put it in the half-second it had; they stood wrong, and their jaws hung wrong, and there were pieces of them missing that a living dwarf keeps. And behind them, filling the back of the room, something huge and winged and hairy from crown to hoof, with a tongue that turned in the air like a thing looking for a current.

Korroc did not wait this time.

He had waited on the parapet because his legs made him wait; he did not wait now, because there were two dwarves in that room, and whatever had happened to them, they had been dwarves. He went in first and put himself in front of the nearest one and reached down through the floor into the deep stone beneath the citadel — his people's stone, cut and laid by hands like his — and pulled its stubbornness up into himself until his boots might as well have been part of the wall.

The hammer came down once. Nageru was beside him before the echo faded and finished it.

"Ghoul," Korroc said, low, watching the thing collapse. It came out of him half as identification and half as something else. *Not a dwarf. Not anymore. Not for a long time before we walked in here.* It did not help as much as he wanted it to.

The second one came at him with all three limbs it had left to come with. Two of the strikes missed entirely. The third found him and skidded off — off his skin, not his plate, off the place where the Stonelord blood ran close to the surface and the flesh remembered it was kin to granite. The ghoul's claws broke on him like a nail on a hearthstone.

Caleth was in the room by then, and Radiance struck the winged thing hard.

It struck back — claws that Caleth swayed away from — and then it did something else. It caught the half-elf's eyes and *held* them.

Korroc saw it happen and could not tell, afterward, how he had known what he was looking at. There was no light and no sound. There was only a demon staring into a man's face, and a pull, a drag, like a slow tide going out — and it was not Caleth's blood it wanted or his breath. It was reaching for the part underneath. The part a man is.

Caleth's jaw set. His eyebrows came down, and he *frowned* at it — the way he'd frown at a bad translation, at a sum that would not come out — and the tide broke on him and slid off.

Korroc's stomach turned over anyway. There were things he had learned to fight and things he had not, and something that reached past a man's flesh to take hold of his soul belonged to the second kind. He put the hammer twice into the second ghoul and it went down twitching, and he moved to put himself where the winged thing would have to come through him to try that again.

Thane tried to tumble past to its rear and was stopped cold — the thing's bulk simply filling the space he'd meant to use — and its claw came at him and missed, and his dagger went in and found nothing.

Then Nageru's fists, twice. Then Radiance, twice more, in and out and in. The demon screamed and slumped, and Thane's dagger arrived in the fold of it and it was gone, banished, the room suddenly ringing with the absence.

Korroc stood among the beds and made himself look at the bodies.

Cultists. All of them. Dwarf and tiefling, robed and marked, the enemy's own — dead in their own barracks, chewed at, the two of them who had risen from it standing guard over the leavings.

"Feeding on its own," he said.

Caleth glanced at him.

"The thing kept house here." Korroc's voice came out rougher than he meant. "These were its allies. It ate them, and what it left of them got up and kept it company." He shook his head slowly. "That's the price of working with demons. Nobody ever tells the new ones. They think there's a bargain. There's no bargain. There's just a thing that's hungry and a fool who's standing nearest."

Nobody argued with him. Nageru bowed his head briefly, the way he did, prayer done before you noticed it starting.

---

The battlement above that stair was empty, and from it they could see the war.

Korroc set his hands on the parapet and looked south and let himself have ten seconds of it. The Ahari lay grey and hard below, and on it and beyond it, small as a thing you'd model in sand, the crusade was fighting — the Queen's colors well up the approach, the tiefling mass bent back from them, the line moving in the direction a line moves when it is winning.

"That's promising," Caleth said. And then, because he was Caleth: "But we still have a job."

Korroc grunted, and looked a moment longer, and turned his back on it. It was harder than he'd expected. Down there men were dying under his family's walls and up here he could do nothing for a single one of them except keep walking north.

*So walk north,* he told himself. *Everything you kill up here is a thing that can't fall on them.*

On the way back down the stairs Caleth had the map out — the Stoneveins' map, his father's and his uncle's and their wives', folded soft as cloth from the handling. The half-elf traced the middle of it with a finger and made a low noise.

"Rubble has fallen all along here," he said. "The middle's shut by it. If we're going to get any further north, we have to go outside to do it." He looked up. "Back up where we fought the brimoraks."

So they climbed the fallen roof again, and came out onto the northern parapet with its dead fires, and walked the whole length of the citadel's back in the open grey wind, all the way to the northernmost wall. Korroc walked it with one hand trailing the merlons, feeling the courses under his glove, reading the work the way he could not stop reading it: *level. plumb. true. Somebody's whole long day, this.* Seventy-five years, three lifetimes of desecration, and the wall was still standing where a dwarf had put it.

They went back in through two sets of double doors, into a hallway, and Thane knelt at the first door in it.

And stopped.

"Metal," he said. "On stone. Grating." His head tilted. "It's stopped now."

He grumbled something in Dwarven about doors, and moved to the hinge side, and pushed it open.

---

The room was a garrison bunkroom, orderly in a way nothing else in this place had been, beds ranked diagonally along the eastern wall. And chained by one ankle to the frame of one of them was a man.

Human. Ragged. His plate dented and half-buckled and his clothes under it torn to strips. He had been hauling at the bed — that was the grating Thane had heard, bed-frame legs on stone — and the reason for it was on the next bunk over: a rapier, laid out neat, a hand's breadth past the end of his chain. Somebody had measured that distance. Somebody had *enjoyed* measuring that distance.

The man froze when the door opened.

Thane went in along the far wall, quick, and picked the rapier up off the bunk before the man could think about it.

Which was when the shouting started.

"*Master Staunton will eat your flesh* — " the man bellowed, throwing himself to the end of the chain — "*and use your skeleton for a coat-rack, you thick-headed pig-legged* — " and on from there, at length, in a register Korroc had last heard outside a Kenabres tavern at closing.

Korroc walked in and stood and looked at him.

And what he saw did not match what he was hearing.

He looked at people the way he looked at metal, and he had never been able to stop; and when a paladin of the Father looks at a man there is a thing that comes back, or does not. Cultists came back foul. The men in Torag's chapel this morning had come back clean and stayed clean while they swung at him. And this shrieking, spitting, threatening wreck of a swordsman came back —

Nothing. No taint at all.

"I do not see evil on the man," Korroc said.

Thane's eyebrow climbed. He turned the rapier over in his hand and said, dryly, "Talking pretty harsh to people who could unchain you, aren't you?"

"Why are you chained?" Korroc asked him.

More obscenity. Korroc looked at him a while longer — at the sores where the shackle had sat for a long time, at the plate that had been good plate before somebody had let it rot on a man's body, at the eyes.

The eyes were wrong. Not evil-wrong. *Vacant*-wrong, like a forge banked and covered where somebody else held the key to the damper.

"Something's not right with this man," he said to Thane.

Caleth came up beside him and studied the wreck with his head slightly tilted, the way he studied a painting.

"You look familiar, sir," the half-elf said pleasantly. "What would be your name?"

The man sputtered at him and gave him nothing.

Caleth's fingers moved. He looked once more, differently, and his mouth thinned.

"He has a charm on him." A beat. "And unless I've lost my eye entirely, this is Maranse Delaskru. A rather famous swashbuckler. He disappeared about two years ago."

Korroc did not weigh it long. There was a man in front of him with something else's hand on the back of his neck, and Korroc had a way to take a hand off a neck.

He reached down — not into the stone this time but into the other place, the golden well the wardstone's shattering had left burning in him, the one he still could not look at directly — and took hold of what he needed and let it out through his palm.

The shouting stopped mid-word.

The man blinked. Looked around the room slowly, taking in the beds, the chain, the four of them. He put a hand to his own head like a man surfacing from a long fever. Then his eyes came up and settled on Korroc — and moved, deliberately, from the shield on his arm to the breastplate on his chest. Torag's hammer and anvil. Iomedae's sunburst, gold on mithral.

"Are you with Staunton," he said.

"No," Korroc growled.

"Hell no," said Thane.

The man nodded and let out a long breath and sat down on the edge of the bunk with his hand still on his head.

Caleth stepped forward and gave him the courtesy in full, the way he always did, the small formal bow that cost him nothing and bought so much.

"My name is Caleth Aranor, a knight of the Fifth Crusade. You have been rescued. There is a battle between two armies outside these walls. You can get out over the wall, make your way to our rear, and report."

"And miss my chance to return the favor to the ones who put me here?"

"You probably aren't in any condition to keep fighting."

"I'm fine."

Korroc watched Caleth's jaw tighten a fraction. The half-elf tried the other door. "We need you to go back and give the Queen a message."

Korroc found himself speaking before he'd decided to. "After that, if you want to join the fight outside, I'm sure they'd welcome another sword."

It was the natural thing to say. It was, he would think later, the exact hinge of the whole day, and it swung so easily he never heard it move.

The man frowned. "I would prefer to find Chorussina, and repay her for what she did to me."

"Who is that?" Thane said.

"A tiefling sorceress. Somewhere down below."

"She got the best of you the first time."

"She got the upper hand." The man's chin came up, and for a moment there was something of the famous swordsman in the ruin. "It won't happen again. Can I have my rapier?"

"I should stab you with it," Thane said, "with all the talk you were shouting at us."

Korroc shot his cousin a look. "Wait, now. You were the one who stopped us before—"

"We couldn't understand her." Thane's voice came back hot and instant, and Korroc heard the raw edge under it, and understood: *Jestak.* The woman in the furs, in the room with twelve heads on one side and twelve bodies on the other, whom Thane had thrown out a hand to save on a hunch, and had been arguing with himself about ever since. Korroc had backed him then. He had put his own trust behind that hunch when Korroc's own reading of the woman had said *evil* in letters a foot high.

The man stood, and walked, and the chain came up short. He looked down at it.

"Can you help me?"

Thane hesitated. Korroc watched him do it — watched his cousin's hands want the picks and his head not want to give them. Then Thane went to one knee and the shackle went *click* and fell open.

"Yes," Thane said, standing. "But you need to leave this citadel. You're in no shape."

"Can I have my sword?"

"No. Not unless you leave this citadel."

"I am going to find Chorussina whether you give me my sword back or not."

And Korroc — because a man walking unarmed into a demon-held fortress was a man walking into a grave, because the swordsman had come back clean under a paladin's eye, because there was a thing in him that had never once in fifty-one years been able to leave a wrong tool in a working man's hand or a right one out of it — shook his head and said the words.

"Just give his sword back."

Thane looked at him a long moment.

Then he flipped the rapier and held it out hilt-first, and the man took it, and something like the ghost of a bow happened, and they had a fifth in the party.

---

He led them north, and he knew the way.

Korroc noticed that and made nothing of it — a man chained in a garrison for two years learns his corridors. They passed through rooms with nothing in them but dust and old wreckage, working toward the citadel's back wall, and the swordsman walked between Nageru and Caleth and talked a little, and moved better as he went, the stiffness of the chain coming out of him with every hundred paces.

At the far wall, Thane knelt at another door, and the swordsman spoke up behind him.

"Be careful. Last time I came through here there were some thoxels."

Thane stopped. Turned. Looked at him.

Caleth looked at him too. "Nasty demons."

The man nodded.

Thane turned back to the door and eased it open a finger's width, and Korroc watched his shoulders go still and then very deliberately loose.

Three of them. Bows in their hands, backs to the door, crowded at arrow slits — shooting *down*, at the colors, at men climbing under a sky that they had every reason to think was safe now.

Thane did not report it. Thane charged.

Korroc was three strides behind and felt it hit — a wave, wide and grey and reasonable, washing back over all of them from the demons at the slits. It got into him the way the boiling blood had not. It said, in a voice that felt entirely like his own: *the danger is behind you. Turn around. The half-elf is at your back with a glaive. Turn around and deal with him first.*

For a quarter-second Korroc's grip shifted on the hammer.

Then the stone in his blood said no, in the flat unbothered way stone says things, and the whole rotten suggestion slid off him and lay there looking stupid.

Thane had already shrugged it off and put a dagger into a demon's back. Caleth was beside him, resisting it too, Radiance going in. Nageru arrived and hit the same demon and Korroc saw the aasimar's eyes flick once toward Thane and away — *he felt it too. He was told to strike his brother and he simply declined* — and then the demon at the slit dropped its bow and came around with a sword in both hands.

Not a sword. Two of them, joined at the grip and running opposite ways, a blade out of each end of a shaft.

It caught Nageru across the chest on the first sweep.

Korroc went past all of them.

That was his work and he knew it in his bones: not the killing, the *standing*. Two more of the things were coming up from the back of the room, and the fight was already a knot, and if those two got in among Thane and Caleth and Nageru while they were busy, men Korroc loved would start bleeding out in a room he could have blocked. So he put himself in the gap and reached down through the floor into the citadel's bones and rooted there, and became the door.

They came at him. He'd expected that; that was the point of him. One of them hit him twice, hard, and Korroc felt the blows land and felt most of them arrive as pressure rather than injury, the way a hammer lands on an anvil and hurts the hammer. He set his teeth and held.

Beside him, unexpectedly, the swordsman fought.

Korroc noted it with the part of his mind that was always keeping the ledger. The man was *good*. Ragged and half-starved and two years off his craft, and he flowed around the demon in front of him like water finding a crack, and his rapier went in and out three times before the thing understood it was being killed. When it turned on him he simply was not where it turned, twice, three times, unhurried.

*Well,* Korroc thought, with a small warm satisfaction he would want back later. *So the sword was the right call after all.*

The farthest demon vanished and reappeared beside Nageru and hit him twice, and blood struck the floor.

Korroc stepped back out of his stance — it cost him, breaking a root always cost him — and reached and put his hand on the aasimar and pushed most of the ruin out of him, and then swept his other hand wide and let the gold go out over all of them at once.

Thane's dagger found the one in front of him. Caleth's blade found it a moment later, and it was gone.

Then Thane came in on the one savaging Nageru while its eyes were forward, and both daggers went in low, and the demon's insides became outsides, and it fell apart before it could finish falling and was gone.

The last of them died between four of them and the swordsman, everyone getting a piece — Caleth, Nageru, Korroc's hammer twice into its shoulder and skull, and then the rapier, driven hard and exactly where it needed to go.

The man came up grinning, chest heaving, and looked at Caleth.

"Nice work with that glaive."

Caleth glanced at him, and at the thin blade in his hand, and murmured something in which the word *toothpick* was distinctly audible, and turned away to clean Radiance.

Nageru made a sound that almost sounded like a laugh. He very nearly hid it.

Korroc healed himself, and finished mending Nageru's chest, and did not think about it again.

---

Past the double doors on the southern side, a dividing wall blocked the room beyond, and something in there was walking.

Thane heard it first, hand up. "Hooves."

Then he did a thing he did rarely and never lightly: he spoke a low Dwarven cadence over the five of them, that settling weight in the bones, the doorkeeper's blessing. Korroc felt it come down on him and felt what he always felt, which was that his cousin's god was quieter than his own and no less present for it.

They eased in along the dividing wall. Beyond it, Korroc could see the shape of a great room — a courtroom, by the bones of it, five columns holding up thirty feet of ceiling, the southeast corner a field of collapsed rubble.

The clop of hooves went on, unhurried, out of sight.

Caleth crossed to the far side of the divider and put one eye past it.

"Minotaur. Fiendish." A pause. "Two of them." And then, as one great head came round and fixed on him: "Damn it."

He cast as it came — a fast rising chant, and the world dropped a half-step slower around Korroc, his own limbs suddenly quicker than the air they moved through — and then the minotaur was on him, head down, horns leading, all that weight behind it.

Caleth did not give ground. He brought Radiance up and across and swept the horns aside, and the bull-thing went past him bellowing.

Korroc crossed to Thane and put a hand on his cousin's shoulder.

He had done this exactly once before, at a bridge, with Nageru. It was not the thread; the thread was always there, the thin constant thing that let him feel where they were and take a measure of what they suffered. This was different. This was *widening* it, opening the channel between himself and one man until half of everything that struck that man would run out of him and into Korroc instead.

"Half your wounds now, cousin," he said. "Go on."

Thane's mouth twitched. "You'll regret that."

"I regret most things."

Thane went, low and fast, around behind the minotaur that had charged Caleth, and one dagger went in and found a place a body cannot afford to be found in. The beast screamed. Nageru came round the wall and hit it twice.

The second minotaur came up behind Thane.

His cousin knew it was coming and did what he could, and it was not enough — a horn caught him and hauled him half off his feet, and the axe came down after it, and Korroc felt both of them arrive.

Not an echo. Not a phantom. *Wounds*, opening on his own body in the places the horn and the axe had gone, real blood running inside his plate, his share taken out of Thane's flesh and put into his. He rocked with it, and grunted, and stayed on his feet.

*There it is,* he thought, through the white edge of it. *That's the trade. Made it with my eyes open.*

Caleth reached down into the golden well the same way Korroc had done and thrust Radiance once, twice, three times, faster than the eye could keep, and the first minotaur bellowed and collapsed and did not get up. Then the world folded and the half-elf was standing next to Thane, casting — and Thane was simply not there anymore, gone from sight between one blink and the next.

Korroc walked up to the second minotaur and put the hammer into it once, to make sure it was looking at him.

It never saw what killed it. Out of nowhere at all, three wounds opened along the beast's flank in the time it took to draw breath, and blood hit the flagstones, and the thing went down like a felled tree with an invisible dwarf standing somewhere in the wreck of it.

---

The next door gave onto maps.

Every wall of the chamber beyond it carried them — the Worldwound and the nations it bordered, drawn large and drawn carefully, the kind of work that took a patient hand months. Korroc had a half-second to hate that. *They study us. They sit in a room my grandfather's people built and they study where to bite next.*

Then the babaus came off the walls at them.

Three of them, all sinew and hooked claws and that oily hide that turned a blade if you didn't put it in true. Thane went straight in without waiting and got a claw and a bite for it, and Korroc took his half and kept walking.

The farthest one shimmered — that same wrongness in the air, a door trying to be a door — and stopped, and stood there, and *babbled*, turning in place, looking for a way out of a room that had quietly stopped having one. Two paladins in a doorway and their two oaths overlapping, and every road out of this chamber shut. Korroc had almost stopped noticing it happen. He noticed now, because the demon's confusion was so complete it was very nearly funny.

Caleth thrust and missed. Korroc moved up beside Thane and — deliberately, the way he'd learned — stepped in *front* of Caleth, so that the half-elf could put the glaive out over his shoulder and work at reach with a wall in front of him. He set his feet and drew the stone up and hit the babau in front of Thane hard enough to fold it double.

Nageru came in through the door like something thrown and the demon nearest him screeched and flattened itself to the floor to get under the blow.

And at the back of the chamber, where the room turned a corner Korroc could not see around, a dwarf stepped into view and raised his hands and cast.

Sound tore the room apart.

It was not a noise so much as a violence — a great brazen crash that got in behind Korroc's eyes and rattled the pieces of him, and he saw Thane go down on one knee with both daggers falling out of his hands.

Two thoughts arrived in Korroc at once, in order, and he would remember the order.

The first was: *not Staunton.* He'd have known Staunton. He had seen that face in the wardstone's golden vision, sitting at his father's table in a room full of laughing dwarves, and it was burned into him deeper than anything he'd ever looked at with his eyes. This was not that face.

The second was worse and came a half-beat behind: *Thane is down and there's a demon two feet from him.*

The babau went for his cousin, and Korroc could not reach it, and the thing lunged for a stunned man's throat and — stumbled. Caught a foot on something. Splayed out full-length on the flagstones a hand's width short.

Korroc did not have breath to thank anyone. He filed it and swung.

The second babau tore into Nageru, twice. The third came at Korroc and Korroc turned both strikes without much trouble; the demon was fast but it had never in its life hit anything that stood like a wall and thought like a smith. Caleth's glaive came over his shoulder and bit it. Korroc healed himself with a fast pull of grace and hammered the thing twice in the chest.

Nageru's fists found the one on him and it was gone.

At the back of the room the dwarf cast again, and this time it came at Korroc — a cold grey weight coming down over his eyes and his ears at once, the world beginning to go silent and dark and *narrow* —

— and Korroc set his jaw and shoved it off, the way you shove a bad weld back into the fire. His sight came back. The room came back with it, loud.

Thane came up off his knee, scooped both daggers, took a claw across the back for the two seconds it cost him, and answered it with a blade in the demon's ribs. A small wound opened on Korroc's own back to match, and he wore it. The demon hit Thane again, several times, fast — and Korroc felt each one land in himself, and his cousin stayed standing, and between the two of them they had enough blood left to finish it.

Caleth stabbed past him. The first thrust missed; the second went through the babau's chest and out, and the demon was gone. Korroc brought the hammer down on the last one's skull and the room was empty of demons.

He looked at the back corner, where a dwarf in good plate stood alone with a warhammer in his hand and a shield on his arm.

And Korroc thought about the map, and about a barracks with twenty-four pieces of a siege crew in it, and about a woman in furs saying *Joran Vhane's down there often*, and *he's not happy with the way his brother treats him.*

"Joran Vhane," he called. "Is that you?"

The dwarf said nothing.

But he stiffened — a small hard flinch through the shoulders, there and gone — and it was answer enough.

Nageru was already moving.

"Don't kill him," Korroc said.

He said it fast and flat, and Nageru's head dipped once without breaking stride, and the aasimar crossed the room and hit Joran Vhane hard enough to fold a lesser dwarf in half.

*Not dead,* Korroc thought. *Not dead is a door that stays open.*

That was when the side door banged wide and another half-fiend minotaur came through it swinging.

Korroc got the shield up in time and no more. The axe came down on it and his whole arm rang like a struck bar, and he heard himself grunt and felt his boots slide half a hand's breadth on the stone.

And behind the minotaur, walking, unhurried, a dwarf came out of the dark.

Thane cursed in Dwarven — one word, ugly, the worst one they had.

Korroc growled. He could not help it. It came up out of somewhere below his ribs, older than thought, the sound a dog makes at something in the yard.

Because it was the face. Seventy-five years and a wardstone's dying vision and half a war between them, and it was *the face from his father's table* — older, harder, gone grey and bloated with something that was not age — walking toward him with a glaive in its hands in a room with maps on the walls.

"Took you long enough to get here," said Staunton Vhane.

And charged Thane.

The glaive went in twice, both of them true, and Korroc's world went red-white as the exact wounds tore open in his own chest and side. He staggered. He did not fall. He had made a bargain at a dividing wall ten minutes ago and this was the bill for it, and he would have signed it again.

Caleth was already moving, a step and a word, and Thane vanished out of the air.

---

What followed, Korroc could only ever afterward remember in pieces, the way you remember a fire.

Joran Vhane swung his hammer at Nageru twice and hit nothing but the space the aasimar had already left.

Korroc reached into the golden well with both hands and pulled, and let a wave of healing roll out over all of them — and then, because the first one had not been enough and there was no time to be careful with himself, reached in and pulled again, and sent a second after it. He felt the well go shallower. He did not care.

"*Talk to him, Nageru!*" he roared.

Because the fight was going badly and Korroc had one idea and it was not a weapon. Because he had seen Joran Vhane flinch at his own name. Because somewhere under this floor was the thing they had climbed a fortress to find and this dwarf knew where it was and would not tell it to a corpse.

Nageru did not break stride and did not raise his voice.

"We know your brother treats you badly," he said, to a man he was in the middle of fighting. "We have good dwarves. Join us, and we will help you."

Korroc, bleeding, watching from twenty feet away, saw Joran Vhane's face come apart and put itself back together wrong. Conflict, plain as a crack in a casting.

"If you want," Nageru added, "you may simply stay here until we are finished. We will not hurt you."

And then the aasimar was gone in that way of his, simply across the room, arriving where he was needed.

Thane, still unseen, went at the minotaur. Korroc watched from the outside as a beast the size of a cart came apart at the seams — daggers finding it three, four times, blood and guts going onto the flagstones, and the great horned thing bellowing as its own flesh opened, and then falling backward, dead, killed by nothing anyone could see.

Then Caleth screamed.

Not loudly. That was the terrible part. Korroc's head came around and Caleth's back had gone rigid and his mouth had come open, and there was a blade in him — driven in from behind, and *cold*, so cold Korroc could see the frost of it blooming out through the half-elf's coat.

And standing behind him, where the swordsman had been standing a heartbeat before, was a woman with wings.

She leaned in and said something in Caleth's ear, close as a lover, and Korroc did not hear it. He heard his own voice instead, tearing out of him with a rage in it that surprised him.

"*Damn it, Thane!*" he bellowed, at a cousin he could not see. "How are you right so often? You wanted to kill that ass!"

*I gave it back its sword.*

That was the thought under the shout, and it went into him deeper than the glaive had. He'd looked at that thing with a paladin's eye and found no evil on it, and he had been right — because there had been nothing there to find, no soul at all in the shape it was wearing, only a hole with a face over it. He'd broken a charm that had never been a charm. He'd handed a demon a blade and told his cousin to stand down and walked it through four rooms of his family's fortress at Caleth's back, and it had waited. It had fought beside them and killed for them and *waited*, patient as any tradesman, for the one moment when the wizard's spine was turned.

*You'll know her,* Nurah had said, in a tent a hundred years ago. *She's a succubus. You'll know her.*

He had not known her. He had unchained her.

Then Staunton Vhane was in front of him and there was no more room for any of it.

"So the Stonevein brothers have finally made it." The voice was almost pleasant. It came out of that face — his father's friend's face — and made a mockery of the room. "And one of them hides like a coward."

The glaive came twice. Korroc's shield was up and his stance was set and it did not matter; the blows went through the guard and through the plate and through him, pure crushing force, and two wounds opened in him and his own blood ran down inside the gold-and-mithral.

Behind Staunton, Joran Vhane cast, and there were suddenly two of him, and then three, and the copies moved apart.

Korroc reached down into the well one more time and gave the last of what he could spare to the others — the gold going out across the room to Thane's unseen hurts and Nageru's chest and the frost-ruin in Caleth's back — and then he reached past the well and down, into the stone, and further, and took hold of the thing he had touched exactly once before in his life, in a library in Kenabres with a dead half-elf at his feet.

He had not known what it was, that time. He had only fallen into it.

This time he went and got it.

The fire came up through him from the soles of his boots and he did not burn. His body opened into it — flesh going to light, the plate ghosting inside a blaze the color of a forge at the exact moment a good blade comes out true, gold and white and clean. His outline blurred. His hands were still hands enough to hold a hammer.

The room got very bright and everyone in it turned to look.

Korroc let the fire wash back through himself and felt his own wounds close in it, the way heat closes a seam, and stood in the middle of a demon's map-room burning like a lamp in the dark.

*Father,* he thought, simply. *I am about Your work.*

Nageru turned on Staunton Vhane. He did not say anything. His face had gone somewhere Korroc had never seen it go — not angry; *decided* — and his fists took fire of their own, the cold gold of a smite, and he brought them down on the traitor three times, hard, one two three.

"*Don't kill him!*"

Thane's voice, out of nowhere, from somewhere near the wall.

Nageru's eyes went wide and his fourth blow stopped in the air. "*What?*"

And Korroc — burning, bleeding under the burning, looking at the face from his father's table — heard himself say it.

"No. You *can* kill him."

He understood his cousin. That was the worst of it. He understood exactly: Thane wanted the man breathing, because a breathing man is a door, and behind this one lay the whole Templar chain, the names, the years, everything an inquisitor could pull out of him with time and patience. Thane had spent his life reading rooms and this was the biggest locked room he'd ever stood in front of.

And Korroc — who had spent the whole of this day handing out mercy like bread, who had put himself between a fight and four charmed men, who had unchained a demon because it looked like a man who needed a sword — looked at Staunton Vhane and found that he had none left for this one. Not a scrap. He had opened his hand all morning and the last thing he had put in it had gone straight into Caleth's back.

*He sat at my father's table. He drank with them and dreamed the same city and then he sold them, and my father's bones are somewhere in this ground because of it.*

"He dies here," Korroc said.

Thane's daggers opened a red line along the succubus's arm — but she rolled off it, flowed around Caleth to his other side, and came up with a rapier of ice that hurt to look at. She thrust. Caleth swayed aside from it, precise as a fencer, and answered inside her guard, and Radiance went into her; and Korroc saw her face, and saw on it the particular outrage of a thing that had expected this to be easy.

Staunton turned on Nageru and stabbed three times.

All three went home. Korroc saw the glaive come out the other side of the aasimar and heard the sound Nageru made, which was almost nothing, and the still water folded up and went down onto the stone and did not move.

Radiance lit.

Korroc could see it from across the room — the white blaze running the length of the leaf-blade as Caleth called his goddess into a weapon that was already holy — and the half-elf drove it into the succubus three times, and she screamed, and the scream did not sound like a woman at all. It came out of the same much older mouth the false Iomedae had screamed with, that morning, in a chapel one floor and a whole lifetime ago. Then she came apart in holy light and was gone.

And Joran Vhane, who had been standing back doing nothing at all, raised his hand and cast a healing on his brother.

Korroc saw it and swore.

*Even now.* Even after everything Jestak had said. Even beaten to his knees and offered a way out, the man's hand went out to his brother, because that was what you did with a brother, and Droskar's own dark toil could not burn that out of a dwarf.

Korroc laid hands on himself and let his own fires do the rest of the work. Then he looked across fifteen feet of open floor at Nageru's body and did the thing he had learned to do at the Ahari, the nameless thing the wardstone's fire had put in him: he took a healing in his hand and he *threw* it, and it crossed the room like it had no distance to cross and landed as though he'd knelt and touched the aasimar's forehead with his palm.

Nageru's eyes came open.

He tried to roll, tried moving to get clear of the traitor's reach — and Staunton's glaive licked out and stopped him where he stood, pinning him in place with two feet of steel.

Then five wounds opened on Staunton Vhane, one after another, faster than counting.

Thane. Still unseen. Still exactly where his mother had taught him to be — in the dark behind a man who had forgotten there was a dark. The daggers went in and in and in and Staunton staggered under it, bewildered, turning to face something that was not there, and went down onto one knee.

And did not fall.

Korroc watched him hold himself upright on the haft of the glaive with an amount of ruin in him that should have ended three dwarves, and understood that whatever the Ivory Sanctum had put in the man, it was not done with him yet.

"So this is how it is." Staunton's voice came out wet. He was looking at Korroc, burning, and then past him at the empty air where the daggers had come from. "One snot-nosed Stonevein turns into a paladin. And the other one is a coward, hiding in the shadows."

And a wave of something black went out of him.

It rolled through all four of them at once — a cold pull with nothing physical in it, the exact opposite of Korroc's gold, a thing that reached into a man and *took*. Korroc felt his own fire gutter and his knees loosen. He heard Caleth grunt somewhere behind him.

Then the glaive came at Korroc, one last time, with everything the traitor had left in him behind it.

It was a killing blow. Korroc knew it while it was still coming — knew it the way a smith knows a bar is going to crack a half-second before it cracks, by something in the sound of it. The angle was right. The weight was right. The man behind it was dying and had decided to spend what was left of himself well, and that glaive was going in under the collarbone and out through the back, and Korroc Stonevein was going to be face-down on a demon's map-room floor before he finished understanding that he had lost.

It went into the gold-and-mithral. Through the mithral, through the mail beneath. Into him.

And the stone said no.

When he had come into this room he had set his feet and drawn the deep stone up through the floor and into himself, the way he had done a hundred times since Kenabres, the way his blood had known how to do long before his head had any words for it. He had been standing in it ever since. Carrying it under everything else. It had turned a babau's claws and eaten most of a minotaur's axe and he had not once thought about it.

It spent itself now. All of it, at once, on the one blow that mattered.

He felt it go. That was the part he would never be able to hand to anybody — not to Thane, not to Caleth, not to any priest of the Father who might someday ask him what it had been like. He felt the ancestor-stone gather in the path of the glaive and refuse to let the killing through. The blade came looking for the place where his death was supposed to be, and something was already standing in it, and it had his father's blood in it, and his grandfather's, and theirs before that, all the way down into the dark under the mountains where his people had first learned that stone remembers.

The steel went in a hand's breadth and stopped like it had struck a mountain.

It had.

He rocked. His vision went to a point. His knee started to go — and the stance was gone now, emptied, spent, and he could feel the absence of it the way you feel for a tool you have just set down and find your hand closing on nothing. There would not be another one of those today. Perhaps not for days.

He put the knee back anyway.

He stood up straight with a glaive in his chest and a man's face six inches from his own, and he looked into the eyes of the thing that had claimed to be his father's friend, and he did not say anything at all, because there was nothing in him worth spending on it.

The glaive came out. Blood came with it.

And then Caleth Aranor stepped past him with Radiance blazing white, and thrust three times, and Staunton Vhane fell on his face and stopped being.

---

Nageru stood up.

Korroc sent one more wave of healing over the room because his hands did it before his head caught up, and the gold rolled out and found all of them, and the fire he was wearing began, slowly, to think about going out.

Joran Vhane stood alone at the back of the chamber with his hammer hanging in his hand.

He looked at his brother's body. He looked around the room — at the dead babaus, at the dead minotaur, at the place on the floor where a succubus had been. Then he looked at Korroc, who was still burning.

He looked for a long time.

And then Joran Vhane dropped to his knees.

Not a surrender-gesture. Nothing that formal. His legs simply stopped holding him, the way legs do at the end of something long, and he knelt among the wreckage of his brother's household with his hammer on the stone beside him.

Korroc walked over and as he did, he let go of the fire.

He looked down and saw exhaustion. That was all that was there. Not defiance, not fear, not even much grief. Just a man who had been tired for a very long time and had finally been allowed to stop.

"Staunton was never the same after he came back from the Ivory Sanctum," Joran said, to the floor. Then he raised his head. "I assume my fate will be the same as his."

Korroc looked at him and, because his eyes could not stop reading, looked at the shield lying beside him.

There was a symbol on it. Korroc knew it the way a man knows the shape of his own house from the wrong side of a hill — because it was almost his. Almost the hammer and the anvil. The same bones, the same idea, and something gone wrong in the making of it, the proportions greedy, the anvil crowded, the whole design bent toward *toil* the way Torag's was bent toward *craft*.

The Dark Smith. Torag's own, once. The one who went down into the deep places and taught the beaten to worship their own beating; whose gospel was work without end and without joy and without the thing at the end of the work that makes a smith set down his hammer and look at what he's made and find it good.

Korroc felt something enormous and complicated move in his chest and did not have any idea what to do with it.

He asked the plain question, because plain questions were what he had.

"Droskar. Is that your god?"

Joran Vhane's eyes came up. There was a long pause in which Korroc could see the man deciding whether lying was worth the effort.

"Yes."

Korroc nodded slowly.

He thought about two brothers of the same forge. He thought about Thorek and Borin Stonevein, who had been brothers too, who had dreamed the same lost city and signed the same letter to the same Queen in the same year and had died in the same season, on this ground, without either one of them ever once turning on the other. He thought about the two of them that were left — himself, burning, and his cousin somewhere in the dark of this room with his blades already being wiped — and about how narrow the difference was, and how much of it was only luck, and whose fathers you happened to get.

And Korroc, who had spent the whole day opening his hand and had been bitten for it twice already and would carry the second bite a long while yet, opened it one more time.

Because that was the work. Not the striking. He had known it since before he could lift a full hammer: any fool can break a thing that has gone wrong. The craft is in what you do with metal that has been beaten badly by somebody else.

"Would you submit yourself to the Queen for judgment?" he said. "You don't have to die."

He looked the man in the eyes when he said it, and he did not soften it, and he did not promise anything he could not forge.

Doubt went across Joran Vhane's face — doubt, and something under the doubt that had not been allowed out in a long time and did not quite trust the door.

So Korroc gave him a place to put his hands. A first small task, the way you hand an apprentice a simple job to steady him.

"You could start," he said, "by telling us where the Sword of Valor is."

And Korroc watched it happen: recognition, and then, behind it, coming up slow and painful the way feeling comes back into a limb that has been slept on —

Hope.

Joran Vhane nodded.
