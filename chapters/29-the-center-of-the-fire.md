# The Center of the Fire

*Chapter Twenty-Nine — No More Games*

---

The hallway turned behind the forge, and Caleth followed it, and thought about doors.

He had been thinking about them since the ivory room. Three doors, and then a dozen, and every one of them a question somebody had written in wood and iron and left for him to answer: *what is behind this, and what does it want, and is it him?* So far the answers had been worms, and soldiers, and a bed nobody slept in, and a demon in a red coat who had taken Thane's own face away from him for the space of a dozen heartbeats. So far the answer to the last question had always been *no.*

He did not like how much that disappointed him.

The passage ran north and then east, narrow, unlit, the walls bare stone with none of the ivory work of the rooms in front. A servants' way. A back stair of a corridor, the sort of passage a house keeps for the things it does not want its guests to see. Thane went first, as always, low and quiet, and stopped at the one door at the end of it, and knelt.

Caleth watched him work. He had watched him work a great many locks by now, and there was always a moment, a little after the beginning, where the dwarf's face went entirely blank and his whole body seemed to be listening through the ends of his fingers. It was the stillest Thane ever was. Caleth had never told him he found it beautiful, and never would. Thane would have stabbed him.

*Click.*

The room beyond was long and plain, and smelled of cedar and old incense. Two long cabinets ran down the north wall and the east, and above them, on pegs set in the stone, robes hung in rows. Black, mostly. Some with red at the hem. Some with a dull gold thread worked into the cuffs in a pattern that, when Caleth let his eye rest on it for a moment, turned out to be a maze.

Vestments. Not clothes for living in. Clothes for standing in front of an altar in.

"The adepts?" Thane said, under his breath, walking the length of the pegs.

"Probably," said Caleth.

He did not know that. He thought it. The woman in the forge had worn black, and the two at the back of the room with the dummies, and he had put them together in his head into a word a demon had given them three nights ago in Drezen. *Blackfire.* But a robe was a robe. A man could hang any cloth he liked on a peg.

Korroc came in behind them, and Nageru, and Arueshalae last, who stopped in the doorway the way she stopped in every doorway, with her bow low and her face turned half back toward the passage. Korroc looked at the robes, and at the cabinets, and sniffed.

"Wardrobe," he said, with contempt. "We came all this way for a wardrobe."

Thane had stopped.

He was at the west wall, between two pegs, and he had put his hand flat against the stone and was standing with his head a little on one side, and Caleth knew that stance too. He went over.

"Secret door," said Thane.

Caleth looked. He would never have seen it. There was nothing to see: a wall, dressed stone, a little darker in one place where the robes had brushed against it for years. But he did not need to see the seam. He looked at the wall the other way — not with his eyes, quite, but with the part of him the academy in Kenabres had spent years training to look for the working under the stone — and there it was. A small hard knot of magic, set into the wall at the height of a man's hand, like a fist clenched round a key.

"It has an arcane lock on it," he said.

Thane looked up at him.

"Trap?"

Caleth shook his head. "I can't see one." A pause. "That's your job."

Thane chuckled, low. "I don't see one either. Just checking."

He went to work. Caleth stood back and watched with more interest than he would have admitted. An arcane lock was not a thing you picked; it was a thing a wizard sealed with his own will, and the proper answer to it was another wizard with a stronger one. But Thane did not seem to know that, or did not seem to care. He put his fingers on the place where the magic was knotted — he could not see it, Caleth was nearly sure he could not see it — and felt along the stone round it, pressing, here, there, a quarter-turn of the wrist against nothing. He frowned. He tried again, in a different order. And again. As if the spell were a set of tumblers and the order of the pressing were a key.

On the fifth try something in the wall *gave*. Caleth felt it go — the knot unclench, the fist open — a half-breath before the door swung inward on silent hinges.

He looked at Thane. Thane was not looking at him; he was looking through the door, and his face had changed.

"The room is lined in —" The dwarf leaned in, slowly. "Is that *lead?*"

Caleth came up at his shoulder.

---

It was a small room. Smaller than the robing room, and square, and every inch of it — walls, ceiling, the back of the door — was sheeted in dull gray metal, beaten flat and riveted at the seams. Lead. Caleth knew the soft gray look of it, the way it took the lamplight and gave nothing back. And all across the lead, scratched into it deep and close, runes. Hundreds of them. They ran in bands round the walls and up over the ceiling and down again, crowded together like the writing in a miser's ledger, and Caleth could not read one of them and did not need to.

The walls were sweating.

That was the only word for it. Beads of something clear stood out on the lead and ran together and trickled down in slow threads, catching the light, the whole room weeping into itself. And the water — if it was water — did not pool on the floor. It ran down to a hand's height above the floor and then went *into* the wall, as if the lead were drinking it.

Thane had leaned one shoulder over the threshold.

He jerked it back. Caleth saw his face go blank — not the lock-blankness, something else, something shocked — and then go dark. The dwarf stood very still.

"Thane?"

"It's gone," said Thane. His voice was flat. "Just then. When I leaned in." He flexed his fingers. "Whatever it is I've got, that answers when I pray. It wasn't there."

He did not like that. Caleth could see how much he did not like it. He pushed his own unease down under it, where it would keep, and looked at the room the other way.

It hit him like heat off a forge door.

Necromancy. Strong, and old, and not the cold clean kind he had read about at the academy, the kind a scholar could study at a careful distance with notes. This was the other kind. This was *hungry.* It came off the runes in a slow pulse, like breath, and under it — under it was a thing he did not have a word for, a feeling of something wanting very badly to be filled.

"What in the mercies," he said softly.

Thane turned and looked at him.

"That's not good," said Caleth. He made himself say the rest plainly. "Strong necromancy. Evil. It's coming straight off the runes."

He should have left it there. He knew he should have left it there. But there was a thread of the clear wet running down the lead an arm's length inside the door, and he wanted to *know*, the way he always wanted to know, the itch he had carried all his life; and before the sensible part of him had finished saying *don't* he had stepped one pace inside and put two fingers to it.

The whole room *groaned.*

It came out of the lead all around them, a low long sound like a ship's timbers in a swell — and in the same instant something reached into his head.

Not a voice. A hand. It went past his eyes and down into him, quick and practiced, the way a thief's hand goes into a coat, feeling for the inside pocket. He felt it go past Kenabres. Past a crowd at Armasse, and a silver dragon turning her great head. Down, and down, looking for the thing he kept closest, and he knew what it was looking for before it found it. A parapet. Two letters. *Brother, I am afraid —*

He shut it.

He did not know how. He shut it the way you slam a door on a draft, with the whole of his weight, and the hand was outside and the room was only a room again and he was standing with his two fingers jerked back against his chest and his heart going like a hammer on an anvil.

He stepped back out over the threshold.

"Caleth." Thane had a hand on his arm. When had Thane put a hand on his arm? "What happened?"

"I felt —" He made himself breathe. "A pulling. In my head." He looked at the wet on his fingertips and wiped it on his coat, hard. "In my memories. As though it wanted one of them."

"Did it get it?"

"No."

Thane looked at him a moment longer than he needed to. Then he let go of his arm and took a step back from the door, and Caleth saw him not like any of this, all the way down.

Caleth looked at the runes again. Not at their meaning. At their *shape* — the bands, the joins, the places where one line of the working closed into the next. He had watched a dwarf with ink on his fingertip do something to a page of runes in Vang's war room not an hour since, something no wizard he had ever met would have thought to do, and it had worked.

"I think," he said, "you could disable them."

Thane looked at him.

He looked at him for a long time. Caleth knew what he was asking. He was asking a man whose magic had just gone out like a candle at the threshold of this room to walk back into it, alone, and put his hands on the thing that had just tried to rob Caleth's head.

Thane sighed.

"Fine," he said. "Then get out of my way."

---

Caleth backed out into the robing room and watched him go in.

He went the way he went everywhere: slowly, looking at the floor first. He did not touch the walls. He walked the edge of the room with his hands at his sides and his head tilted back, reading the bands of runes the way he read a lock, and Caleth could see his lips moving and could not hear what he said. Then he stopped at the back wall, low down, near where the weeping ran into the lead. He bent over it.

He took a stick of charcoal out of a pocket. The kind a mason uses to mark a stone for cutting. He looked at it a moment, and then at the wall, and then — not at any rune, Caleth saw, but at the place *between* two of them, at the join — he drew a line.

Nothing happened. He drew another. Frowned. Rubbed one out with his thumb and drew it again, a hair to the left.

Behind Caleth, Korroc said very quietly, "What's he doing?"

"Breaking a sentence," said Caleth. "I think."

Thane worked. Caleth lost count of the marks. The dwarf moved along the back wall a hand's width at a time, crouched, and then on his knees, and then he went still with the charcoal lifted, looking at one place for so long that Caleth began to be afraid for him —

And put the mark down.

The room let go.

Caleth felt it like a held breath going out of a crowd. The hunger in the lead was simply *gone*, in an instant, as if it had never been there; the room was lighter, somehow, though no lamp had changed. The threads of water on the walls slowed, and stopped, and hung there in beads.

Thane sat back on his heels. "There," he said.

And the water on the floor began to rise.

---

It did not come down the walls. It came *up*, out of the lead at the foot of the walls where it had been going in, beads of it lifting off the floor and drawing together in the middle of the room as if the room were exhaling everything it had swallowed. A column of it. Taller than Caleth. Shaping.

Thane had both knives out before it was half made, and went at it.

It was a skeleton. Or the idea of one, put together out of wet and dark and bone — a tall, gaunt, horned thing, the long skull crowned with two great backswept horns, the ribs hanging open like a cage with the door left ajar. It held a staff in one long hand. The eye sockets lit, as Caleth watched, from nothing to a slow seething red, like coals somebody has just breathed on.

Thane's mother's blade went into it between two ribs and came out again with a sound like a chisel on bone.

"*Thanadaemon,*" said Caleth.

He knew it from a page. Not a demon; that was the first thing the page had said, and the second thing was that the difference mattered. Demons were the Abyss, all that hunger and appetite and noise. Daemons were something colder. Daemons were the things that wanted everything that lived to stop. This one, the page had said, ferried souls. Took them across. Kept some.

*It wanted one of them.*

He lifted his hand and said the words that send a thing home — that take hold of the thread an outsider has been pulled along into the world and simply *pull it back.* He felt the working go out of him and close on the thing.

It did not go.

It took the spell the way a rock takes rain, and turned its long skull, and looked at him with those two red coals. Not at Thane, who was cutting it. At him. And there was something in the look that he did not like at all: a slow, considering malice, as if the thing had felt his reaching hand and remembered that it had been inside his head a moment ago, and had liked it there.

Something went past him in the doorway like a gust of wind.

Nageru.

The monk crossed the robing room and came through the secret door in three strides and was in the inner room before the thanadaemon had finished turning its head, and his fists landed four times so fast it sounded like one blow, high and low and high and low, bone cracking on each. The thing *screamed* — a dry high sound, like wind through a broken shutter — and swung round on him.

Its staff came down. Once, twice, three times. Caleth heard every blow land.

And the creature whispered.

He did not catch the words. He felt them. Cold went through the room, through both rooms — not the cold of Caleth's own spells, which was a clean cold, a winter-morning cold, but something damp and old, the cold at the bottom of a well — and behind him, in the robing room, the dark thickened.

He turned.

There were two of them, between him and Korroc. They had not come through any door. They had simply gathered, out of the shadows under the robes and the corners of the cabinets, the way dusk gathers under trees: two man-shaped holes in the air, a deeper black than any shadow had a right to be, with long arms that ended in claws and great wings folded at their backs like a bat's. They had no faces. Where faces should have been there was only more dark, and somewhere in the dark, very faint, something that might have been the shape of a mouth.

*Shadow,* he thought. *Shadow given claws.*

The nearer one came for him.

He brought Radiance round. There was no room for it, in that doorway, with the lead behind him and the robes before; but the glaive did not need much room in his hands any more, and the white blade came up and across and met the first claw and turned it aside with a sound like nothing at all. And he came back round with it, low, and cut.

The blade went through.

It did not *bite*. That was the strange part. It went through the dark the way a hand goes through smoke, and the dark parted round it and closed behind it, and the thing staggered, and screamed, and was hurt — he felt Radiance know it was hurt, felt the bane in the steel wake and flare — but it was not cut, not the way a body is cut. There was nothing in there to cut.

The second claw came in under the blade and took him across the ribs.

It did not get through. It hit the stone of his skin and skidded off it, a dry scrape, the way a knife skids off a whetstone; the gray hard skin his own spell had laid over him before they came into this house took the whole of it, and gave back nothing but a thin whisper of cold, like a draft through a keyhole, that went into him and was gone.

Across the robing room the other one had gone for Korroc.

Caleth saw it in pieces, past the shoulder of his own. The dwarf's shield came up. The claws went at it, and found shield, and found shield again, and never once found Korroc. And then something happened to Korroc that Caleth had seen a dozen times and never stopped feeling in his own chest: the dwarf said one word in Dwarven, low, and the boots on his feet woke and he went from slow to quick; and he set his feet on the floor and *settled* into it, as if the stone of the Sanctum had come up into him through his soles and made itself at home. The hammer came round.

Four times. Once, it found something. Three times, the dark simply flowed out of its way like water round a stone, and closed again.

Korroc said something in Dwarven that Caleth, who spoke it, chose not to translate even in the privacy of his own head.

In the inner room, Thane's knives were going into the thanadaemon. Three times. Bone chipped. The thing did not seem to notice him much. It was looking at Nageru, and at Caleth.

*Enough.*

He reached down inside himself.

Not for a spell. He had spent the one he wanted, on the soldiers in the room with the dummies, two rooms and an hour ago; it was gone from his memory the way a word goes out of your head in the middle of a sentence. But there was another place in him, under the place where spells were kept. A place the wardstone had lit, a year ago, in the fire at Kenabres, and left burning. He put his hand into that fire and *pulled*, and the spell came back up out of it into his memory as clean and whole as if he had never cast it at all.

He pointed past Nageru's shoulder, at the middle of everything, and let it go.

The fireball burst between the rooms.

Fire went everywhere, into the lead room and the robing room together, a great orange bloom of it that blew the robes off their pegs and set them burning in the air. It washed over Nageru. Over Thane, in the back corner. Over Korroc in the outer room and over Caleth himself in the doorway, and none of them so much as flinched, because he had laid the old ward against fire over all of them before they ever set foot in this house, and it was still holding. It took the heat and turned it to a warm wind.

The demons and the daemon took it all.

They reeled. The two shadows went thin and ragged at the edges like burned paper. The thanadaemon's red eyes flared and its whole frame shook. But none of the three went down.

Nageru did not wait to see if they would.

He was already moving, in the lead room, inside the reach of the staff. Four blows. Caleth saw the last one land, square in the middle of the open cage of the ribs, and the thanadaemon came apart.

Not like the demons came apart. It did not go thin and black like ink in water. It went *wet* — fell into itself, all at once, back into the clear water it had been made of, and splashed across the lead floor, and ran away into nothing.

And the dark came down.

---

He did not see it come. One moment there were burning robes and the shapes of his friends; the next there was nothing. Not night. Night had stars in it, and edges. This was a black so whole that he lifted his hand in front of his face and could not tell whether he had lifted it.

He heard Korroc swear. *The dwarves can't see either,* he thought, and the thought frightened him more than the dark did.

A claw found him. He felt it hit the stone of his skin, and skid, and the thin breath of cold come in behind it.

Across the robing room something hit Korroc. Caleth heard the grunt — a deep one, not the grunt Korroc gave for a scratch — and felt it too, a moment later, faint, down the warm thread the dwarf kept running between all of them. Cold. A great deal of cold, more than had reached Caleth, as though the shadow had found a seam in the dwarf and poured itself in.

"*Incoming!*" Korroc roared.

The fire came down out of the ceiling.

Caleth saw it — the only thing in the world he could see, a column of gold and white light falling straight down through the dark onto the robing room like a spear thrown from the top of a tower. It roared. It struck. And it should have struck all of them, the way the dwarf's holy fire always struck everything under it; but it did not. Caleth felt the fire wash past him and *part*, the holy part of it, like a river round a rock. It knew him. It went round him, and round Nageru and Korroc and the woman in the hall, and only the heat came on to them, and his ward drank the heat.

The shadows screamed. Both of them. A sound like silk tearing.

In the corner of the lead room Thane said something, a short prayer, and Caleth felt the spell go out of him, and nothing happened. No light. The dark ate it.

*This is not darkness,* Caleth thought. *This is a working. And a working can be answered.*

He found the wand by touch. Third from the left, on his belt, the one with the smooth bone grip. He knew it by its weight, and he drew it and lifted it over his head and spoke the word.

The dark broke.

Not slowly. All at once, like a dropped bowl: light, sudden and white and total, as if somebody had taken the roof off the Sanctum and let the noon sun of the summer at Kenabres pour straight down into the room. Every shadow in the place went flat and small and crept back under the things it belonged to.

Except two.

The two shadow-things were still there, in the robing room, solid black against the white light, between him and Korroc — and the one nearest him came at him at once, as though the light had stung it into fury. Claw, claw, bite. He felt all three hit. He felt the stone take all three, and the cold come in behind each like a draft. Across the room the other one went at Korroc again and found shield, and shield.

He was in the inner room. He had been backed into it. He saw that now, in the light: the doorway, the shadow filling it, Thane behind him in the lead room with nowhere to go either. Nageru, past the shadow, out of reach.

Korroc swung his hammer.

He was still fast, still rooted in the floor, and the hammer came round three times and Caleth heard all three land, *thud, thud, thud*, like a man beating a rug — and Korroc's shadow went to rags and blew away.

Caleth thrust Radiance at his own. Missed. Thrust again, and the white blade went into the dark and the bane in it flared, and the thing screamed and did not go.

He tried to step past it.

It was a small thing, the slide. He had done it a hundred times. Not a fold, not the great tearing journey across a country; just a shimmer, a single step taken *sideways* through the world, so that he would come out on the far side of the shadow in the robing room with the room to swing. He felt himself begin to do it. He felt the world thin.

And then it did not. It was like stepping onto a stair in the dark and finding the stair was not there; his whole body lurched, braced for a step that never came, and he was standing exactly where he had been, in the doorway of a lead room, with a shadow in his face.

*Locked,* he thought. Not this room. The working that stopped him had not come from the lead. It was wider than that. It was in the stone. *The whole house is locked.*

He filed it. He would need it later.

And with the second shadow gone, there was suddenly room — and Nageru was in it. Caleth did not see him start. He saw him go *past*, a tumble and a roll along the floor under the shadow's wings, out of the inner room and up again on the far side of it with his fists already moving. The first blow missed. The aasimar shifted his feet a hand's width, and the next three did not.

The shadow screamed, and went thin, and was not there.

---

The light from the wand lay over everything, flat and white and merciless. It showed the robing room with its robes burned to black rags on the floor, and the two cabinets scorched, and the smoke of the fire hanging under the ceiling. It showed Korroc, breathing hard, with a gray tinge to his face from the cold that had got into him. It showed Arueshalae in the hall door with an arrow on her string, unharmed, watching the place where the last shadow had been as though she expected it to come back.

It showed the lead room behind him: small, sheeted, scratched with runes gone dead, and a few beads of water drying on the floor.

And it showed the shelves.

He had not looked at them before. There had not been time. There were stone shelves cut into the back wall of the lead room, three of them, deep, running the whole width of it — and they were full.

Caleth stepped into the room and went to them.

Behind him Thane said, "What do you see?"

Caleth reached up.

"Those shelves," said Thane slowly, "are empty."

Caleth stopped with his hand in the air. He turned his head and looked at the dwarf, who was looking at him with the expression of a man watching his friend reach for nothing on a bare shelf in a demon's house; and Nageru, beside him, with a faint polite frown; and Korroc, coming to the secret door, wiping his face.

He laughed. He could not help it.

"No," he said. "They're not."

Thane looked at the shelves. He looked at Caleth. And then he snorted, through his nose, a long disgusted sound.

"Of course they're not," he said. "Deception on top of deception. Why would anything in this house be what it looks like?"

"Remember," said Caleth, "I can see what is invisible."

He watched Thane remember. It had not been so long ago: not long after the banner went up over Drezen, Caleth had laid two workings on himself that did not end. One let him see in the dark as well as any dwarf. The other let him see what other people had hidden from sight. Expensive, both of them, and permanent, and he had not regretted the cost.

The dwarf nodded slowly. "So what's on there?"

Caleth looked along the shelves.

"Armor," he said. "Chests. Weapons." He paused. "It looks like a trophy room. To be honest."

It did. It looked like Vang's box of trophies in the bottom of the sarcophagus, made larger, and made careful. Things laid out on the stone in rows, each with its own space round it, the way a collector lays out the things he is proudest of. He reached up and took hold of the nearest — a breastplate, cold, heavier than it looked — and as soon as his hands closed on it, it was *there*, for the others: a shimmer in the air, and then a suit of armor in his arms.

He looked down at it.

Full plate, every piece of it, and not steel. The metal had the pale silvery gleam that only one metal has, the color of moonlight on water: mithral. Light, for what it was. Light enough to wear all day. There were old marks on it. Dents, polished out and not quite gone.

Radiance moved.

He had laid the glaive in the crook of his arm to take the armor down, and he felt it — not with his hands; somewhere under his breastbone, where the blade had lived since it chose him. A warmth. A single slow pulse, the way the blade had pulsed once at the gates of Drezen, where she fell.

He knew it then. He did not need anything else.

"Yaniel's armor," he said.

Korroc's head came up. Thane's.

"Yaniel," said Korroc. "The — your sword's —"

"Yes."

Caleth stood holding it. The woman who had carried Radiance before him, half a century ago, through the Fourth Crusade to the gates of the citadel his friends had taken back. She had fallen at those gates with the sword in her hand. The sword had been lost and found and stolen and found again, and come at last to him. He had not once, in a year of carrying it, thought to wonder where the rest of her had gone.

Here. On a shelf, under a spell, in a lead box, in the house of a thing made of worms, kept like a butterfly on a pin.

*He keeps trophies,* Korroc had said, an hour ago, over a box.

He set the armor down very gently on the floor of the lead room, and reached up for the next thing.

---

It went on a long time.

A shield. A sword with a broken guard and a dull red stone in the pommel. A helm with the crest shorn off. Chests, small and heavy, that Thane went over for traps before anyone opened them. Each thing he took down came into being in his hands for the others, one after another, so that it began to look, he thought, like a conjuror's act at a fair: the half-elf reaching into nothing and drawing out a crusade.

And then, a little way along, on the lowest shelf, books.

Many books. A whole shelf of them, end to end, a long double row of spines in leather and board and vellum, and he knelt in front of them and took one down and opened it, and stopped.

He knew the marks inside the cover. Not his uncle's moons. The marks of a house rather than a man, stamped small in faded ink on the flyleaf, and symbols beside them that any student of the academy in Kenabres would know anywhere.

He took down another. The same marks. Another. The same, and a shelfmark written under it in a fine small hand.

"These," he said, and found his voice was not quite steady, and made it so. "These are the books that were stolen from the Blackwing Library."

He went along the shelf. The spines blurred. *The Blackwing.* The library Vang had destroyed, according to a dossier in an elf's careful hand; the library he had somehow commanded demons to break. And Caleth knew what none of the others knew, what he had known since a cave under Kenabres and a half-blind elf looking at him too long in the dark: that the Blackwing had been more than a library. That it had belonged to the Riftwardens. That the people who kept these books, and wrote these shelfmarks, had been his mother's people and his father's.

He looked back along the shelves at the rest of it. The armor. The shield. The chests.

"A great deal of this," he said slowly, "was taken from the Blackwing Library."

Nobody asked him how he knew. He was grateful.

He did not look for names. He wanted to — he felt the want rise in him, very strong, to sit down on the floor of that lead room and take every book off the shelf and open every one, looking for two names in a fine small hand. He did not. Not here. Not with the house still standing round them and its master somewhere in it.

*Later.* He had been saying *later* a great deal today. Thane, he suspected, had noticed.

It all went into the bag. All of it — the armor, carefully, each piece; the shield and the swords and the chests; the books, an armful at a time, Caleth's hands slower with those than with anything else. All but one thing. Near the end, standing on its own at the end of the top shelf, there was a staff, plain pale wood bound in silver, and when he took it down and it came into the light Korroc said, *"Oh,"* in a voice Caleth had never heard him use before, and held out his hand for it.

"Healing," said the dwarf, turning it over. "There's healing in this. A lot of it." He looked up. "I'll carry it. If nobody minds."

Nobody minded. He slung it across his back beside his shield, and looked, Caleth thought, about as pleased as he had looked over the master's tools.

The lead room was a dead end. There was nothing past it but lead. They went back out through the robing room, through the smoke and the burned rags, back down the servants' passage to the wide hallway outside the forge; and stood there a moment, the five of them, looking up and down it.

There was only one way left.

---

The stair went down off the north hallway, through the first pair of doors, the way it had gone down an hour ago when they had decided to leave it. Cold air came up it. Thane went to the top and stood there a moment, listening, and then did the thing he did that Caleth had never been able to see the trick of: he went down into the dark and became part of it.

Caleth followed, a flight behind, with the others at his back.

It was not far. One flight, turning once, and then a landing. Not a new floor at all; only a broad open place where the stair stopped for breath, with a wide opening on the far side of it, and through the opening, light.

The floor of the landing was tiled in basalt. Black, polished, each tile cut with one small piece of a maze, so that the tiles together made a great labyrinth across the whole floor, and the eye that tried to follow any one path through it got lost in three steps. Columns held up the ceiling, sheathed in ivory, and every inch of the ivory was carved. Mazes. Bull-headed men in the mazes. And men and women, naked, twisting round the columns in such a way that it took a moment to see whether what was on their faces was pleasure or pain, and a moment longer to understand that the carver had meant you to be unable to tell.

Caleth stepped onto the first black tile, and his shoulder caught fire.

He did not cry out. He nearly did. It went through him from the left shoulder blade, hard and sudden, the way a nail goes through a board — the Spiral, under his coat and his shirt and the stone of his skin, *burning*, not the slow ache of the dreams but a pulse, and another, like something knocking on a door from the inside. His hand went to it before he could stop it. He stood on the black tile with his hand clamped over his own shoulder and his teeth together, and breathed.

He felt Korroc feel it.

The warm thread between them pulled, once, like a hand on a rope — and he heard the dwarf, behind him on the stair, stop. Heard his breath go out. He did not turn round. He could feel the dwarf's eyes on the back of his neck, and Nageru's, and he did not turn round.

Nobody said anything.

He took his hand off his shoulder. It took more than it should have.

*He's here,* he thought. *He's close.* He did not know how he knew. He had not been sure, in a year of dreams, whether the mark was a door or a window or only a wound, and he was not sure now. But it knew. Whatever it was, it knew, and it was telling him so.

Ahead, Thane had crept to the edge of the opening. He was flat against the near column, at the edge of the light, easing his head round the edge of the carved ivory to look.

And a voice spoke.

"Ah," it said. "They sent the quiet one first."

Caleth's skin crawled under the stone.

It was not loud. It was conversational, even pleasant, the voice of a man remarking on the weather from a window. But it was *wrong*. It had too many edges, as though it were being made by several mouths at once and none of them quite agreed on the shape of the words, and under it, very faint, there was a wet soft sound, like a great many small things moving against one another in a small space.

He had heard that sound before. In water. In a scrying, a month and more ago, with a beaten elf in a cage.

Thane turned his head and looked back up at them.

Caleth had seen Thane's face do a great many things in a year. He had never seen it do this. It was not fear, quite. It was the face of a man who has put his hand on a door in the dark, and felt someone on the other side of it, holding it shut.

Korroc was moving before Caleth was. Caleth went down after him.

"No more games," said the voice, as they came down. Patient. Amused. "Come on out. Let's talk."

Caleth reached the landing, and stopped beside Thane, and looked at the opening, and the light beyond it.

*Twenty-five years,* he thought. Not quite. Twenty-five years, less a few weeks, since a man and a woman had walked into the Worldwound and not walked out.

"Looks like this is it," he said, under his breath. "Let's go."

---

They stepped out together, all five of them, into the light.

The stair did not end on the landing. It turned, at a right angle, and went down again, a short second flight of a few broad steps, into a hall so long Caleth's eye could not take it in at once. It ran away to his right and to his left like the nave of a temple. To the left, toward the front of the Sanctum, it opened into a great dim entrance chamber, empty. To the right, through a high arch, it went on into a vaster room still, lit red by braziers, where at the far end something enormous and pale stood up out of the gloom on a dais.

And in front of him, at the foot of the short stair, in the middle hall between the two, they were waiting.

Two templars at the foot of the steps, in black half-plate, with glaives. Behind them, two great shapes that took Caleth a moment to understand: huge, hunched, bull-headed, with horns as long as his arm and red eyes and hides gone dark and scaled and wrong — minotaurs, of the kind they had fought in Staunton's hall, fiend-blooded, each of them half again the height of a man. And between the minotaurs, side by side, as easy as two guests at a dinner, a woman and a man.

The woman was Jerribeth.

He knew her face. He had seen it in a cavern at the Molten Scar, over his own blade, while she bled on it. Beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful, every proportion doing what it ought; and furious. She was looking at him the way he imagined he would have looked at her, if he had been a different kind of man. She was not hiding it.

The man beside her wore robes.

Long robes, dark, the hood up. And the robes *moved.* Not with any wind. All over, continuously, a slow restless shifting of the cloth from within, as though there were something under it that never stopped settling, never quite found its shape, rolled and slid and shifted and rolled. The hood was turned a little away. Under it there was only darkness.

Caleth looked, as he had known for a year that he would look, at the floor beneath the man's feet.

He could not tell. The braziers were behind, and the light was red and moving, and the hem of the robe was moving too much for any shadow to keep still under it. He looked, and he could not tell, and it frightened him more than if he could have.

Two more templars, far back, in the red light of the temple room behind. Four, then. Two minotaurs. Jerribeth.

And the thing in the robes.

"Ah," it said, and the hood turned, not toward Caleth — toward the back of their little group, toward the woman with the bow. "You've brought the broken one with you."

Arueshalae did not move. Caleth did not look round at her. He heard her breathing behind him change, and that was all.

"I do not appreciate," said the man in the robes, "you breaking into my home."

"No different," said Caleth, "than you breaking into ours."

He heard his own voice come out level, and was surprised by it. He had imagined this — he had imagined it in a thousand ways, on a thousand nights, in the dark of every camp since a page in an elf's handwriting in Drezen. He had imagined shouting. He had imagined not being able to speak at all. He had not imagined that his voice would sound like his own, measured, a little dry, the voice he used across a table.

The hood tilted. The sound under the voice shifted, wetly, like a man smiling.

"We could come to an arrangement," it said. "Though I have *so* many questions. For each of you." It took a step forward, to the top of the short flight, and the robes rolled over themselves. "You destroyed the wardstone. One would think that would have killed you. And yet here you stand in front of me — *different.*" A pause. "How is that?"

Caleth thought of the fire under Kenabres. The four of them in it, and the stone breaking, and the light going into them without asking.

"The will of the wardstone," he said.

The hood turned toward Jerribeth.

She stood very straight. She was seething. Caleth could see it in the line of her jaw, the hands at her sides, the way her eyes had not left him since they came down the stair.

"*These* are the ones," said the man in the robes, as though he were only now putting it together, and found it delightful, "who took your crusaders away from you?" He turned back. "Then I think we can come to an arrangement after all. I will stop. I will. If one of you will stay here with me, and —"

"*No.*"

It came out of Caleth like a blade out of a sheath.

He had known. He had known which one of them the thing meant before it had finished the word *one*. He felt it in the mark, burning, knocking, a pulse with every word the thing said. *Another interesting specimen.* He was not going to stand on these steps and let it finish the sentence.

"You will stop," he said, "because you will die."

The robes went still for a moment. Entirely still. It was the first time.

"So confident," said the voice softly. "Aren't you?"

The pain in Caleth's shoulder was a nail now, a red-hot nail being driven in by slow blows, every one landing a little deeper. He did not touch it. He kept his hands where they were, his right on Radiance and his left at his belt, and he did not touch it, and he did not look away.

The man in the robes reached up, unhurried, and pushed back his hood.

There was no face.

There was a head, the shape of a head, and it was made of worms. Pale, finger-long, slick, thousands of them, knotted and coiling and sliding over one another in a slow ceaseless writhe, so that the shape of the skull held but nothing in it ever held still. And in the middle of the mass, where eyes should have been, two small points of red light. Steady. The only steady things in it.

They looked at Caleth.

Caleth looked back. He felt Thane, at his elbow, go rigid. He felt Korroc behind him say something under his breath that might have been a prayer. He felt Nageru not move at all.

"So," said Caleth, "you don't wish to concede?"

The worms rippled. It was laughter. He was sure of it.

"Oh, *no,*" said Xanthir Vang. "Come on, my boy. No. No."

*My boy.*

Something in Caleth went very cold and very clear, the way water goes clear when the mud in it finally settles.

He moved.

---

His left hand closed on the Pauper's Thighbone at his belt, and the gold in it answered, one of the nine small fires he kept there; and he spoke a word and swept his hand back over his friends, and the world went slow round them — no. *They* went quick. He felt it take hold of all of them at once, Korroc and Thane and Nageru and the woman with the bow and himself, every heart beating a little faster, every limb a little lighter, the air going thick as honey round everything that was not them.

His hand left the Thighbone for the wand beside it. Short, black, cold, the one that did not cast anything itself but made whatever he cast through it *more.* He took it in his fist.

And reached down, past the place where his spells were kept, into the fire the wardstone had left.

The cold came out of him in a cone.

It was not frost. It was winter, the deep middle of it, the cold of the far north on the longest night, poured out of his outstretched hand in a widening roar of white across the foot of the stair; and the wardstone's fire went into it and drove it on, and the wand made it more; and the two minotaurs and the woman between them were *inside* it, and then they were not anything at all but three shapes of ice.

Solid. Rimed white to the horns. Jerribeth's furious face, behind a sheet of clear ice, looking at him still.

Behind him Nageru was already gone.

Caleth did not see him go. He felt the air move at his back, and turned his head enough to see the monk across the hall at the arch of the temple room, where one of the two far templars had started forward — and the templar had not finished starting. Four blows. The man folded up on the black floor like a dropped coat.

Thane, at Caleth's elbow, was not at Caleth's elbow. There was a shimmer in the air where he had been, and then nothing; and a heartbeat later one of the templars at the foot of the stair jerked, and a long red wound opened down his side from nothing, and he staggered.

And Vang lifted one hand of worms, and spoke, and was gone.

Gone to the others. Not to Caleth.

Caleth saw him. He saw the shimmer close round the robed shape, and the shape go on standing there for a moment, perfectly clear to him and to him alone, the worms of its face turning up toward the ceiling. Then it rose. It went straight up into the air with the robes rolling about it like smoke, up past the tops of the columns, into the shadows of the vaulted roof, and hung there, and turned, and looked down at them all.

*He doesn't know I can see him.*

The thought was so cold and so clean that it frightened him.

Vang pointed down. A thin green ray came out of the worms of his hand, no thicker than a reed, and struck Korroc full in the chest.

Caleth felt it through the bond. Not the pain — Korroc took that — but the *shape* of it, the horrible shape. It was not a burn and not a cut. It was a spell that took things *apart*: that went into a man and found the joins between the smallest parts of him and asked them, very politely, to let go of one another. He felt Korroc refuse. Felt the dwarf set his feet on the stone and simply *decline* to come apart, the way a mountain declines; and the ray went into him and found nothing that would answer, or almost nothing — and Korroc staggered, and grunted, and stayed exactly where he was, and was hurt.

"Korroc," said Caleth.

"*Fine,*" said the dwarf through his teeth. "Get on with it."

He got on with it.

He shut his eyes for one half-breath and found the other thing, the thing he had learned to do since the wardstone: to reach into the fire as it left him and turn it. Every bit of power that came off his hands from now until the end of the fight, cold or lightning or acid, would leave him as *fire*. He felt the air shimmer round him with it. Then his hand found the wand again, and he gathered the spell he wanted and leaned on it, hard, with all his will behind it, to make it bite.

And let it go.

The fire went out from his hand like a whip. It struck Jerribeth first — straight into the ice that held her, and through it — and then it *leapt*, the way the spell was made to leap, from her to the first minotaur, from the first to the second, up into the shadows of the roof to the place where Vang hung invisible, and down again, and on, from templar to templar, every enemy in that long hall, one after another, a single chain of fire strung between them all like a burning rope.

The ice went to pieces.

It exploded off the three of them in a cloud of steam and splinters, and the two minotaurs, freed of it, swayed on their great hooves for a moment with their red eyes rolling. Then they went down. One, and the other, falling forward onto the black floor like felled trees, and did not get up.

Jerribeth did not go down.

She stood in the steam with the ice running off her, and she looked at Caleth, and she smiled. It was not a beautiful smile.

Down at the foot of the stair the wounded templar had spun round toward the place his blood had come from, and swung his glaive at the empty air — and by luck or skill or some god's malice it found something. Caleth heard Thane's grunt. Felt a little of it come down the warm thread, faint, and saw a thin spatter of red appear on the black floor out of nowhere.

And Jerribeth began to grow.

---

It began in her shoulders. They broadened, and kept broadening, and the pale skin of them darkened and thickened and split along lines like the joins in plate armor. Her arms lengthened. There were more of them. Two great arms came out of her sides below the first, and the first pair stretched and swelled and their hands bent back and hardened and split apart into two great pincers, each one longer than a man's leg. Her face pushed outward into a muzzle and filled with teeth. And she went up, and up, ten feet, twelve, more, until she stood over the foot of the stair in the steam and the firelight like something out of the bottom of a well: a vast dog-headed horror with four arms, two clawed and two ending in those great pincers, and the last of the lovely woman sloughing off her like a skin.

Behind Caleth, very flatly, Korroc said:

"Huh. Not a succubus."

"*Definitely,*" said Caleth, "not a succubus."

It came for him.

It was fast, for something that size. It came up the short stair in two strides, and the great jaws snapped at his face, and he was not there — he had gone a half-step left, the small step his body had learned to take before his mind asked it to, the step that looks like nothing. A claw came at him and he went inside it. The second claw found him. Hit him square in the ribs and drove him half a pace back, and the stone of his skin took it with a crack like a flagstone splitting. Then the pincers. The first came at his head and he ducked it, and felt it close above him with a sound like a pair of shears the size of a door. The second took him around the body.

He felt the stone hold. He felt it begin to give, the gray hard skin his own spell had laid on him before they came into this house, crushing in; and then the pincer opened, and he was free, and hurt — a little, not much, through all that stone.

And then less. The warm thread pulled, and half of what had got through went down it, to Korroc, and was so small by the time it got there that it simply went away into the bond like a drop of wine into a river.

Behind him he heard Korroc say a word in Dwarven, and felt the dwarf settle into the stone of the floor again, and then something else — a prayer, deep, short, the kind Korroc said to Torag and nobody else — and a faint shimmer went up around the dwarf like heat off a summer road. A shield of his faith, Korroc called it. Caleth had heard him call it that, once, without any embarrassment at all.

"*Caleth,*" said Korroc. Quiet. Urgent. "The ceiling. Left of the third column."

Caleth looked. Vang was there. Of course he was there. But Korroc could not see him.

"How —"

"I can *feel* him." The dwarf's voice had gone strange. "I can feel every living thing round me, and there's — Torag, Caleth, there's *thousands* of them. Up there. All in one place." A pause. "That's him. That's what he is."

*He knows,* Caleth thought. *Good. Then there are two of us.*

The floor opened.

It opened under him and under Korroc together, without a sound, a black square of nothing where the basalt maze had been; and Caleth felt himself drop and threw himself sideways and his boot found the edge and he was on the tiles again, on his hands, with the edge of a pit gaping beside him that went down further than the light went. Korroc had thrown himself the other way. He was on the far lip of it, on one knee, and Caleth saw his face and knew what it had cost: the dwarf had torn himself up out of the stone to do it, broken the root he had sunk into the floor, and it had come out of him all at once. He looked suddenly gray and old and tired, the way a man looks after a day and a night at the forge.

"Up," said Korroc to himself, and got up.

Away behind them all, in the temple room, something enormous moved.

---

Caleth did not see it at first. He felt Nageru's surprise — not down the bond; the bond did not carry surprise; he simply *knew* it, the way you know a friend has stopped walking behind you. He looked round, past the giant pincered thing coming for him again, through the arch into the great red room.

It was vast. It ran away under a vaulted ceiling painted dark blue and sprinkled with stars, down a long walkway between rows of black columns speckled white like a starling's breast, to a dais at the very back; and on the dais, twenty-five feet high, ivory, pale as bone in the light of two great braziers burning either side of it, a goat-headed man with a glaive in his hand. The glaive's blade was a crescent moon.

Caleth knew whose image it was. He had seen it on a wall upstairs, in an ivory room, in a carving no taller than himself. It had been bad enough at that size.

And out from behind it, out from behind the curtains that hung red as blood on either side of the dais, three demons came.

They were big. Bigger than men, bigger than the minotaurs had been, broad and heavy and blue — a deep bruised blue, like a drowned man's lips — with great curling horns and long arms that ended in claws like a bear's. They came down off the dais at a lope.

One went straight at Nageru, and its claws took him twice before he had got his feet set. One went for Arueshalae, who had gone forward to the arch of the temple room to shoot, and Caleth saw her twist away from the first blow and not the second, and blood come out along her arm.

The third went for Nageru too. It bit him. Caleth saw the head go down and the horns come up into the monk's side, and Nageru went back a step.

And Jerribeth came at Caleth again.

Claw. He saw it coming and turned it on Radiance's haft, a long ringing parry that jarred his arm to the shoulder — and came back off the parry with the point, the way the blade wanted to go, and drove it into her.

Deep. All the way in, a foot of white steel into the hard dark hide below her ribs, and the bane in it woke and *flared*, white, he felt it, like a struck bell. It knew what she was. It had always known what she was. It had known in the cavern at the Molten Scar.

She did not even slow down. Both pincers came for him at once.

He went back. One long step, off the top of the stair, out of their reach — and they closed on the air where he had been with that great shearing sound; and the muzzle came down after him instead, too fast, and the teeth took him in the shoulder. The left shoulder. Right over the Spiral.

He did not scream. The stone took most of it. But the teeth found the place where the mark was already burning, and for a moment the two pains were one pain and the world went white at the edges.

He saw Nageru look up.

The monk was in the temple room with two blue demons on him and blood down his side. He looked across the hall, past them, at Caleth on the stair with a giant's jaws in his shoulder. Caleth saw him decide. It took no time at all.

Nageru went *down*. Not falling: rolling, under the reach of the nearest blue demon, along the black floor between its legs and out the other side, and up, and running. Across the floor of the hall. Up the short stair. And his fists came up as he ran, and they were lit — a clean hard holy light, gold and white, the color of a summer sunrise, pouring off his knuckles like water off a millwheel.

"*You will not get away again!*"

Caleth had never heard Nageru shout. Not once, in a year.

The first blow took her in the side, where Radiance had gone in. The second. Each one flared as it landed, the holy light going into her like a smith's hammer into hot iron, white sparks of it bursting off her hide. Three. Four. The giant thing let go of Caleth's shoulder and turned on the monk, the pincers swinging round —

Five.

Six.

She came apart.

Not slowly. She went thin, all of her at once, the whole vast dog-headed mass of her going gray and then black and then translucent, like ink poured into running water; and for one instant Caleth saw, inside the dark of her, the shape of a beautiful furious woman, looking at him. Then even that went, and there was nothing on the stair but a smell of scorched hair and Nageru, standing where she had been with his fists still alight, breathing hard.

*Gone,* Caleth thought. *Not dead. They don't die here. Sent home, and barred from coming back for a hundred years.*

A hundred years. He would be an old man. Nageru would be dead. It would have to do.

"Thank you," he said.

Nageru did not answer. He was already turning back toward the temple room, where Arueshalae was.

---

Across the hall, out of nowhere, chains came for the monk.

Caleth saw them form — iron, black, cold, coming out of the air round Nageru's wrists and ankles and closing — and saw Nageru simply not be there when they closed. The shackles snapped shut on air and fell clattering and were gone.

Caleth looked up.

Vang was moving. Gliding back through the air under the vault of the hall, away from them, back toward the dim entrance chamber at the front of the Sanctum, further than where he had stood to greet them; and as he went he turned his face of worms back over his shoulder and spoke one more word, and lifted one hand.

The cloud came up out of the floor all round them.

It was pale and thin and it smelled of nothing at all, and the moment it touched him Caleth felt his life begin to go out of him into it. Not a wound. A slow draining, like a cup with a crack in it; he felt it in his hands first, and then his knees, and then behind his eyes, the warmth going out of him into that thin gray air. He heard Korroc grunt, and felt the bond pull — once, twice, three times, everyone in it bleeding into the cloud and half of all of it running down the thread to the dwarf.

Caleth did not stop to think. He ran.

Not away. Forward — out of the cloud, down off the stair, across the black tiles of the middle hall, under the place where the thing hung in the air, toward the entrance chamber. Toward Vang.

His left hand found the Thighbone again. The gold answered. He flung out his hand and spoke, and a fistful of light went up from it into the air before the hanging robes — a burst of glittering dust, gold, sparkling, a thousand bright motes of it that hung in the air and clung to everything they touched.

They touched Vang.

He came into being for all of them — there, in the air, the robed shape suddenly *visible* in a glittering coat of gold, every worm of him picked out in sparks like frost on a spiderweb. The red points of his eyes were full of it. Caleth saw the head jerk back. Saw a hand of worms come up, clawing at the place where the eyes were, as if to scrape them clear.

*Blind.*

Caleth's hand went to the wand. He reached down through himself — past the spells, past the fire, to the other thing. The thing that was not a wizard's at all. The thing a paladin of Shelyn has when he looks at evil and names it to her face. He felt it rise in him, hot and gold and clean, and go into the spell as it left him.

Three rays of fire, one after the other, from his outstretched hand.

They went into Vang one, two, three. Into the glittering robes, into the coiling worms of the chest, and the smite went in with them, and the fire *found* him. Caleth heard him make a sound for the first time — not words. A high, wet, many-voiced shriek, like a nest of something disturbed in the dark.

Behind him, in the cloud, one of the templars came stumbling out toward the stair, coughing, trying to get clear of it — and ran straight onto a knife held by nobody at all. Caleth heard the sound. He did not look round. The templar fell.

Korroc came out of the cloud.

He came out slow, gray-faced, bleeding from more places than Caleth wanted to count, and fetched up beside him, at his left shoulder, and stood there a moment just breathing. Then he put his hand flat on his own chest and gold went through him, and Caleth saw his shoulders come up. He set his boots on the basalt and *sank* into it, the root going down again, the stone coming up. And he turned round, toward the temple room, and threw his open hand out after Nageru as if he were throwing a stone, and a wash of gold went across the hall from it and hit the monk in the back, and Caleth saw half the blood stop.

From the far end of the temple room Arueshalae cried out.

Caleth looked. She had gone up — she had tried to; her wings were open, and she had gone up off the floor toward the starry ceiling to get clear of the blue demons and shoot down on them. One of them had her by the ankle. It had dragged her down out of the air, and the other was coming in, and she had her bow up across her body like a staff and was fighting with that.

Nageru reached them. His fists flared. He hit the first one so hard it went off its feet, and again before it landed, and it was gone before it hit the floor; and he turned on the second, the one that had her, and his fist found it once —

And Thane, out of the cloud, out of nowhere, came into sight at the foot of the stair with his bow in his hand.

He had the arrow on the string. *The* arrow — the one from the platinum box, white-fletched, the head that held the light. He drew. Caleth saw him take a breath, the way Thane did before anything that mattered, and hold it, and loose.

It missed.

It went past the drifting glittering shape of Vang by a hand's width and on into the dark of the entrance chamber, and was gone; and Thane lowered the bow and said one word in Dwarven, and the word was not a polite one.

And Vang landed.

---

He came down out of the air in front of Caleth, right in front of him, the robes settling round him in a wet heavy slither, still glittering, the worms of his face still scraping at the dust in his eyes. Close enough to touch. Close enough that Caleth could smell him: old paper, and something bitter, and under it rot. He had smelled that in a dream.

Caleth did not wait for him to see.

Radiance was long. That was the thing about a glaive. He did not have to step in. He drove it straight out from where he stood, all his weight behind it, into the middle of the coiling chest —

And it went through.

Through. All the way. There was nothing to stop it. The worms simply *moved*, parted round the white blade as it went in the way water parts round an oar, and slid along the steel, and closed again behind it, so that Radiance stood out a foot from the thing's back and there was no wound anywhere at all.

He let go of what was in it.

He had put it there in Drezen, on the Purity Forge, Thane's channels and his own spell: a stroke of lightning, laid in the steel and waiting. He let it go now, into the worms, and as it went the turning he had laid on himself caught it — and it did not leave him as lightning. It did not leave him as fire either. It came out of the blade as *cold*. Deep, killing cold, all down the length of Radiance at once, into everything it touched.

*Cold is not the road,* he had thought, once, in a cavern. He had been wrong. It had only been the wrong road for her.

The worms froze where they touched the steel. Hundreds of them. Went white and stiff and brittle along the blade.

And the rest came *for him*.

They left the shape of the man. All of a sudden, all at once, as though a word had been spoken; the robes collapsed and the worms poured out of them and up along Radiance toward his hands, a pale writhing river of them, over the white steel, over the hilt, over his fingers. Up his wrists. Up his arms. They were cold and slick and they were *everywhere*, and they were in his collar and on his neck and climbing his face, and he could feel them, thousands of them, each one small and soft and hungry, moving with a single purpose over the stone of his skin, feeling for a way in. Feeling for his mouth. For his eyes.

For the place on his left shoulder blade where the mark was burning.

He heard Korroc shout. He heard Thane.

He could not see. They were over his eyes. He could hear them, the soft wet sliding of them all over him, the sound he had heard in a dream on a night at Sesker's Gully. And under it, in it, out of all of those small mouths at once, very close to his ear:

*Interesting.*

His left hand found the Thighbone.

He did not need to see it. He knew where it was the way he knew where his own heart was. The gold in it was low; he had spent it on haste and on glittering dust and on a dozen folds over a dozen days. He put his hand flat on it, inside the writhing mass of worms, and *pushed* — the thing he had never been able to explain to an elf on a forge floor, the knack, the reaching into an emptied place and filling it. He felt the gold come up in the bone under his palm, warm, one rune and another waking. And under that, deeper, the wardstone's fire. He took hold of that too.

He opened his mouth. A worm went in over his lip. He spat it out.

"You killed my father," said Caleth Aranor.

The worms went still. Just for a moment. All of them.

"You killed my mother."

And he made himself the center of the fire.

---

It came out of him.

Not out of his hand. Out of *him* — out of his chest, out of his whole body at once, the way the light had come out of the wardstone a year ago with him standing in it. A great white-gold burst of fire with Caleth Aranor at its heart, roaring out in every direction at once.

He felt it go through him. He felt it, the way you feel the heat of a forge when you open the door — the ward over him drank the worst of it, the old protection against fire, and turned it to a hot wind that he stood in like a man standing in a gale. It hurt. It did not hurt much.

The worms did not have a ward.

He felt them go. Felt the whole cold slick weight of them simply *lift* off him all at once, flung away in every direction, burning, a storm of small burning things blown off him like sparks off an anvil. He was standing in the middle of the hall with his eyes open and his arms out and smoke coming off his coat, and the air all around him was full of falling, flaming worms.

Korroc had got his shield up. Caleth saw it in the firelight, the great scarred face of it turned toward him, and the fire breaking round it and pouring past on either side; and when the fire was gone the shield was *covered*. Plastered, rim to boss, with burning worms, sizzling, sliding down the metal in little smoking clumps.

Thane had gone into a roll. He came up out of it ten feet away with worms in his hair and on his back and down the neck of his shirt, swatting at himself in a frenzy.

And Korroc lowered his shield, and looked at the front of it, and said very quietly in Dwarven:

*"Torag. In my beard. Every time. Every single time, it's the beard."*

Caleth, who spoke Dwarven, nearly laughed. He could not. Not yet.

Because they were still moving.

All over the hall, scattered on the black floor in their hundreds, burning, smoking, curling — some of them were still moving. Crawling. Toward each other. He could see it. A worm here and a worm there turning, as if they could hear one another, beginning to inch across the basalt toward the place where the robes lay empty in a heap.

*No.*

He did not know if it was enough. He did not know if anything was enough. He had read the dossier; he had read Aravashnial's untested theory in a locked journal, of a man who had died and been raised up again by a demon lord's blessing. He did not know what it took to kill a thing like that, or whether it could be done at all. He only knew that he was not going to stand here and watch it put itself back together.

He reached down into the fire again. Deep. Deeper than he ever had. There was not much left there, and he took all of it.

He lifted his hand over the hall and dropped a fireball on it.

It burst in the middle of the scattered worms with a sound like a great door slamming, and rolled out over the whole of that long black floor, and everything on the floor burned. Every worm. Every rag of robe. The fire went over Thane, still rolling, and his ward drank it. It went over Korroc —

Square over Korroc.

The dwarf stood in the middle of it with his shield half up and the fire going round him, and his ward took most of it, but not all; and when it cleared his beard was smoking, and his eyebrows were noticeably shorter, and there were small roasted things caught all through the braids of his beard like cloves stuck in a ham.

He looked at Caleth.

Caleth looked at him.

"Sorry," said Caleth.

Korroc did not say anything. He reached up, slowly, and pulled one small blackened curl out of his beard, and looked at it, and dropped it on the floor. Then he shut his eyes.

Caleth felt him do it. Felt the warm thread pull, and go round all of them, the way the dwarf counted his people after every fight the way a shepherd counts sheep. Caleth: fine. Better than fine — Caleth's own spells fed a little life back into him every time he cast them, the way the academy had taught him to cast them, and he had cast a great deal. Nageru: still standing, the last healing still holding in him. Thane: hardly touched; a man nobody could see was a man nobody could hit. They were all well. They were all well because for the whole of this fight, every blow that had landed on any of them, half of it had gone to one place.

Korroc opened his eyes, and he was bleeding from a dozen places.

He put his hand on his chest, and the gold went through him, and a few of them closed. Then he tore his feet up out of the stone — Caleth saw what it cost him again, the gray coming back into his face — and turned his back on all of it and ran. Heavily, in his plate, across the hall and through the arch into the temple room, toward the far end, where Arueshalae was still on the floor with a blue demon on her.

Caleth ran after him.

He did not look back at the floor. He did not want to see whether anything on it was still moving.

---

Nageru reached the blue demons first. He always did. His fists flashed with holy light, gold and white, three times, four, and one of the two remaining went thin and black and away.

Caleth came in at the last one from the side, and drove Radiance into it.

Korroc, behind him, threw his open hand out toward Arueshalae on the floor. Gold went across the air and into her, and Caleth saw her breathe, and saw some of the color come back into her face.

The demon turned on Caleth. It hit him four times — claw, claw, bite, horns — and he felt every one, and felt the stone take nearly all of every one, the gray skin cracking now like an old plate, and the little that got through go down the thread to Korroc and vanish in it like snow falling into a fire.

And Nageru crossed the last of the floor and started on it.

Caleth did not count the blows. Before the monk was done it was gone.

---

The quiet afterward was very large.

It went out in every direction, into the dim entrance chamber and the long hall and the red temple room, and filled them up to the starry ceiling. The braziers crackled at the feet of the goat-headed statue. Somewhere a little smoke was still going up from the floor of the hall.

Caleth stood with Radiance hanging from his hand and breathed.

The templars were on the floor. All four. One at the arch, where Nageru had found him first; one near the stair, where Thane had; two more he had not seen go down and did not ask about. The two minotaurs lay where they had fallen, enormous, one with a charred horn. Of the demons there was nothing at all. There never was.

And of Xanthir Vang, there was a heap of empty robes, scorched through in a dozen places, in the middle of the black floor of the hall. And worms. Hundreds of them, scattered over the basalt like grain spilled from a sack, black and curled and roasted.

Not one of them moved.

He went back through the arch and stood over the robes and looked down at them for a long time. He made himself do it. He looked at every worm he could see, one after another, the way Thane had once taught him to look at a floor, and not one of them moved.

He did not trust it.

He wanted to burn it again. That was the plain truth of it. He wanted to stand here and pour fire on that floor until there was nothing left of it but black glass, and then do it again, and then go and find something heavier. He wanted to be *sure*. And he could not be sure; there was no spell he knew that would tell him, no page he had ever read that said *this, and then it is done*. There was only a floor full of dead worms, and a heap of empty cloth, and the silence.

He thought, *I wanted to see his face when it happened.*

And then, *He did not have one.*

Behind him, Korroc had come back through the arch and stopped a little way off. He was picking at his beard. Caleth could hear him: a small steady sound, pluck, and a pause, and a soft disgusted noise, and pluck.

"Is that it?" said Korroc.

Caleth did not answer. He did not know.

It was Arueshalae who answered. She had come up behind them, slowly, one hand pressed against the arm where a claw had opened it, her bow in the other. She was looking past them, down the hall, toward the dark of the entrance chamber at the front of the house.

"No," she said. "There are still rooms we have not looked at. Several." She paused. "And there is still the one who guards the front door."

Korroc muttered something in Dwarven into his beard. Then he nodded. He pulled another small black thing out of the braids and flicked it away.

Thane had gone past them all, down the long hall, into the entrance chamber, walking slowly with his eyes on the floor. Caleth watched him stop, and bend, and straighten again with a white-fletched arrow in his hand, and turn it over, and look along its shaft to see whether it was true. It seemed to be. He put it away.

Then the dwarf looked back up the length of the hall, at Caleth, standing over the robes. He did not say anything. He only looked, the way he had been looking at Caleth for weeks now, the way a man looks at a lock he means to come back to.

Caleth looked away first.

---

They searched the bodies. They went over the templars and the robes and the hall, and Caleth let them, and helped, and did not remember afterward most of what he had picked up or put into the bag.

He was thinking about his shoulder.

He did not notice at first. It was the way you do not notice a noise that has been going on so long you have stopped hearing it, until it stops; and then the silence where it had been is the loudest thing in the room. He was bending over a dead templar to unbuckle a belt pouch, and he straightened, and something was different, and it took him three breaths to understand what.

It did not hurt.

The Spiral did not hurt. Not the knocking from the landing, not the nail. Not the slow ache of the dreams that had been under every night for weeks like a stone under a mattress. Nothing. He put his hand up to his left shoulder blade, under the coat, through the shirt, and pressed, and there was only his own shoulder, and the stone of his skin, cracked, and under it a little bruise where a giant's teeth had been.

He stood very still with his hand there.

*Gone,* he thought, and then, more carefully, because the academy had trained him to say only what he knew: *quiet.*

He did not know what that meant. Whether the mark had stopped because the thing that had been watching him through it had died on the black floor of this hall; or because it had only gone somewhere else, somewhere further, and the mark could no longer reach it; or because it had never been a window at all, and whatever it truly was had simply finished telling him what it had been trying to tell him. He did not know. His father had been afraid of what the records said about it, and he had never read the records.

He took his hand away from his shoulder. He put it inside his coat instead, on the left side, the way he always did. Over the letters, and the note, and the small hard shape of the potion. And next to them, flat against his ribs, the plain dark book with three moons on the cover, and the middle one full.

He had not opened it. He had not let himself. He would not here, either, with the front door still to come and a thing waiting there that turned men into stone.

But his hand stayed on it a while. And he thought — for the first time since a page in an elf's handwriting in a study in Drezen had given him a name — that he might, after all, be allowed to live long enough to read it.

"Caleth," said Korroc, from the arch. Not urgent. Only his name. "Rooms."

"Yes," said Caleth. He took his hand out of his coat. "Rooms."

He went after them, down the long hall, toward the front of the house.
