# Children Are Not Equations

*Chapter Twenty-Three — Runs in the Blood*

---

Nobody tried to stop him leaving the command room, which was a mercy, and nobody tried to follow him, which was a larger one.

He said the name, and the room took it the way a room takes a stone dropped into a well. Korroc's hand came off his shoulder. Irabeth began to ask something and did not finish. And Caleth stood up off the bench, found that his legs would hold him, and went out through the door and along the corridor and up the stair to the second floor of the west range with the taste of struck flint still in his mouth. His hands were not shaking. He noted that. It seemed like information.

*A worm that walks.* The librarians had said it at the Blackwing a year ago, and he had written it down as a figure of speech because scholars under strain reach for figures of speech. It had not been a figure of speech. He had just watched it hold a man against iron with arms made of its own body.

He knew three things about Xanthir Vang now that he had not known yesterday morning. He knew what he was. He knew what he had done. He knew that he had heard a Riftwarden go still in a cage and had drawn the correct conclusion from it.

He did not know how to kill him, and that was the only one of the four that mattered.

The study was as he had left it. The papers he had dropped the day before were still on the boards in a fan around the chair, where they had fallen, and nobody had picked them up. That was his fault, and he stepped over them.

He had been through the stacks. He had found the dossier and the Woundworm page and the notes on the eastern bridge. What he had not done, because yesterday he had been looking for a dragon and had found a name instead, was look *properly* — the way you look at a room when you want to know what a man kept from you, rather than what he left out for you to find.

He looked properly.

The shelves: working books, most of them brought north in the last month, spines he knew, spines he did not. The chest from Vilareth Ford, banded and sealed and still unopened, sitting under the window where the elf had set it down with both palms flat on the lid. Caleth looked at it for a long moment. He did not touch it.

The desk.

It was a steward's desk, heavy, older than the fall, built by somebody who expected it to outlast him and had been right. Three drawers down the right side. The top two slid out when he pulled them and held nothing he wanted — quills, sand, a ruler, a knife for cutting pens, an apple that had given up.

The third drawer did not move.

He pulled it once, a test, and felt the tongue of a lock catch against the frame with a small, solid, well-made refusal.

Aravashnial did not lock things. That was not a guess. Caleth had watched him leave a book of Abyssal ritual open on a mess table in a camp full of soldiers because he had been called away mid-sentence and could not be bothered to close it. He left his door unbarred. He left his wards on the citadel and none on his own room. In a year Caleth had never seen him turn a key.

He had turned this one.

Caleth straightened, went to the door, and put his head out into the corridor.

"Thane!"

It carried. The west range was all stone and short on people this week, and a name went down it like a thrown knife.

It took a little while. Then there were boots on the stair, not hurrying, and Thane came around the corner and down the corridor with the expression of a dwarf who has been summoned by a half-elf shouting his name through a fortress and would like to know why before he decides how to feel about it.

He stopped in the doorway and looked at Caleth. It was a long look. It went over the dropped papers and the pulled-out chair and whatever Caleth's face was doing, and came back to his eyes.

"I need your help," Caleth said, and pointed at the desk. "Open that."

Thane looked at the drawer. Then he looked at Caleth again, and it was a different look — the one that said *this is an absent man's desk and you know it and I know it* — and whatever Thane saw in Caleth's face settled the question, because he said nothing at all. He came in, knelt down in front of the desk on the dropped papers, and took a roll of oilcloth out of his coat.

Korroc arrived in the doorway as the picks came out. He must have followed the shout, or followed Thane, or simply followed the general direction of trouble the way he always seemed to. He took in the room. He took in Caleth. He leaned one shoulder on the doorframe and folded his arms and did not come in, which in Korroc was a considerable act of restraint.

Thane worked with his head a little on one side, listening with his fingertips. It was not a long job. It was a good lock, and good locks had stopped being much of a conversation for Thane some years ago.

*Click.*

Caleth had the drawer open before Thane's hands were clear of it.

A book.

Not a spellbook. He knew the elf's spellbook — that was in a cavern, or had been, in a woman's hand with a woman's finger tapping its notation. This was smaller, and thicker, and very much older, bound in leather gone dark and soft at the corners, the spine broken in so many places that it fell open of its own accord when he lifted it out.

A journal.

He turned it over. He opened it at the front. The first pages were brown, and the ink on them had gone the color of dried blood, in a hand he recognized and did not recognize — Aravashnial's, but younger, upright, the letters not yet leaning into the wind. He turned forward. The ink changed. The hand began to slope. Decades went by under his thumb. The last entries were in ink that was still black, in the dense downhill script he had been reading over the elf's shoulder for a year.

He began to turn pages fast, looking for the name.

He did not find the name. He found himself.

It took him several pages to understand what he was seeing, because he was looking for *Vang*, and his own name was not a thing his eye had been told to catch. Then it caught. And once it had caught, it would not stop catching.

His birth. A date, and a line under it.

A family in Kenabres — his family, the people who had raised him, not named but described so exactly that he could see the kitchen.

Later, Kenabres Academy. The year he finished. A line about the ceremony that meant the writer had been *in the room.*

And a winter he remembered only in pieces: a fever that would not break, a house gone quiet around him, his foster mother's hand cool on his forehead and her face above him trying not to be frightened — and then, one morning, medicine. A small box on the step. Nobody had ordered it. Nobody had ever found out who sent it. His foster father had asked at every apothecary in the city and been told the same thing at each of them, and the family had called it a kindness from a neighbor and had stopped asking, and Caleth had been eight years old and had lived.

There was an entry for that winter. It was short.

Caleth stopped turning pages.

He stood with the book open in both hands and understood that for twenty-five years a man had been standing just outside the edge of his life. Not in it. Never in it. Outside it, at the margin, where you could see the whole of a thing and not be seen. Every time something had come for him that he could not have stopped himself — somebody had stopped it, and had written down that they had, and had gone away again.

*Medicine arriving unexpected.*

*Aravashnial.*

He had given that man a restoration potion in a cave under Kenabres because it had been a calculation, and the calculation had said *this one will be useful.* He had been proud of how cold it was.

Something slid out of the journal and fell.

It turned once in the air, a folded square, and landed by his boot. He crouched and picked it up.

It was heavy paper, the good kind that records are made on, folded in quarters and gone soft along the folds. Every edge was burned. Not scorched from lying near a candle — *burned*, blackened in from the margins as if it had been in a fire and somebody had reached in and taken it out again before the fire finished. The char came off on his fingers.

He unfolded it.

*Certificate of Birth.*

The heading was in a clerk's formal black letter under a device of a tree in a ring of stars. Beneath it, in the flat script of a public record:

*Let it be known to all who may see this document that a child was born and recorded in the archives of Kenabres.*

FATHER: *Aelariel*

MOTHER: *Talia Aranor*

CHILD: *Caleth Aranor*

BIRTHPLACE: *Kenabres*

*Recorded in the Year of the World 4689 AR, on the 14th day of Sarenith.*

Two signatures at the foot. A Keeper of Records for the Kenabres Archives, and a Magister of the Council. A seal in the corner, the same tree, a ring of letters he did not stop to read.

Caleth looked at it for a long time.

He looked at the two names above his own. He had never seen them. He had never heard them. For twenty-five years they had been a pair of shapes that walked into the Worldwound a month after he was born and did not walk out, and he had carried the shapes the way you carry a stone in your pocket, and here they were in a clerk's hand, spelled out, with a line under each.

*Aelariel.* No second name. An elven name, and elves in the archives of a human city did not always give one.

*Talia Aranor.*

He had been told, all his life, that Aranor was the name he had been left with. He had never once asked whose it was.

*Kenabres.* Born there. Not at the Spire, not on the road, not somewhere out past the edge of the maps where Riftwardens went. In the city he had grown up in. In the city that had burned. He had perhaps walked past the building where it happened a thousand times, on his way to the Academy, on his way to a back room that smelled of lamp oil and cats.

He breathed out, hard, once. It was the only sound he made.

Then he folded the certificate along its soft old creases, put it back between the pages where it had been, and closed the journal on it.

He was aware of the two dwarves. Thane had not got up off his knees; he was still crouched by the open drawer, very still, looking at nothing in particular in the way that meant he was looking at everything. Korroc had not moved off the doorframe. Neither of them said a word, and neither of them had seen the page. Caleth was nearly sure of that, and he found he was grateful for it in a way that had an edge of shame on it.

He opened the journal again and made himself go looking for what he had come for.

He found it two-thirds of the way through, in a stretch of entries where the hand was tired and the ink had been cut with water to make it last. A heading, underlined:

*A worm who walks.*

It was a theory, and it said so. Aravashnial had been careful to say so, the way he was always careful — *this is what I believe; I have not been able to test it; do not mistake it for knowledge.* Xanthir Vang had been a man. A human, in the Third Crusade, flesh and blood, as the dossier had said. And he had died. Somewhere in that war, by some means the entries did not know, he had died — and something had happened to him after, and he had not stayed dead. What had got up again was what they had seen in the green light.

*A blessing from Deskari,* the elf had written, and underlined *blessing* as if the word disgusted him.

What Vang had done to earn it, the journal did not say. Aravashnial had not known. He had not been able to find out. He had wanted to, badly; there were three separate entries that began with some variation of *if I could learn what he gave* and ended without an answer.

Caleth read it twice.

It was not a weakness. It was not a way to kill him. It was a scholar's honest *I don't know*, and Caleth had heard that from Aravashnial across a forge floor and found it infuriating, and would have given a great deal to hear it again in person.

Someone knocked on the open door.

A young soldier, one Caleth half recognized from the courtyard, stood behind Korroc with the stiffness of a man who has been sent up four flights of stairs to interrupt three knights.

"Sirs. Commander Tirabade requests your presence on the north parapet."

Caleth closed the journal and slid it into the inner pocket of his coat, on the side opposite the vial, and felt the weight of it settle there against his ribs.

"Of course," he said.

---

Irabeth was standing at the crenellations with a spyglass to her eye, and she did not lower it when they came up.

The north wall of Drezen looked out over the Worldwound, and the Worldwound looked back. Caleth had stood on it perhaps twenty times since the Banner went up and had never stopped disliking the view. The land ran away north and east in shades of wrong — rust, and bruise, and the pale diseased green of things that had been fields — to a horizon that never sat quite level under a sky that never sat quite still. Nageru was already there, at Irabeth's shoulder, his hands folded into his sleeves.

"There," Irabeth said.

They all looked.

It took Caleth a moment. Then he had it: a speck, low on the horizon, red against the bruised gray. It was moving. It was coming this way.

Irabeth turned and held out the spyglass without a word.

Caleth took it and put it to his eye and found the speck and brought it up. It jumped in the lens, lost itself, came back. Small. Winged. A body like a withered child's with a face like a gargoyle had been drawn by somebody who hated children, and a pair of leathery wings working too fast for the size of it.

"A quasit," he said.

He handed the glass to Thane, who found it faster than he had.

"It's carrying something," Thane said.

"It's a quasit," Caleth said. "A minor demon. If it comes to that, we can crush it."

Nageru narrowed his eyes at the horizon. "It is not carrying a bomb of some sort, is it?"

Caleth took the glass back and looked again. Something held against its chest in both claws, flat and squared-off.

"It looks like a book."

Nobody said anything to that. Caleth lowered the spyglass and handed it back to Irabeth.

They waited.

It took a long time. Quasits were not fast; they were small, and malicious, and very good at being somewhere they were not supposed to be, and the long flight across open ground toward a fortress full of soldiers was not a thing they were built for. It came on in fits, dropping and climbing, and the book in its claws did not seem to be helping.

Men had gathered along the wall to either side by the time it arrived. Crossbows came up. Irabeth lifted one hand, and the crossbows stayed where they were.

It stopped about thirty feet out from the parapet and hung there, wings going, level with their faces.

It was giggling.

That was the first thing and it was the worst thing. A high, wet, delighted sound, the laughter of something that had been told a joke on the way over and could not stop thinking about it. Its eyes went over the four of them and stopped on each face in turn, and every time they stopped the giggling got a little worse.

The four of them looked at one another. Then back at it.

"I bring a message," it called out, in a voice like a hinge, "from my master —"

"Who is your master?" said Caleth.

It stopped, annoyed at being cut off. Then the annoyance melted into something happier.

"Xanthir Vang," it said. "Of *course.*"

It paused, and giggled, and hugged the book.

"He demands that you abandon Drezen within the week. He will give you one week to leave."

"Why should we do that?" Caleth said.

"Because if you do not, he will come and take more."

*More.* It was a small word and the quasit said it with its whole mouth. Caleth felt it go into the place under his breastbone where the journal was.

"We've already killed the one that took the first five," he said. His voice came out perfectly level. "We'll kill others."

"If you do," the quasit said, "you will not like the results." It tilted its head, a parody of a thoughtful man. "You should leave. He gives you one week."

And it flung what was in its claws.

It was a book. It had been a book. It came apart in the air as it left the quasit's hands — it had already been torn apart, the stitching cut and the gatherings pulled loose and the whole thing held together by nothing but the demon's grip — and the cover came over the parapet and struck the stones at Caleth's feet with a flat, heavy thud, and the pages went up.

They went up like birds. The wind off the Worldwound caught them and scattered them along the wallwalk and over the crenellations and down into the court, dozens of them, turning over, white and brown and closely written.

"That was only a piece of it," the quasit called, over the wind. "Stay longer, and I'll bring more."

Then it turned on one wing and flew away, giggling, and did not look back, and Irabeth's hand stayed up, and nobody loosed.

For one second nobody moved.

Then everybody did.

Caleth went after the pages on the wallwalk. Thane went over the inner edge of the parapet to the stair and down it three steps at a time after the ones that had gone into the court. Soldiers along the wall were catching them out of the air, stamping on them where they skidded along the stone, one young man leaning out over the drop between two merlons with a friend holding his belt. Korroc caught three against his breastplate with one flat gauntlet as they went by him and stood holding them there like a man pinning moths.

Caleth caught one and looked at it.

He knew the hand. He had known it before he turned the page over.

For a moment his heart did something he did not permit it to do. *The spellbook* — the woman's finger tapping the notation, *this isn't standard* — and he had the page up close to his eyes before he let himself see what it actually was.

It was not the spellbook.

It was a list of observations. A sketch of a ward-line along the eastern wall of Drezen, with measurements in the margin. A reminder, underlined, about a shipment of reagents that had not come. The next page he caught was a half-page of notes on the weather, and a sketch of a bird.

His field journal. The book Aravashnial carried everywhere and wrote in standing up — walking, riding, with the ink bottle wedged in his coat. The daily one. The unimportant one.

*That was only a piece of it.*

Caleth stood on the wall with a page about the weather in his hand and made himself breathe.

They got most of it. Not all; some of it had gone out over the wall into the ditch and some of it had gone out into the Worldwound on the wind, and nobody was going to go and fetch those. But Thane came back up the stair with a double handful, and the soldiers brought theirs, and Korroc peeled his off his breastplate, and between them they got perhaps three parts of four into a pile on the parapet with a piece of broken masonry on top to hold it down.

Nageru had picked up the cover.

He was turning it over in his hands. It was only boards and leather, the spine torn out of it where the gatherings had been ripped loose, and he held it the way he held most things, with attention and without hurry. Caleth watched him run his thumb along the inside edge of the back board. Stop. Run it back again, slower.

Nageru turned and held it out to him.

"There is something inside this cover, my friend."

Caleth stopped with a page in his hand and frowned at it. Then he took the cover.

Nageru was right. Along one edge, under the pasted-down endpaper, there was a seam — not a binder's seam, a second one, fine and even and very carefully made, and behind it the board was fractionally thicker than it had any reason to be. You would never have found it by looking. You would have had to be holding it and paying attention to your hands.

He pushed his thumb along it, and it split.

Envelopes fell out.

Two of them. They were old — the paper yellowed, the corners worn soft, the edges brittle enough that one of them crackled when it landed across his boot. He picked them up. There were names on them in two different hands, and the names were *Aravashnial.*

He should not open them. He knew that as clearly as he had ever known anything. They were another man's letters, hidden by that man inside a cover where no one would find them, and carried by him every day for what must have been — Caleth looked at the yellowed paper — years. They were a thing a person kept from the world.

He had also just lifted a certificate of his own birth out of the same man's locked drawer.

He opened them.

---

The first was yellowed with age and brittle at the edges, and whatever seal had held it had long since cracked away. Across the front, in a graceful human hand, someone had written:

*To Aravashnial, wherever in the Abyss-forsaken world he has managed to wander this time.*

He unfolded it.

> *My dear Aravashnial,*
>
> *If this letter has actually found you, then I can only conclude that either the gods have taken a personal interest in our family affairs or your brother has finally learned how to provide a forwarding address.*
>
> *Aelariel assures me that neither explanation is likely.*

Caleth's eyes stopped.

*Aelariel.*

He read the line again. *Your brother.* He read it a third time. The words did not move. He had been reading since before he could remember and he knew perfectly well what the words meant, and for the space of several heartbeats they meant nothing at all, because the mind had been handed two things it could not put in the same room.

Aelariel, on a certificate of birth, above his own name.

Aelariel, *your brother*, in a letter to Aravashnial.

He went on reading because the alternative was to stop, and he could not stop.

> *We have news.*
>
> *Wonderful news.*
>
> *Though I suspect by the time this reaches you, your brother will already have spent several pages explaining it in excruciating detail and attaching diagrams where no diagrams were required.*
>
> *We are expecting a child.*
>
> *There. I have written it plainly, before Aelariel can begin lecturing about probabilities, omens, and inherited magical tendencies.*
>
> *He is already insufferable.*
>
> *The poor thing is not even born yet and your brother has somehow convinced himself that he must prepare for every possible future. Yesterday I found him attempting to calculate the likelihood of our child becoming a wizard.*
>
> *I reminded him that children are not equations.*
>
> *He informed me that this did not prevent them from having variables.*

Something happened in Caleth's chest that he had no name for.

He knew that man. He had never met him and he knew him the way you know your own hand. *Prepare for every possible future.* A man at a table, running the futures one after another, down to the last one, because the alternative was to be surprised, and being surprised was the one thing he could not bear. *Calculate the likelihood.* He had done it that morning over a kettle of water. He had done it in the underdark with a restoration potion in his hand. He had done it every day of his life since he was old enough to understand that a card revealed is a card spent.

He had thought the Lantern Seer taught him that.

> *I married a brilliant man. Sometimes I wonder if that was wise.*
>
> *The truth is that he is happy.*
>
> *Happier than I have ever seen him.*
>
> *He tries very hard not to show it.*
>
> *He fails.*
>
> *Frequently.*
>
> *When he thinks no one is watching, he catches himself staring at the crib he built and smiling like a fool.*
>
> *If I tell him I wrote that, he will deny it.*
>
> *You have my permission to remind him.*

*He tries very hard not to show it. He fails.*

Caleth's face did nothing. It was very good at doing nothing. Somewhere behind it, a man he did not recognize was standing very still in the middle of a wall with the wind in his hair, and that man had just learned that his father built a crib.

> *We hope you will come home when your duties allow.*
>
> *I know the Riftwardens keep you busy, and I know there is always another breach to seal, another mystery to chase, another corner of reality threatening to come apart at the seams.*
>
> *But you are going to be an uncle.*

He stopped reading.

He did not decide to stop. His eyes simply would not go on to the next line. They stayed on that one — *you are going to be an uncle* — the way a man's hand stays on a hot iron for a half-second after the mind has understood, because the message has not yet arrived at the hand.

*Uncle.*

The underdark. A burned face in a cave, half-blind, and a young man kneeling with a potion he had calculated the value of.

Gwerm Manor. A whisper in a corridor, and the understanding that the elf had known him on sight. And Caleth had thought: *he knows what I am.* Not who. What. A Riftwarden's child, a lineage, a thing an elder of the order would recognize the way a smith recognizes a mark on a blade.

The Blackwing, the weeks in the rubble, the one time Caleth had pressed too hard and the temper had come out of the elf like a blade out of a sheath and Caleth had never, ever made that mistake again.

A forge floor. *We should talk later.*

A cage. *I suppose that runs in the family.*

He went on reading, because there was more and he had to have all of it.

> *Aelariel and I would very much like our child to know their family.*
>
> *More selfishly, I would like someone else around who understands your brother's particular variety of stubbornness.*
>
> *I am beginning to suspect it runs in the blood.*

*Runs in the blood.*

He read it once, and something went down his spine like cold water poured from a height.

He put it away. He could not afford it here. He put it in the place where he put things he would take out later, and he went on.

> *The child is due within a few months.*
>
> *If fortune favors us, perhaps you will arrive in time.*
>
> *If not, then arrive when you can.*
>
> *There will always be a place for you at our table.*
>
> *And if our son or daughter inherits even half the curiosity that seems to plague the men of your family, I suspect they will have many questions for their uncle.*
>
> *Be safe.*
>
> *Return to us when your work allows.*
>
> *We miss you.*
>
> *Your brother misses you more than he will ever admit.*
>
> *With affection,*
>
> *Talia*

And below it, in a different hand — elegant, precise, every letter formed exactly as it ought to be, and so unmistakably elven that Caleth could have told it across a room:

> *Aravashnial,*
>
> *Ignore at least half of what she has written.*
>
> *The other half is accurate.*
>
> *Come home when you are able.*
>
> *— Aelariel*

*Many questions for their uncle.*

He had had many questions. He had asked perhaps a dozen of them, over a year, and been shut out of every one. He had thought the elf was protecting the order. He had thought the elf was protecting himself. He had spent a year, three paces back and one to the left, being careful with a man who — he understood now — had been being careful with *him*, for twenty-five years, for reasons Caleth still did not have.

He opened the second letter.

It was shorter. It was in the elegant hand, and there was no graceful human script anywhere on it.

> *Brother,*
>
> *For once, I shall attempt brevity. You are an uncle.*
>
> *Three days ago, Talia gave birth to a son. Mother and child are healthy, despite the best efforts of the latter to arrive several weeks earlier than planned.*
>
> *His name is Caleth Aranor. We have decided he will carry Talia's family name. I know some among our people might object, but neither of us particularly cares what those people think. The name suits him, and that is reason enough.*

He had been named on purpose.

That was a foolish thing to catch on, and he caught on it anyway. He had carried the name like a coat left on him by strangers, and it had never occurred to him that somebody had *chosen* it, had sat with it, had weighed the objections of *some among our people* and set them aside because the name suited him.

His mother's name. *The name suits him.*

> *You would like him. Though perhaps not immediately. He possesses an impressive set of lungs and has already demonstrated a remarkable disregard for sleep. I am beginning to understand why parents always appear tired. Talia finds this amusing.*
>
> *I had intended to write a longer letter. There are a hundred small things I wished to tell you. The way he curls his hand around a finger. The way he grows quiet when Talia sings to him. The strange certainty I feel whenever I look at him. As though I have spent my entire life preparing for something without realizing it.*

*The way he grows quiet when Talia sings to him.*

He had no memory of it. He could not have. He had been a month old when they walked into the Worldwound. And yet something in him that was older than memory went very still at the line, as though a door had been opened in a house he did not know he lived in, and a sound had come through it that he recognized without being able to say from where.

> *I had intended this letter to be full of those things.*
>
> *Instead, I find myself writing about something else.*
>
> *The mark was on him when he was born. The midwife thought it was a birthmark. Talia thought nothing of it.*
>
> *I knew better.*
>
> *It is the Spiral. The Seeker's Spiral.*

His shoulder did not hurt. It did not burn or itch or ache. He was nevertheless suddenly, acutely aware of it, the whole shape of the thing he could not see without a mirror, lying across his shoulder blade under his shirt and his tabard in the cold wind.

> *I have examined it myself. Again and again. I have convinced myself half a dozen times that I am mistaken. Each time I discover I am not. The mark remains.*
>
> *I know what the records say. I know the stories. I know every argument we ever had regarding them. I know exactly what you would tell me if you were sitting across this table. That most of the accounts are contradictory. That half are myths. That correlation is not causation. That a symbol does not determine a life. I know all of that. I have repeated every one of those arguments to myself.*
>
> *None of them have helped.*
>
> *Brother, I am afraid.*

Caleth read that line and then did not read anything for a while.

His father had been afraid of him.

No. That was not what it said, and he was not going to let himself read what it did not say. His father had been afraid *for* him. Of something about him. Of a mark, and of whatever the records and the stories said about the mark, which Caleth did not know, which nobody had ever told him, which his foster parents had named in careful tones and never explained. He had assumed for twenty-five years that the Spiral meant *Riftwarden's child* and nothing more. A lineage. A family crest someone had drawn on him from the inside.

His father had looked at it every day for three days and had written to his brother *I am afraid.*

> *I need you to come home.*
>
> *I know what I am asking. I know your work cannot simply be abandoned, and I know how rarely either of us has allowed family to interfere with duty. I do not care.*
>
> *Come home, Aravashnial.*
>
> *I need your eyes on this mark. I need you to tell me I have overlooked something. That there is another explanation buried in some record I have forgotten, or some account I dismissed too quickly. And if there is not —*
>
> *Then I need my brother here.*

That was the end.

There was no signature. He turned it over and there was nothing on the back. It did not trail off, and there was no torn edge where more had been; it simply stopped, the way a man stops when he has said the only thing he meant to say and cannot trust himself to go on and close a letter properly. *Then I need my brother here.* And then nothing, because there was nothing else to put.

Less than a month after that letter, the two of them had walked into the Worldwound on a mission no one would ever explain to their son.

*Did you come?* Caleth thought, at a man in an iron cage in the dark, somewhere, with needles in his neck. *He begged you. Did you come in time? Were you there when they went? Did you let them go? Did you go after them?*

*Is that why you never told me?*

He did not know. The letters did not say. The man who could have said was the one man he could not ask.

He became aware that he had been standing in the same place for a long time, and that the wind had not stopped, and that someone was standing near him and waiting.

Irabeth. She had been waiting a while. She had waited, he realized, through both letters, with the spyglass under her arm, and had not said a word, and it had plainly cost her something, because she was the commander of a fortress that had just been told to leave.

She stirred.

"We're not listening to that thing," she said. "Are we? We just took Drezen. We're not leaving."

It was not quite a question. It was a commander putting a thing on the table so that the people around her could knock it off.

Korroc knocked it off. He shook his head once, firmly, the braids going, and said:

"Nope."

That was the whole speech. Caleth, who had spent a great deal of his life composing speeches and then not giving them, found that he envied it.

Irabeth nodded.

"Then we need to get this place in shape," she said, "and ready for a better defense than the one it had last week." And she turned on her heel, and went down the stair, calling for Aron before she was halfway to the bottom.

Caleth folded both letters along their old creases and put them inside his coat, in the innermost pocket, over his heart, with the small heavy vial and the page that named Xanthir Vang.

It was getting crowded in there.

---

The afternoon broke apart the way afternoons did in Drezen now, into four directions at once.

Nageru went with Irabeth. Caleth watched him go without a word, which was how Nageru went everywhere, and understood that the aasimar had looked at the four of them and decided that the one place nobody else was going to be useful was on the walls with a commander who needed hands. That was Nageru. You never saw him choose; you only saw where he had ended up.

Thane went back to the bridge. Aron's engineers at the excavation east of the walls were down to the last of the fill, and that ground had a claim on Thane that Caleth understood exactly and did not need explained. Thane went down the stair from the parapet without saying where he was going, and did not need to.

Caleth went back to the study.

And Korroc went back and forth between the two of them, all afternoon, like a man carrying water between two fires, making sure neither of them burned the building down.

Caleth heard him on the stair perhaps four times. Once he came in with bread and cheese and set them on the corner of the desk and went away again. Once he came in and simply stood in the doorway and looked at Caleth for a while and then said, "The bridge is fine. He's fine. He's hitting a rock with a pick, which I'd say is healthy." Once he came in and said nothing whatsoever and picked up the papers still fanned around the chair from yesterday, and squared them, and put them on the table, and went out.

Caleth read.

He read the journal from the front, properly this time, in order. He read the entries about himself one by one and found that he could only take perhaps three at a time before he had to get up and walk to the window and look at nothing. He went looking for anything about Aelariel, and Talia, and a mission into the Worldwound twenty-five years ago, and found gaps — a stretch of months with nothing at all, and then an entry so brief it was barely a line, and then the hand picking up again much later as though it had been ill.

He went looking for the Lantern Seer's words, for any hint of where Aravashnial could have found them. He did not find them. That did not mean they were not there. It meant he had not found them yet.

He read the field journal pages, too, spread out on the table in the order he could reconstruct, and learned a great deal about the wards on Drezen's eastern wall and the weather and the reagent shipment that had not come, and nothing at all about anything that mattered.

He read the letters again.

He read them sitting down, the second time, with the light going amber across the table, and it was the second time through that the thing he had put away on the wall came back out and would not be put away again.

*I am beginning to suspect it runs in the blood.*

And a voice in the dark, warm and companionable, delighted with itself, speaking to a man in a cage:

*He was stubborn. I suppose that runs in the family.*

*I took it from another elf. Another Riftwarden.*

And Aravashnial — who had never raised his voice in Caleth's hearing, not once in a year, not with a vrock at his back and a rift fighting him down to the last inch — coming up off the floor of a cage against the limit of his chains, again, and again, with the whole iron frame ringing like a bell:

*YOU MURDERED HIM!*

Caleth had stood over the kettle and heard *family*, and turned it over in a mind gone cold and precise, and thought: *someone. A brother, a son, a friend, an old love. Somebody he had and never mentioned.* He had thought it was Aravashnial's grief, and had been ashamed that he had never asked.

He had not once thought it was his own.

He thought it now.

*Vang killed my parents.* He had known that since yesterday. It had been written down for him, in the elf's hand, in a different ink. *He is the thing that killed your parents.* He had carried it for a day like a stone in his coat.

But that was a sentence, and this was a sound. A sentence can be carried. A man coming up off the floor of a cage is not a thing you can carry; it is a thing that happens to you. And if the elf Vang killed — the stubborn one, the Riftwarden, the one whose book Vang had kept *for years* and taken out to read aloud to his brother in the dark — if that elf was Aelariel —

Then Caleth had stood in a command room in Drezen and listened to his uncle scream that his father had been murdered. And had not known. And had thought: *I should have asked him who.*

*Especially the young half-elf.*

And the stillness. The total, instant, absolute stillness of a man in chains, who had been fighting iron four seconds before.

*He went still because it was about me,* Caleth had thought, over the kettle. *And that is why it was worth something.*

He had not known the half of it.

He sat at the old steward's desk in the last of the light with the letters in front of him and his hands flat on either side of them, and did not move for a long time. The window went from amber to rose to gray. He did not light a lamp.

*Children are not equations,* his mother had written, in a graceful human hand, to a brother-in-law she had evidently liked very much.

*He informed me that this did not prevent them from having variables.*

He sat in the dark and discovered that he could not run it. For the first time he could remember, he could not run the futures. He knew the variables. He had every one of them. He had a name and a creature and a theory and a cage and a collar and a week, and he knew precisely what he wanted, and every future he sent down came back to him with the same thing at the end of it, which was a man in an iron cage who had spent twenty-five years at the margin of his life, and who Caleth had never once in a year thought to thank for anything.

---

Nightfall found him still there.

Korroc came in again at some hour after the bells. He lit the lamp, which Caleth had not, and looked at him in the new light, and said, "You should sleep, lad."

"Yes."

"In a bed."

"Very likely."

Korroc waited. Caleth did not get up.

"I'll put it plainer," Korroc said. "You're going to fall over. And when you fall over, you're going to fall over on me, because I'm the one who'll pick you up, and I've had a long day hauling a cousin off a rock. I'd rather you fell over in a bed, where I don't have to lift you."

"That's a reasonable position."

"It's the only position. I've been saying it for three hours."

Caleth looked at him. He was so tired that the dwarf's outline had a faint shine at its edges, the way things do when the eyes have been open too long.

He held out his hand.

"Give me a wand."

Korroc hesitated. It was only a second — a second in which Caleth watched him weigh *he is exhausted* against *he is asking* and decide, as Korroc always decided, that a man who asks for a thing plainly usually knows why he wants it. He reached into his belt and took out one of his healing wands, the one that had been rattling low since the cavern, and put it in Caleth's hand.

Caleth closed his fingers around it and did the thing he did not understand.

It was not a spell. It was never a spell. It was a kind of attention, a folding-inward, like listening very hard for a sound at the edge of hearing — and somewhere under the attention a small warm current went out of him, down his arm, through his palm and into the wood, and the wand drank it the way dry ground drinks water.

He opened his hand. Korroc took the wand back, turned it once in his fingers, felt whatever he felt in it, and nodded, and put it away.

Nobody asked how. Nobody ever did.

*Riftwarden's son,* Caleth thought, watching the wand go back into the belt. *Aelariel's son. Who looked at a mark on me for three days and was afraid.*

*What else did you see, that you did not write down?*

"Sleep," Korroc said.

"I shall," said Caleth, "sleep."

Korroc looked at him a moment longer. Then he went out, and did not close the door all the way, and Caleth heard his steps go down the stair and stop at the bottom and not move for some time before they went on.

The steward's office had a settle along one wall, a hard narrow thing of old oak that somebody had built for sitting up straight on and nobody had ever built for sleeping. Caleth lay down on it in his clothes with the lamp still lit and his coat over him and the letters inside it and his hand on his chest, over the pocket.

He did not remember falling asleep. He never did.

---

He was walking through Drezen.

It was night. There was no one in the streets.

No guards on the walls. No light in any window. No voices from the barracks, no hooves, no hammers, no dogs. The wind was gone. Even the Worldwound was gone — he could feel its absence at his back, the way you feel it when a sound you had stopped hearing suddenly stops.

But the moon was impossibly bright.

It hung over the citadel huge and white, far too close, and it laid a hard clean light over everything, so that every building cast a long black shadow across the street.

So did he. He looked down and saw it, sharp-edged, pouring away from his feet over the cobbles.

He kept walking.

Ahead of him, in the middle of the street, a man stood waiting.

He could not see the man's face. He could see the shape of him, and the dark robes, falling straight to the ground without a stir, and the way he stood — patient, the way a man stands who has nowhere to be and has decided to be there anyway.

The moonlight fell full across him.

There was no shadow at his feet.

Caleth stopped.

His shoulder began to ache. Not badly. Just enough — a slow dull pressure across the blade of it, like a hand resting there, just enough that he reached up and back without thinking to touch it.

The moment his fingers found the Spiral, the man turned his head toward him.

---

He woke.

He was lying on the settle in the steward's office with the lamp guttering and the coat half off him, and his shoulder hurt.

He lay still and let it. For several seconds it went on — a real ache, deep in the bone, exactly where the mark was — and then, slowly, as if it had been waiting to see whether he was paying attention, it faded.

He sat up. He got his coat off, and his tabard, and his shirt, in a cold room at an hour of the night he did not know, and turned his back to the dark window and twisted until he could see the edge of his own shoulder blade in the black glass.

Nothing had changed. It was the same mark. It was the same shape it had been every day of his life. It was, by the lamp's last light, a little paler than the skin around it, and very still.

He put his shirt back on.

He sat on the edge of the settle for some time with his forearms on his knees.

He did not tell anyone.

---

In the morning he scried for Aravashnial. It was the second day.

He did it alone, early, in the steward's office with a basin from the kitchen instead of the black iron kettle, because he did not want the command room and he did not want an audience, and he built the bridge one true length at a time out over a gap he could not see the far side of.

It did not come down. It was *taken* down. He felt it start to take hold — felt the far end of it touch something, the faint first hook of it — and then something on the other side put out a hand and closed it, and the whole span was gone, and he was standing over a basin of water in an empty room.

Not severed, the way the collar had severed it, with the force coming back up the line and into him. Only shut. As if a door had been closed in his face by somebody who did not trouble to slam it.

He tried again on the third day. The same.

On the third day Irabeth had the east gate reinforced and doubled the watch on the walls. Nageru spent it on the parapets with her, and Caleth heard from three separate soldiers that the aasimar had carried a beam up a ladder that four men had been failing to carry up it, and had not seemed to notice he was doing anything unusual. Thane spent it at the bridge. Korroc spent it going between the bridge and the west range and, once, as far as the kitchens, from which he returned with a pie that he put on Caleth's desk without comment.

Caleth spent it with the journal, and with the field journal's torn pages, and found nothing, and spent the evening with the letters, and found everything again, and did not dream.

Four days left, he thought, lying down on the settle. Of Vang's week. He did not believe for one second that the quasit's master would keep to the week, or that he had ever meant to, or that it mattered what they did with it. It was an hourglass turned over and set on the table where they would see it. Caleth watched the sand anyway. He was built that way.

On the fourth morning the spell was shut in his face again.

And on the fourth morning, Horgus Gwerm sent for them.

---

That was unusual in itself. Horgus did not send for people. People came to Horgus, generally in the hope of being given something, and Horgus considered their request and made them wait and then usually gave it to them, grudgingly, with an itemized account of what it had cost him. He had turned a looted quartermaster's store off the south court into a small and ferociously organized kingdom, and he received visitors in it the way a minor lord receives petitioners.

He did not send a runner up four flights of stairs to fetch the knights.

They came together — Caleth from the west range, Nageru off the wall, Korroc and Thane up from the bridge with stone dust still in their beards — and met at the door, and exchanged the look of four people who have each separately decided that this is going to be something terrible.

Horgus was standing behind his long table, which was the only barrier he trusted between himself and the world. There was another dwarf in the room with him.

She was sitting on a crate by the wall as if the crate had been provided for her and was not quite adequate. She was not young. She had the build of every dwarf woman Caleth had ever seen in the trade quarters of Kenabres — broad through the shoulder, square in the hand — and a traveling cloak that had come a long way and been brushed that morning anyway. Her hands were folded in her lap. They were a stonemason's hands, scarred across the knuckles and flat across the palm, and they did not move.

Horgus saw them in the door, and his whole body sagged with relief.

"Oh, thank the gods," he said flatly, and pointed at the woman. "Get her out of here."

The woman looked at Horgus the way a quarry looks at a chisel. Then she looked past him at the door.

Korroc had stopped moving.

It was a small thing. Caleth only caught it because he was standing half a step behind him. The dwarf had come through the doorway at his usual heavy roll and simply stopped, the way a cart stops when the wheel meets a stone, and every line of him changed at once.

Thane, beside him, was looking at the floor.

Caleth looked from one of them to the other and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said: *Dagna.*

Dagna Stonevein — the last of the four, a letter in a small upright hand that bore down on the downstrokes, the woman who had written *after seventy-five years, I think it is time I went home* — rose off Horgus's crate, set both square hands on her hips, and looked at her son and her nephew.

"Do you two *not* know how to write a letter?"

She said it in Dwarven. Caleth understood every word, and privately agreed with most of them.

Korroc started to grin. It began somewhere under the beard and got out of control before it reached his eyes.

Thane found the floor extremely interesting. He studied it with the whole attention of a man who has discovered it is made of something rare. He had been going to write to her. He had been going to write to her for a long time. He had not written to her, and she was here, and he had not written.

"You'll have heard," Korroc said, and the grin was all the way out now and he had given up on it, "that we've been *very* busy."

"I've *heard*," said Dagna.

"And Aron told me," said Horgus, from behind the fortification of his table, raising his voice over the whole exchange with the air of a man clearing out a room he wished to have back, "that if I saw you I was to send you to the bridge. So. I have seen you. Go to the bridge." He waved both hands. "Go away."

---

They talked all the way down the halls.

Or rather Dagna talked, in Dwarven, and Korroc answered her, and Thane walked on her other side with his hands behind his back and said perhaps one word in five, and Caleth and Nageru walked behind the three of them and did not need to be told to leave space.

Caleth listened, because he could not help listening, and because a family talking in a language they believed was private is one of the few things in the world that no one ever performs.

She had come by the river road and then overland, with a supply column for the last part of it. She had hated the supply column. The sergeant had been an idiot, the mules had been worse, and the cook had put something in the stew that she was still considering whether to forgive. She had been in Drezen since yesterday evening and had spent it with Horgus because Horgus had been the first person she found who was in charge of anything, and she had not thought much of him but she respected a man who kept a list. Korroc had got thin. Thane had not got thin, but he had got something else, and she looked at him sideways when she said it and he did not look back. They had both got old.

"We have *not*," said Korroc.

"You have. I can see it from here. It starts in the corners of the beard. I can see it from here."

And Caleth, walking two paces behind with his hands folded, watched Korroc's hand come up and touch the corner of his own beard without his seeming to know he had done it, and felt something go through him that was not quite pain.

He had held his mother's name in his hand for four days. He had read that she sang, and that his father was insufferable, and that there would always be a place at their table. He had it on paper, in an inner pocket, folded along creases older than he was.

Korroc's mother had walked through a door.

He did not begrudge it. He examined himself carefully for it, the way you probe a tooth, and found that he did not begrudge it at all. That was something. He noted it.

*This is what it looks like,* he thought, *from the outside. This is what I would have had.*

The horns went.

Three blasts. Short, hard, one on the heels of the next, from the east watch — and the whole corridor stopped. Every soldier in it stopped. Two masons carrying a beam stopped with it between them. Dagna stopped in the middle of a sentence with one hand still up.

"What is that?" she said quickly.

Korroc put his hand on her shoulder.

"Go to the central court," he said. Not in Dwarven; in Common, which meant he wanted the soldiers to hear him give the order. "Find the command room. Wait for us there."

She frowned up at him. It was the frown he had, exactly, the same furrow in the same place.

"What is going *on*?"

"Something's attacking." Korroc squeezed the shoulder once. "I'll find you after."

She looked at him for about a second — a second in which Caleth watched seventy-five years of a woman who had lost a city and a husband and a sister-in-law decide not to argue with a son at the one moment it would cost him — and nodded, and turned, and went down the corridor toward the middle of the citadel at something between a walk and a run.

They went up.

---

From the east parapet Caleth saw them coming in.

Five of them, low and fast over the broken ground from the east — five long lean winged shapes the dirty red of an old burn, wings that were more membrane than wing, heads like sharpened spades. Rift drakes. And on each back a rider, crouched low, with a long lance couched along the drake's neck.

They were not coming for the walls. They were not coming for the gate. They swung wide of both in a long curve that took them south of the eastern bridge, over the broken ground and the spoil heaps, straight down onto the excavation.

The workers were already running. Caleth saw them go — small figures scrambling out of the cut, dropping tools, going for the bridge and the walls in a scattered stream. Nobody had to tell them. People who lived in Drezen did not wait to find out what a winged thing wanted.

The drakes came down in the cut. The riders slid off. And then the drakes went up again, empty, five red shapes climbing back into the sky and wheeling there, and the riders stood alone in the excavation with their lances, in among the abandoned barrows and the half-cleared fill of a tunnel mouth that two dwarves had been waiting a long time to reach.

"Cultists," Thane said. His voice was very flat.

*A target of opportunity,* Caleth thought. *Or not. It does not matter which. Five on the ground. Five in the air. Every one of them faster than we are.*

He was already building the fold.

Behind him Korroc turned and put his gauntlet flat on Nageru's chest and spoke over it in Dwarven, low, and Caleth felt the working go out and take — the thread between the two of them thickening into something heavier, so that half of whatever happened to the aasimar in the next minute would happen to the dwarf instead. Nageru did not look down at the hand. He simply stood and let it be put there.

Thane shut his eyes and spoke one word to Alseta, and the blessing came down over the four of them like a hand laid flat against a door.

Caleth closed his own hand on the fold and let go of it.

The world went away, that small cool nothing, half a second of nowhere —

— and came back full of wind and dust and the smell of turned earth, and the four of them were standing on the lip of the excavation with five men and five lances and five drakes circling overhead.

---

Nageru moved first. He always did.

There were five riders on the ground, spread through the cut, and he went for the one on the northern edge — crossed the distance in the way he had that made distance seem like an old habit he had given up — and hit him, once, square in the chest as he arrived. The man staggered and did not fall.

A second rider was already on him. The lance came in at Nageru's side and took him, and as the point went in the whole length of the weapon *cracked* with light — a blue-white snap of lightning coursing down the shaft and into the wound.

Caleth saw Nageru barely react. Saw Korroc, twenty feet away, jolt as though someone had struck his shield from behind, and grunt, and keep his feet.

*Half.*

A third lance came at Thane and Thane went under it as though he had been expecting it since breakfast.

Something passed over Caleth's head.

He felt it before he saw it — a shadow, a rush of air — and a drake came down out of the sky in a long shallow dive and swept its tail at Korroc. The tail was barbed, a whip of sinew and bone as long as a man, and it came around in a flat arc and clanged off Torag's hammer and anvil on the dwarf's shield like a bell struck with a bar.

The second drake came for Caleth.

He saw it and did not get out of its way. The tail took him across the ribs and knocked the breath out of him and turned him half around, and he had one clear and ungenerous thought — *I should have been paying attention to the sky and I was paying attention to the ground* — and came back to his feet with Radiance up.

Thane was already inside the reach of the man who had tried to stab him. Both hands. Three strikes, fast, one two three, the knives going in low and coming out, and the man doubled over them — and did not go down.

A third drake dove.

It did not dive at anyone. It dove at the ground in front of Caleth, and opened its jaws at the bottom of the dive, and spat.

A ball of something green and viscous hit the earth ten feet from him and *burst*.

It went out in a cloud. A thick, choking, caustic cloud that rolled over the ground as fast as a thrown blanket and ate everything it touched, and Caleth had time to see it coming and throw an arm over his face and twist aside, and it caught him anyway, stinging along his hands and the side of his neck like a scald.

Thane rolled. Thane was simply not there by the time it reached where he had been.

And Korroc, who had spent a lifetime learning to put a shield between himself and anything that came at him, stood in the path of it and took it full — because a cloud is not a thing you can block with a shield. It went around the shield. It went over the rim and under it and into every seam of his armor at once, and Caleth heard him hiss through his teeth.

*Acid,* Caleth thought. *Of course. Again.*

He went two steps to Korroc's side.

He did not think about it. There was no time to think about it and he did not need to; he had done it once before, in a ring in the Wintersun's hall, and the shape of it was still in his hands. First the easy part — a ward against fire, laid over the dwarf fast and close, so that flame would slide off him the way rain slides off oiled leather.

Then the hard part.

He reached out past the ward and took hold of the air itself, thirty feet all around where he stood, and bent it. Not a spell. Not quite. The same instinct that let him take a fireball apart and put it back together as ice, turned outward and held there — a standing inversion, a sphere of *wrongness* hung in the air with himself at the center of it, with one rule written into it: *anything that comes in here is not what it was.*

Acid that crossed that line would not arrive as acid.

It would arrive as fire. And on the dwarf, the fire would meet the ward. And the ward would take it.

He felt it settle and hold. It cost him. He let it cost him.

*Stay within thirty feet of him.* That was the whole of the discipline now. The working went where Caleth went, and it was only worth anything while Korroc was inside it.

Korroc felt it too. The dwarf's head turned — not to Caleth, but to the air, the way a man turns when the pressure in a room changes — and something in his face said he understood exactly what had just been done to the space around him, even if he could not have put a name to it.

Then Korroc put his free hand flat over his own breastplate and hauled, and healed himself.

*Himself first.* Caleth noted it, even then. Korroc always went to himself last — it was the eleventh time, the twentieth, he had stopped counting. But Korroc had been taking half of Nageru's wounds and all of his own and a cloud of acid besides, and some part of the dwarf had done the sum and decided that a healer on the ground is no use to anyone. It was the right sum. It was not a sum Korroc usually let himself make.

Then Korroc turned on the nearest drake and flung out his hand, and two rays of fire leapt off his palm and took it along the flank, and it screamed and climbed.

The rider Nageru had hit at the very start had got his breath back. He came at Nageru's back with the lance and missed completely — Nageru shifted perhaps a hand's width and the point went past. A drake swept its tail at Caleth and missed, and Caleth had time to feel almost smug about it before the fifth rider came in from behind Nageru and put his lance into him, hard, the lightning snapping blue.

Blood opened on Nageru's side. And on Korroc's, in the same place, at the same instant.

Nageru turned and hit the first man again. And again. And a third time, with his whole body behind it, and the man went down in the dirt and did not get up.

He turned on the next and hit him twice — and then, because there were three of them now around him and every lance was on him, he stepped out. Just stepped out, backward, out of the ring of them. The lances followed him as he went. Two missed. One found him.

The man on Thane stepped back half a pace, set his feet, and stabbed — and this time the point went home, and the lightning went into Thane and made every muscle in the dwarf's body clench at once.

A drake came past Caleth low enough to touch, the tail flicking out, and he ducked under it by the width of his hair.

One of the riders peeled off after Nageru, lance leveled, and missed him as he closed.

A drake dropped on Korroc from behind and caught him across the back of the helm with its tail — a sound like a hammer on a pot — and climbed away.

Thane went back in on his man. Two strikes. The man was bleeding from four places now and was still standing, and Caleth saw Thane's face and saw the particular flat look on it that meant Thane had started to take this personally.

Another drake came at Caleth and missed him completely, and he stopped watching the ground.

*Enough.*

He reached down into the deep place — the golden thing the wardstone had left in him, the reserve that was not a spell but was the thing spells were built out of — and pulled, and built a fire on top of it that he did not bother to turn to ice, because he was not fighting anything that minded fire less than it minded cold, and flung it up.

It went up off his palm like a spark thrown off a whetstone and opened in the sky between two drakes into a flower of fire the size of a house.

They were quick. They rolled away from it in opposite directions, tucking their wings, and the worst of it went past them — but only the worst. The rest caught them both, and a great deal of it caught them, because Caleth had put more into that fire than he had put into anything in weeks. Both of them came out the far side of it smoking, and one had a wing that was not working as it should.

Down in the cut, the man on Thane stabbed three times.

The first went wide. The second found a seam at Thane's side, under the arm, where the chain shirt had to have a seam because a man has to be able to lift his arm — and the lance went in, deep, and the lightning went in after it.

Thane's eyes rolled up.

He went down.

He did not stagger. He simply went down, the way a thing goes down when all its strings are cut at once, onto his side in the dirt of the excavation with his knives still in his hands.

And a drake came down out of the sky, took him in its talons, and went back up.

---

Caleth watched it happen and was too far away to do anything about it.

*Again.* The same thought, at the same pitch, from the same place under his ribs. *Again. You planned for the ground. You planned for the lances. And a thing came out of the sky and took one of them in front of you, and you are too far away.*

A ceiling in a cavern. A templar with his hand on a shoulder.

*No.*

Korroc's eyes went wide.

And Korroc Stonevein, who had never in Caleth's hearing willingly moved faster than a walk if he could avoid it, reached down to his boots and woke the thing in them.

He went across the excavation like something fired from a siege engine. A cultist in his path, astonished, stuck out a lance to stop him and did not come close — the dwarf was past him before the lance was level — and Korroc ran on, under the drake, *under* it, as it climbed with Thane hanging limp in its claws.

It was too high. It was already too high. Korroc could not reach it, and Caleth could see him understand that in the middle of a stride.

So Korroc threw a healing spell at it.

He *threw* it. Caleth would not have believed it if he had not seen it. The dwarf drew back one arm and flung it up at the sky like a man throwing a stone, and a spark of golden warmth went up off his palm in a long arc, and hit Thane.

Thane's eyes opened.

He came awake twenty feet in the air, in a drake's claws, with the wind in his face and the ground falling away beneath him, and Caleth saw even from where he stood the moment Thane understood where he was.

A cultist came at Korroc from the side while his arm was still up, trying to take the gift of an open flank. The lance went into Korroc's shield and slid off it. Korroc did not look at him.

Across the cut, Nageru finished a man. Caleth did not see how; he saw only the end of it, the aasimar stepping back and the man on the ground.

A lance came at Caleth.

He was not there when it arrived. He took one step to his left, through the fold of the world, and came out of it at Korroc's elbow with Radiance up, and heard the lance hit empty air behind him and its owner swear.

Another rider put his lance into Nageru, and Caleth felt Korroc flinch beside him as blood came through the dwarf's surcoat. A drake came past Nageru and missed him. A drake came past Korroc and missed him too.

And up in the air, Thane twisted.

It was a single hard movement, his whole body, like a fish going off a hook — and the drake's grip failed, and Thane came out of its claws and fell.

Caleth saw his hand go to his belt as he fell. Saw the silver flash — Terendelev's scale, the charge in it answering — and Thane stopped falling. He hung in the air, high over the cut, turning slowly, with his knives in his hands and a look of pure outrage on his face.

The drake screamed, and wheeled, and came back around.

And took him again.

Caleth watched it and his face was calm. He could feel how calm it was. It was the calm of a man who has run out of futures to run and has one left.

*Get him and get out. Take hold, and fold the two of you down to the ground, and let the drake keep the air.*

He built it and went.

The world folded and he came out of it in the sky.

Right beside the drake. Close enough to smell it — hot, reptile, something rotten — close enough to see the scales along its flank overlap and the talons locked through Thane's belt and coat. Thane's face turned toward him, astonished. Caleth reached out.

His fingers closed on air a finger's width from Thane's sleeve.

The drake had kept flying. That was all. It had not dodged. It had simply not stopped, the way birds and dragons and falling things do not stop, and in the half-second it took Caleth to arrive it had moved on by the width of a finger, and a finger was all he had needed.

Then he was falling.

It was not a long fall. It was long enough. He had time to curl, and to get Radiance away from himself, and to try to roll as he landed — and he hit the ground wrong, and the roll went sideways, and he came out of it on his back in the dirt with the sky wheeling above him and every bone in his body complaining in order.

*Saint's mercy,* he thought.

Above him the drake was climbing with Thane.

Caleth lay on his back and lifted his free hand and let go of lightning. It went up off his fingers in a single white crack, a spear of it, and took the drake along the belly, and the drake shrieked and lurched in the air and did not let go.

A drake swept past Nageru and missed him.

And Korroc planted his feet, and put one hand on his own chest, and healed himself — *first again,* Caleth thought, *good* — and then drew back his arm and threw another spark of golden light up at the sky, and it took Thane full in the chest, and Caleth saw the wounds on him close up under it.

Then the dwarf reached down into the deep place in himself, the way Caleth had a minute before, and let go.

It came off him in a wave. Warm, golden, washing out over the excavation in every direction at once, and it went over Caleth where he lay and he felt his ribs stop grinding and the acid-scald on his hands go cool; it went over Nageru; it went up, all the way up into the air, and found Thane. It went over all four of them and did not stop until it had found every one.

Nageru walked to the nearest cultist, swung twice, and the man was on the ground.

The others closed on him again. There were not many others left. One got a lance into him. A drake came down low and spat a ball of acid at him as it passed, and Nageru simply dropped under it — not a dodge, a *drop*, all at once, as if his knees had been cut — and the ball burst on the earth behind him and he was not in the cloud when it rose.

One of the riderless drakes wheeled away east and did not come back. It had seen enough.

Up in the air, Thane twisted again. The drake was ready for it this time. It shifted its grip and held on, and Thane twisted a second time and could not get free, and the drake beat its wings and began to climb and turn away east, away from the walls, carrying him.

*No.*

Caleth got up.

He did not remember deciding to. He was on his back and then he was on his feet, and Radiance was in his hand, and the drake was going away with Thane in its claws.

He took one step and folded, and came out of the fold on the ground in front of it — directly in its path, square in front of it, low — and lifted his hand, and built fire.

Three rays. Three long bright lines of fire off his palm, one after another, into the drake's chest.

The first went in. The drake shrieked.

The second went in. The drake's wings faltered.

The third went in through the place where the second had opened it, and the light went out of its eyes in mid-air, and it came down.

It came down the way a dead thing comes down, all at once and with nothing left in it to choose where. Its talons opened. Thane came out of them five feet off the ground and went into a tumble as he hit and came up out of it on one knee, knives out, whole.

The drake did not tumble. It fell straight on top of Caleth.

There was a moment, very clear, in which he saw it coming and understood precisely what he had done — *stood directly in front of a thing and killed it, and it was coming this way* — and then something the weight of three horses landed on him, and the world went brown and full of dust and very, very quiet.

---

He was alive. He knew that, because it hurt.

He was on his back in the dirt with most of a drake across his legs and chest, and dust in his mouth, and a wing lying over his face like a leather tent. He could breathe. Not well. He could not move his legs, but that seemed to be because there was a drake on them and not because anything had come apart. He could hear.

He could hear Korroc, somewhere, fire going off his hands again — the soft roar of it, twice — and a drake screaming. He could hear boots running.

He could hear Thane, very close, and a man with him. A lance scraping on stone. A grunt. Two blows, fast, the particular wet sound Thane's knives made, and a third — and then a body going down, and Thane breathing hard over it.

*The one who put him down,* Caleth thought, under the drake. *Of course. He was not going to let that one leave.*

Farther off, wings. A drake coming down low, and a man's voice shouting, and the wings going up again heavy with weight. And a second set of wings, going away.

Then quiet.

"*Caleth!*"

Korroc. And under it, closer, *through* him somehow, the thread — Caleth had felt it all fight long without once thinking about it, the dwarf taking half and half and half, and now he felt it pulled on, hard, from the other end, like a man tugging a rope in the dark to see if anyone was still holding it.

He was still holding it. He thought Korroc could probably tell.

"Caleth! Are you all right?"

Thane was there first — Caleth could tell by the swearing — then Nageru, then Korroc, and the wing came off his face and there was sky, and three faces, and dust going up gold in the light.

"I'm under a drake," Caleth said.

"We can see that," said Thane.

"I killed it."

"We saw that too."

"From the *front*," said Korroc, in the tone of a man reporting a crime.

It did not take much, in the end. Nageru got his shoulder under the thing's neck and Korroc got a grip on a foreleg and Thane got Caleth under the arms, and between the three of them they shifted it enough to haul him out, and Caleth came out from under it on his back in the dirt and lay there for a moment looking up at the sky, which was empty now. All of it. Every drake gone that was not dead, and the last cultist gone with one of them.

Korroc put a hand on his chest, and warmth went through him, and the ribs stopped arguing.

Thane stood up, and looked east after the drakes, and then looked down at the tunnel mouth — the half-cleared fill, the timbers Aron's men had set, the barrows lying on their sides where the workers had dropped them. Caleth watched his face do the arithmetic and arrive somewhere.

"We need to find Aron," Thane said. "He's the one who called us out here to begin with."

---

They heard her before they saw her.

They were halfway back across the broken ground toward the eastern bridge, walking in the loose tired straggle of people who have just survived something and have not yet got around to being frightened by it, when a voice rose above the noise of the soldiers coming out to meet them — rose in Dwarven, at a pitch and volume that turned heads all along the wall.

Dagna Stonevein was coming down off the bridge at a run.

She had two guards with her. They were young, and armored, and had plainly been assigned to her by somebody who outranked them, and they were doing their level best to persuade her to stop. They were not succeeding. One of them had a hand out toward her elbow and was being ignored with such totality that the hand appeared to have become embarrassed and was trying to retreat.

"*Thane!*" She reached him first, and took him by both arms, and looked him up and down like a mason looking for cracks. "Are you all right? That thing tried to carry you *off* —"

"I'm fine, Aunt Dagna."

"You were *carried off!* I watched you from the *wall!*"

"Only briefly."

She let go of him and rounded on Caleth, who had not been introduced to her and had been walking in the hope that this would remain the case for some time.

"And you! My *gods!* What were you thinking — standing in *front* of something you were about to kill?"

Caleth opened his mouth.

"You stood right in front of it! I saw it! It fell on you! What did you think it was going to *do?*"

He closed his mouth again. There was, he found, no answer to that which was both true and complimentary to himself, and she did not appear to be waiting for one.

She did not stop. She went on at all four of them, turn and turn about, in Dwarven and then in Common for the benefit of Nageru, who received his share of it with a small grave bow of the head that seemed to disarm her for almost two seconds. She had words for the drakes, the cultists, the architecture, the guards, the commander who had evidently let four people go out and fight five dragons on their own, and a separate and extensive set of words for Korroc, who had told her to go to the command room, and whom she had been perfectly within her rights to ignore, *because she was his mother.*

Korroc chuckled. He did it three or four times over the course of the walk, low in his chest, and about halfway back to the bridge he put his arm around her shoulders.

She did not stop talking. But she let him leave it there.

And somewhere on the long walk back across the broken ground, with the citadel coming up ahead of them, Thane told her.

Caleth watched him decide to. He saw the moment. Thane had been walking on her other side, silent, letting Korroc have her, and then something in his shoulders set — the same set they got before a lock he did not expect to like — and he said, in Dwarven, quietly, "Aunt Dagna. There's something I should have written."

She heard it in his voice. She stopped mid-sentence and looked at him.

He told her all of it. Aravashnial's four sources. The forty who left Kenabres and the eight who came back. Staunton. The prison. The records, both names, over and over, a nuisance in a ledger for a year. Twenty feet of stone. The riverbed. *Neither of them is on the list of the dead.* He told it the way Thane told anything, in order, with the facts in the right places and nothing between them, and he did not look at her while he did it.

Caleth looked at her.

He saw it go into her. He saw her face open, just for an instant — the whole of it, every line — the way a door opens in a strong wind before the hand on the latch can catch it.

Then she caught it. And it was gone as fast as it came, and she was a stonemason again, walking.

"Well," she said. "We'll look into this."

She let a few strides go by. Caleth saw her glance at Thane, sidelong, a quick measuring look — and understood that whatever she said next was going to be said *for him*, the way a mason sets a brace under a wall she has decided is carrying too much.

"But you'll not go getting your hopes up too far, the pair of you. We've believed them dead a very long time, and I'll tell you what I believe in my heart. If they were alive, they'd have tried to come home to us. Both of them. There's no wall on this earth that would have kept your father from it, and none that would have kept his."

Thane nodded.

"But at least finding bodies would —"

He stopped. He looked away, out over the broken ground to the east where the riverbed was, and did not finish.

Dagna reached across Korroc and put a hand on Thane's shoulder.

"I know, dear," she said. "I know."

---

As the gate came up ahead of them she looked sideways at her son.

"Word was put out," she said, in the tone of a woman explaining something that should have been obvious, "that they needed stonemasons for the citadel. So I came."

Korroc nodded gravely. "I'd expect nothing less of you, Ma. I'm surprised you weren't here sooner."

She scoffed at him, a sound like a chisel on granite.

"You know how far I traveled."

Korroc grinned down at her, and nodded.

"Aye," he said. "Aye. I do."

Caleth walked behind them through the gate, and said nothing, and kept his hand over the inside of his coat.

---

Korroc and Thane found Aron. Or Aron found them; he came across the court at a half-run with a slate under his arm and dust to the elbows, and he had the look of a man who has spent the last hour certain that his excavation was going to be torn apart by dragons and has just been told otherwise.

"The workers?" said Korroc.

"Frightened. Not hurt. Nobody hurt." Aron looked from one dwarf to the other. "It's why I sent for you. Before any of that." He waved east, at the sky, at the whole business of the drakes. "We need to head back out there. We're ready to open the passage."

Thane went absolutely still.

It took most of an hour to get the workers back. They had to be reassured, and some of them had to be reassured twice, and one old laborer had to be personally escorted to the lip of the cut by Korroc, who stood beside him and said something in a low voice that made the man laugh and pick his tools back up. Then they went back out, all of them — the four, and Aron, and his engineers, and a double handful of workers with picks and levers, and Dagna, whom nobody had told to come and nobody was going to tell to stay — back across the broken ground to the excavation, past the dead drake, which somebody had already started to butcher, to the tunnel mouth.

It was not much to look at. A hole in the side of a bank, shored with fresh timber, choked at the back with the last of the fill. Behind the fill, Aron said, the old pre-fall water tunnel ran on into the dark. He had sounded it. He had had a man put an ear to it. There was open air beyond.

"Everyone step back," Aron said, "and let them finish it —"

The workers put their levers in. The last of the rock shifted and came down in a long slithering rush, and dust came out of the opening in a cloud.

And Korroc and Thane went in through it before it had settled.

Nobody stopped them. Aron opened his mouth and shut it. Caleth lit Radiance with a word, and the leaf-blade went bright and pale, and he followed them in with Nageru at his shoulder and Dagna, silent now for the first time all day, one step behind.

The tunnel was dry. It was old dwarven work, smooth-walled and round, and it went away into the dark straight and level the way water tunnels do. The air was stale and cold.

There were bones.

The light found them a few yards in. Skeletons, lying where they had fallen, on the floor of the tunnel and against its walls. Five. Six. More past the edge of the light. Some of them had been lying on their sides. One had been sitting against the wall with its legs out, as though it had stopped to rest and never got up. There were scraps of cloth on some of them, and on one a rusted chain around the ankle, cut through.

Korroc went from one to the next.

He did it slowly. He knelt by each one and looked at it the way he looked at a joint in a wall — the length of the long bones, the breadth of the shoulders, the shape of the skull. He did not touch them. He did not need to. Caleth watched him do it and understood, halfway through, what he was doing, and felt his stomach turn over.

Thane did not kneel. Thane stood in the middle of the tunnel with Radiance's light on him and looked at each one hard from where he stood, and Caleth watched his eyes go from skeleton to skeleton and arrive, each time, at the same conclusion a little faster than Korroc did.

Too long in the leg. Too narrow in the shoulder. Too tall.

None of them were dwarves.

Korroc came back from the last one and stood up, slowly, and looked at his cousin, and shook his head.

Thane's face did not change very much. It did not need to. There was a line between his brows, hard and deep, and his mouth had set, and the whole of him had gone to stone in a way that was not the way Korroc went to stone — not rooting, not strength. Only disappointment, so hard and so complete that it had nowhere to go but in.

Dagna came up beside him, and put her hand on his shoulder, and looked at him. She did not say anything. She did not need to either.

Thane turned his head and looked back toward the tunnel mouth, where a soldier stood silhouetted against the light.

"Can someone go and ask Sosiel to come here," he said. "We need his help."

The soldier turned to go.

He did not have to. There were footsteps at the mouth of the tunnel already, and Sosiel Vaenic came in through the dust with his cleric's satchel over his shoulder and the open, unhurried face of a man who has decided not to let a war hollow him.

"I just heard my name," he said. "You need me? When I heard the tunnel was open, I came to see."

Thane was at him before he had finished.

"Can you help us?" he said. "Talk to one of these dead. I want to ask them if they knew our fathers. See if we can get anything."

Sosiel looked past him down the tunnel at the bones in the light. Then at Thane. Then, for a moment, at Korroc, and at Dagna standing between them.

"I can do that," he said. "Of course."

Caleth watched him prepare. He had seen it done perhaps twice before and it was not a thing you grew easy with. Sosiel knelt on the tunnel floor and took a few small things out of the pouches at his belt — Caleth could not see them properly and did not try — and laid them out, and was quiet for a little while with his head bowed. Then he looked up.

"Which one?"

Thane looked down the tunnel. He looked at each of them again, one by one, and Caleth did not know what he was looking for and suspected Thane did not either. Then he pointed.

"That one."

A human. One of the ones against the wall, sitting, with its legs out in front of it — the one that had stopped to rest.

Sosiel went and knelt in front of it, and put two fingers on the brow of the skull, and spoke. It was not loud. Shelyn's name was in it, and Caleth heard it and felt the old small tightening in his chest that he always felt when Sosiel said it, and ignored it.

The air in the tunnel changed. It was hard to say how. It got colder, or older. Radiance's light did not dim, but it seemed to be shining on something that was a very long way off.

Sosiel nodded, slightly, and looked up at Thane.

"You may ask."

Thane came and stood in front of the bones. He did not kneel.

"Did you know Borin and Thorek Stonevein?"

The jaw of the skull did not move. The voice came out of the air around it — dry, faint, faraway, like a voice heard through a wall in another house.

*"Yes. They are the reason we were able to escape."*

Thane's hands, at his sides, closed. Caleth saw it and saw them open again.

"Why are you still here," Thane said, "and they are not?"

*"I told them to go. I told them I would stay, and give them a chance to escape."*

The tunnel was very quiet. Somewhere back toward the mouth a worker had stopped with his pick in the air.

"Do you know," Thane said, "if they got out?"

*"No."*

One word, from a long way off, with nothing behind it. Thane stood and took it the way he took a blade he had seen coming, without moving, and Caleth watched him put it somewhere and close the lid.

"What kind of condition were they in," said Thane, "when they left?"

*"Borin was injured."*

Caleth did not look at Korroc. He did not have to. He felt it — through the ground, almost, through the thread the dwarf had never quite let go of since the fight — a jolt, like a hand on a rope when the weight at the other end shifts.

Korroc came forward and put a hand on Thane's shoulder.

Thane turned his head. The two of them looked at each other. It was a long look, and Caleth could not read all of it and did not try, but he could read the shape: Thane had asked his four and had one more he wanted to ask and knew that it was not his to ask. *Borin was injured.* That was Korroc's father. The next question belonged to his son.

Thane nodded, and stepped back.

Korroc stood in front of the bones in his place.

"Did they have a plan," he said, "for where they were going?"

*"They spoke of a crypt that would have supplies. They told us that if we got separated we should follow the riverbed north. To the crypt."*

And that was all. The cold went out of the air. Sosiel took his fingers off the skull and sat back on his heels, and whatever had been speaking was not there any more, and it was only bones against a wall.

Five questions. Caleth had counted them without meaning to.

Dagna's face had changed.

He saw it happen. Something came across it — not the shock of the walk back, not a door blowing open. Something slower. The look of a woman walking into a room she has not stood in for seventy-five years and finding the furniture where she left it.

"It's logical," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"They'd not have tried to make it home. Not from here, not with one of them hurt and every demon north of the river out looking. They'd go to ground somewhere with supplies, and stay there while they worked out whatever their plan was." She was talking to the tunnel wall, half to herself. "There's a graveyard. North, up the riverbed, and a little west. A day out. Our people — the ones who died when Khar-Zadûn fell." She said the name the way a person says the name of a relative. "We buried a great many of them there. And it was a good place, and a sheltered one, so somebody built a waystation on the grounds, for travelers — food, water, a roof. A place to stop." She turned and looked at Thane and Korroc. "If it wasn't destroyed when the Worldwound came through, the station might even still be there."

Thane nodded. It was one nod, hard, the whole head going down and up.

"I need a horse."

Several people groaned. Caleth was fairly sure one of them was Aron. One of them might have been himself.

Aron looked at the tunnel mouth, where the light had gone amber and was going red.

"It's nightfall," he said. He looked from Thane to Korroc to Nageru to Caleth, and Caleth saw him take in the dust, the blood dried on Nageru's side, the scorched edge of Korroc's surcoat, the way Caleth himself was standing. "You were just in a fight. Shouldn't you rest? Heal a little?"

Thane shook his head.

"If I can't get a horse," he said, "I'll walk."

Aron's eyes went up and past Thane, and found Korroc, with the look of a man appealing to a higher court.

Korroc shrugged.

"If he insists on going," he said, "I'm going too."

That settled it. Caleth had known it would settle it the moment Thane opened his mouth, and he was already doing the arithmetic of what they would need and in what order. Food. Water. Light. And one thing he would not go without.

"Someone get us a kettle," he said. "To take with us. We'll use it as we travel."

Dagna stepped forward.

"I just watched you nearly carried off by a drake," she said to Thane. "And *that* one" — a finger, square at Caleth — "had it *land* on him. This is insanity. You can rest tonight and leave in the morning."

Korroc went to her, and put his arm around her again.

"Ma," he said, gently. "We're leaving now, or we're leaving in the morning. Is there really that much difference? We're going to go. We're going to get there. And we'll be back."

Dagna put her fingers to her temples and closed her eyes.

"You two," she said, "are *definitely* your fathers' sons."

She let out a long breath.

"Fine. But you'd better come back to me."

Then she opened her eyes and turned them on Caleth and Nageru, who were standing a little apart, and the look she gave them was the look of a woman handing over something breakable to strangers.

"And the two of *you* had better bring them back," she said, "or I swear I'll hunt you down myself."

"Yes," said Nageru, simply, and bowed.

Caleth found that he had bowed too.

She looked around the tunnel then — the smooth old walls, the bones, the timbers at the mouth — and sighed, and something in the sigh was almost wonder.

"Who figured out that this passage was here?" she said. "That this escape could have happened at all? I want to talk to this person."

And Caleth heard himself answer before he had decided to.

"His name is Aravashnial."

She turned to him.

He could feel the letters against his chest. He could feel the certificate inside the journal, and the page with three lines in a second ink, and the vial, all of them together in one pocket, the weight of them settling against each other as he breathed.

*His name is Aravashnial. He is my father's brother. He has been my uncle for twenty-five years and I found out four days ago, from a letter a demon threw at me. He watched me graduate. He sent me medicine when I was eight and dying of a fever. He found your husband's trail in four sources and moved a bridge up the list so that your son could follow it. And the last time I saw him, he was in an iron cage too small to stand in, with needles in his neck, screaming that my father had been murdered.*

"He is also missing," Caleth said. "We are doing our best to find him and bring him back."

Dagna looked at him for a moment. Caleth had the uncomfortable feeling that she was looking at a wall she had decided was carrying too much.

Then she nodded.

"Well," she said. "That's about the way things go up here, isn't it?"

---

Aron had horses brought up to the gate. Four of them, saddled, with bedrolls and water, which meant he had sent for them before he had finished arguing, which Caleth respected.

Nageru held up both hands at the sight of his.

"I am fine," he said, and smiled, and that was the end of it. Caleth had long since stopped asking how the aasimar kept pace with horses. He simply did, all day, on bare feet, and did not seem to find it remarkable.

The two dwarves mounted with help. A great deal of help. Korroc required two soldiers and a mounting block and a running commentary, in Dwarven, on horses, on the ancestry of horses, on the kind of person who would sit on a horse by choice, and on how he was only doing this for Thane, and how Thane had better appreciate it, and how he would be bringing this up at the family table for the next hundred years. Thane got up on his without a word and sat there like a man on a scaffold. His jaw was set. He had not taken his eyes off the east since the tunnel.

The sun was down. The light was going fast out of the sky.

"The horses can't see in the dark," Caleth said, to nobody in particular, and lifted Radiance, and spoke, and daylight came off the tip of the blade and pooled around the four of them in a clean white circle.

Korroc leaned down out of his saddle — carefully; he did not trust the horse — and took his mother's hand.

"We'll be back," he said. "I promise."

She held on to his hand. She was looking at him, and then past him at Thane, and her face was doing the thing it had done on the walk back, the door in the wind — but she was holding it this time, both hands on the latch.

"You'd better," she said.

They left.

---

They rode for hours.

North, and then north and a little west, along the edge of the dry riverbed that ran away from Drezen into the dark. It had been a river once — a real one, broad, with dwarves on its banks — and now it was a long pale scar of cracked mud and river stones under a sky with no stars in it, and the daylight off Radiance made a moving island of white on it that went ahead of them and behind them and showed them nothing but more of the same.

Nobody spoke. The horses' hooves made a sound on the stones like a slow drum. Nageru ran beside them — not fast, not slow, a long easy stride he could apparently hold until the end of the world — and his breathing was the only other sound.

Caleth rode with his hand over his coat.

He thought about a crib. He thought about a man staring at it and smiling like a fool when he thought no one was watching, and failing, frequently, to hide it. He thought about a woman who sang.

He thought about his uncle, somewhere under this same black sky, in the dark, with nobody to sing to him.

After a long time, when he had lost track of how many hours, he broke the silence.

"I think," he said, "some rest would be best. Before we get wherever we are going."

Thane did not answer at once. He rode on for perhaps a dozen strides, looking north. Then his shoulders came down a fraction.

"We can stop," he said. "I just wanted to get this started. And get out of that citadel."

Caleth understood that entirely.

---

They made camp in the lee of the old riverbank where a spur of rock broke the wind. A small fire, because Caleth let the daylight go and somebody had to see to eat by. A light meal out of Aron's saddlebags. Nobody talked much. Korroc checked everyone twice, with a hand on the shoulder and a look, and Caleth felt the thread go taut and slack again each time.

Caleth lay down with his coat over him and his hand on his chest.

He was dozing almost at once. He had not slept properly in four days, and the fight had taken the rest of him, and the long ride had taken what was left of that. He felt himself go.

---

He was standing in a corridor he had never seen before.

Stone walls. Stone floor. No windows.

There were doors on either side of him, iron-banded, shut. He knew, the way dreams know, that none of them mattered.

Except one.

At the end of the corridor there was a closed iron door.

Someone was behind it. He knew that with the absolute certainty dreams sometimes give — not a guess, not a hope, a *fact*, like the fact of the floor.

Aravashnial was behind that door.

He started toward it.

The Spiral burned.

Three steps. The pain got worse — a hot pressure across his shoulder blade, spreading.

Five.

He heard something behind the door. A chain, dragged across stone.

He ran.

The corridor stretched. The walls went on and on and the door at the end of them got no closer, however fast he ran, however hard — it sat at the end of the long stone throat like the moon sits at the end of a road.

Another sound. Metal.

And then Aravashnial's voice.

Not shouting. Not calling for help. Just one word, quiet, the way the elf said things when he had finished arguing and meant it.

*"No."*

Caleth reached for the door.

A hand closed around his wrist.

He turned.

There was no one there.

Only a shadow on the wall. A man's shadow, tall, in the shape of a man in robes, lying long across the stone beside him with one arm out, its hand closed around the shadow of his wrist.

There was no man casting it.

The Spiral burned as if someone had pressed a hot iron flat against his shoulder and was leaning on it.

---

He woke with his hand clamped over the mark.

He had jerked upright. He knew that because he was sitting, and the coat was on the ground, and his heart was going like a hammer on an anvil, and he had made a sound — he did not know what sound — loud enough that everyone around the fire was moving.

His shoulder was on fire.

Korroc got to him first. He was on his knees beside him before Caleth had finished understanding where he was, one gauntlet on Caleth's arm, turning him toward the embers.

"What happened?" Korroc's eyes went to the hand on the shoulder, and his voice sharpened. "Some sort of attack?"

Caleth did not move the hand. He kept it there, flat over his shirt, over the mark, and let the dwarf look at the hand and the shoulder around it and nothing else.

He shook his head.

"I think," he said, and his voice came out rough, "Aravashnial is trying to send me a message."

They all heard it. He saw them hear it. Thane had sat up on his bedroll with one knife half out of habit and stopped with it there. Nageru was standing at the edge of the firelight, very still, looking at him.

Korroc rocked back on his heels and looked at him.

"Are you all right?"

Caleth nodded. "I'm fine."

Korroc nodded too. He did not move away. He stayed there on his knees and looked at Caleth's face for a long moment — long enough that Caleth felt the thread between them go taut, as though the dwarf were testing it — and then, slowly, he got up and went back to his side of the fire.

He did not lie down for some time.

Caleth sat with his hand on his shoulder and felt the burn go out of it, slowly, the way it had in Drezen. It left an ache behind that lasted until he could see the sky go gray.

*He is trying to reach me,* he thought. *He is behind a door and he cannot open it and he is trying to reach me the only way that is left.*

He believed it. He found that he believed it absolutely.

He did not let himself think about the hand on his wrist.

---

In the morning he scried.

He did it at the fire, with the kettle Aron had found them — a dented copper thing that had been in a kitchen until yesterday afternoon — and water from the skins, and the other three sitting around him because none of them were going to be anywhere else. He pushed his sleeve up. He said the name.

"Aravashnial."

It was different this time.

He knew it was different almost at once. For three mornings the bridge had been shut in his face before it touched the far bank. This time it *reached*. He felt it go out and touch and hook and hold, the way it had in the command room, and the relief went through him so hard he nearly lost it — *there* — and the surface of the water went dark, and began to clear, and he leaned forward to look —

— and something turned it off.

Not the collar's chisel through the wire. Something bigger. Something that was *waiting* for it. The span did not break; it was denied. As though the spell had opened a door and found a hand on the other side already holding it shut, and the hand had simply leaned.

It threw him backward off his knees.

He landed on his back in the dirt beside the fire, and the kettle went over, and the water hissed on the embers.

They were all at his side in a heartbeat. Korroc's hand, Thane's, Nageru's, all at once.

Caleth lay there for a moment, looking up at a gray sky, and got his breath back.

"It felt like the magic connected," he said. "And as it opened — something just turned it off. Something *denied* it."

He sat up.

"Hard."

Nobody said anything. Korroc looked at him. Thane looked at the overturned kettle. Nageru looked north, up the riverbed, toward whatever was a day's ride out there.

Caleth got up. He dusted himself off. He said something about needing a moment to think it through, and walked away from the fire, out along the old riverbank, with his hands behind his back in the posture of a man considering a problem, until he was around a spur of rock and out of sight of all three of them.

Then he took off his coat. And his tabard. And his shirt. He took a small hand mirror out of his pack — a vanity he had carried since the Academy and had been mocked for exactly once — and turned his back to the pale morning, and twisted, and held the glass up over his shoulder until he could see it.

The Seeker's Spiral.

It lay across his shoulder blade as it had lain every day of his life. The same shape. The same curve. The same faint paleness against the skin around it, as if it had been drawn there in a lighter ink than the rest of him.

He looked at it for a long time.

Nothing had changed.

He stood behind a rock in the Worldwound in the cold with his shirt in his hand, a day north of Drezen on a trail two dwarves had followed decades ago, three days short of a demon's deadline, and looked at the mark his father had been afraid of.

*Nothing has changed,* he told himself.

He found he could not tell whether that was meant to be a comfort.
