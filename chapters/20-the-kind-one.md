# The Kind One

*Chapter Twenty — Three Attempts*

---

The light he had made was the brightest thing in a hundred miles, and Caleth had been walking under it for six hours doing the only arithmetic that mattered, which was: *nothing has come yet.*

That was not a result. He knew it was not a result. A man who lights a fire on a plain and is not eaten has learned nothing about what lives on the plain; he has only learned that it was not hungry in the last six hours. But the sum kept presenting itself anyway, the way sums did when he was tired, and Caleth Aranor had been awake since a morning that had contained a lava chasm and a possessed priest and a demon he had killed by drifting four inches sideways, and he was tired in a way that had gotten into his teeth.

So he walked, and carried noon on the point of a glaive through the middle of the Worldwound, and counted.

Fifty feet of hard white daylight in every direction. Black shadows thrown off every rock, sharp as cut paper, wheeling slowly as the light moved with him. Beyond that a ring of gray where the light gave out, and beyond the gray, nothing — the Wounded Lands going away east under a sky with too few stars in it, and everything out there able to see exactly where he was.

Which had been the point. He had said so out loud, to Thane, in a tone he was slightly ashamed of now. *I would rather be attacked in the light than ambushed in the dark.*

He still believed it. He had simply not expected to have to keep believing it for six hours.

Nageru walked on his left, a pace off his shoulder, barefoot on ground that would have opened Caleth's soles in an hour. The aasimar had not spoken since the second hour. He did not seem to be enduring the walk. He seemed to be *doing* it, the way another man does a craft.

Behind them, the wagon: Thane on the box with the reins loose in his hands, the horse plodding with its head down and its ears back because horses in the Worldwound learn things; and Korroc in the bed with his back against the boards and his hammer across his knees, sitting up over a sleeping priest like a man sitting up with a sick child.

Caleth looked back at them at intervals. Not for a reason. It was simply a thing he had started doing some weeks ago and had not stopped.

On the fourth of those looks, Korroc was not looking at the wagon bed. He was looking up and behind, out past the edge of the light, with his head tipped.

"Um," said Korroc.

Caleth stopped walking. The light stopped with him, and the shadows swung around and settled.

"That was big," Korroc said.

Thane turned around on the box. "What?"

"Something in the distance behind us." The dwarf shrugged, one gauntlet coming up and going down. "In the sky. It went across the moon."

"How big?" said Thane.

"Big enough that I could tell it went across the moon."

Thane did not turn back. He sat sideways on the box with the reins gathered in one hand and his shoulders squared to the dark behind the wagon, and he stayed that way — watching, while everybody else talked.

Caleth looked where Korroc had looked. There was the moon, thin and low and the color of old bone. There was the sky. There was nothing in it at all.

He held that for four full seconds — long enough to be sure it was nothing, and then long enough after that to be sure the nothing was not simply the light in his own eyes making a hole out of the dark. Which it was. Which it would be, regardless. He had built himself a lantern and stood inside it and made the entire sky unavailable to himself, and he had known that when he built it, and had traded it away on purpose for fifty feet of certainty at his feet.

*Every choice is a trade. Make the trade and stop admiring it.*

"Anything else?" he said.

"No," said Korroc.

On the box, Thane went on looking at the dark for a while longer.

Then he frowned, and nodded — to himself, at nothing, agreeing with some private assessment nobody else had been offered — and turned back around and picked up the reins and did not say a word, which from Thane was a full sentence with a subject and a verb.

They went on.

Caleth put the shape in the sky into the ledger where he put things he could do nothing about, which was a large ledger and was getting larger, and had entries in it going back nineteen years.

---

Not long after that, the horse screamed.

It was not a whinny. It was the other sound, the one horses make perhaps twice in their lives, and Caleth had spun and drawn breath for a spell before his mind had finished translating it.

The horse was up on its back legs in the traces, hauling the whole wagon two feet sideways with a scream of dry axle, and Thane was already off the box and going for the bridle, and Nageru —

Nageru was gone from Caleth's shoulder and standing at the horse's head, both hands on its cheekstraps, his face six inches from a rolling white eye. He said something in a low even voice that Caleth did not catch. The horse came down onto four feet with a crash and stood there shuddering.

And out of the dark, running, came the reason.

It came in low and fast over broken ground, and the light found it at forty feet and made it entirely, horribly clear.

A spider. That was the first word, and it was so inadequate that Caleth's mind rejected it and then had to take it back, because there was no other word. It was the size of a war elephant. Its body was a dark blue-black chitin that the daylight did not gloss so much as *sink into*, and it ran on six legs — and the front two were not legs at all. They were scythes. Two curved blades of the same blue chitin, longer than a man, held up and forward like a mantis's, and they came into the light with the whole weight of the thing behind them.

Below the eyes was a mouth full of fangs, and below the mouth, two stingers, working.

"*Bebilith,*" Caleth said. He heard his own voice do it — flat, loud, and entirely certain, the voice of a man reading a label.

He had read about them in the Blackwing, in a book with a cracked spine, in the days when he had believed reading about a thing was a kind of preparation. The relevant fact came up whole and was worse than the animal.

*It is not a demon's servant. It hunts demons. In the Abyss it eats them. It came here because it followed something, or because it was hungry, and there is no third reason.*

Which meant there was nothing to negotiate with. There was no cult behind it, no plan, no leader to kill. There was a thing that ate demons for a living and it had smelled four men and a horse.

The arithmetic came up in half a heartbeat and came up ugly. Korroc was in a wagon bed with an unconscious man. Thane was on the ground and in its path. Nageru had both hands full of terrified horse. Caleth had six spells worth having and roughly two seconds.

Korroc solved his own problem first. He came up out of the wagon bed, reached over the front board, and put one gauntlet flat against Thane's shoulder — and Caleth felt the working go, a low golden pull like a rope drawing taut somewhere under the world. Korroc always carried a share of what the others took. Now he had gone and widened the pipe.

*Half,* Caleth thought. *He's taking half.*

Then the dwarf vaulted the side of the wagon in seventy pounds of gold-and-mithral plate, hit the ground with a crack you could feel through your boots, and set his shield.

Thane hauled the horse's head around and backed the wagon four steps and put his own body between it and the thing coming, which was insane, and which was Thane.

Caleth crossed to him in three strides and put two fingers on the back of his neck and spoke.

Thane wavered, the way heat wavers, and was not there.

"Left," said Thane's voice, from somewhere, moving.

The bebilith did not slow. It had picked Korroc — the biggest, the brightest, the one standing still — and it came the last twenty feet in a rush that flung gravel behind it, and the daylight ran along its scythes like water off oil.

Thane struck first. Caleth saw it: a wound opening on the near foreleg out of nothing at all, a black split, a spray. But the thing was *fast*, faster than a body that size had any business being, and it was already past where the blow had been aimed, and the cut went shallow and wrong.

Then it hit Korroc.

Caleth had watched Korroc Stonevein take hits for two years. He had seen him take a chimera's ice full in the chest and go down to one knee and get up. This was the first time he had ever seen him *moved.* The fangs came down and skated off the shield rim with a shriek of metal. The first scythe came in on the low line and hit the shield square, and Korroc's boots plowed six inches of gravel backward and held.

The second scythe went over the top.

There was simply too much of it. The shield was a shield, and the thing was an elephant, and the blade came over the upper edge and down and took Korroc across the shoulder and back, and the sound of it on that armor was the sound of a chisel finding a seam.

Korroc grunted. That was all.

Caleth was already moving his hands.

He built the ray-form fast and clean, and he built it hot, because hot was the one his hands knew without being asked.

There was a half-second in there — there always was — where he could have taken the shape apart and put it back together wrong. It was the thing he did. Fire was a recipe; recipes had ingredients; and if you were fast enough and rude enough you could swap one out on the way to the table. He had turned fire into ice on a bridge over the Ahari and into acid over a gorge and into killing frost in a chapel full of ghouls, and Aravashnial had never once been satisfied with his explanation of how.

He did not take the half-second. Korroc was being opened up eleven feet away and the half-second was a half-second.

Three rays of fire left his hand and went across the gap in a line and struck the thing's flank and thorax, one two three, and the blue chitin blackened and cracked and the whole beast shuddered.

And Caleth, watching it, felt the specific small sinking of a man who has been given his answer immediately.

*Not enough.* It had gone in — it had certainly gone in — but it had gone in like a lamp held against a wall, spreading and thinning, and the thing had eaten most of it on the way. Of course it had. It had been born in a furnace. It had spent its whole life in one. He had known that four seconds ago; he had put it in a sentence about the Abyss and then failed to draw the obvious conclusion from his own sentence because there had been an armored dwarf in the way of his attention.

*You had the half-second,* he thought. *You had it and you spent it being fast.*

The bebilith made a noise. Caleth had no comparison for it and did not want one.

"*Now,* Korroc," he said.

Korroc had already reached down and turned something on. Caleth felt it more than saw it — a blur at the ankles, a wrongness in the dwarf's timing, everything about him suddenly arriving a half-beat early. He had found the boots in a demon's wardrobe under Drezen and had been quietly delighted about them for three weeks.

Then Korroc did the other thing, the one Caleth had never gotten used to. He set his back foot and reached *down*, past the gravel, into whatever was under it, and the stone answered him. You could see it happen. A man went from standing on the ground to being part of it.

The hammer came up and came down.

Once — into the shoulder joint of the near scythe, and the joint gave.

Twice — across the thorax, into the black crack Caleth's fire had opened, and the crack ran a foot further.

Three times — and the third one was the one. It caught the thing across the head and something in there broke inward with a crunch that Caleth felt in his own jaw.

Thane's daggers opened the far side again out of empty air.

The bebilith reeled — and came back in. It should not have been able to. It hit Korroc again, and again the one scythe found the way over the shield and hacked down into him, and the fangs and the other blade came off the shield with nothing to show, and Caleth thrust Radiance into the gap between them, hard, both hands, the leaf-blade lit and going in at the seam of a joint.

It moved. The great blue body slid four feet sideways in a way that nothing that size should manage, and the glaive went past it into the dark.

*Twice,* Caleth thought, disgusted. *Twice now.*

Korroc's hammer landed twice more. Then the thing learned, and shifted, and the third swing went by.

And then Thane killed it.

There was no drama in it, which was the Thane of it. Two ragged gashes opened along the underside where nothing was standing, one and then the other, deep and unhurried and precisely where a man would put them if he had been walking beside the animal for the last ten seconds studying its gait.

The bebilith's legs went out from under it in the wrong order. It came down on its side, hard enough to shake the wagon. It spasmed once, the scythes closing on nothing.

And then it was not there. It went out of the world like a held breath let go — not a body, not a corpse, just an absence and a smell and a shallow dent in the gravel where an elephant had lain for a second and a half.

Silence. The horse blowing. The daylight standing over all of it, flat and cheerful and hideous.

Thane shimmered back into being about eight feet from where Caleth had thought he was.

"Well," he said, "that's what the light was for."

Caleth looked at him.

"I'm agreeing with you," Thane said. "Don't make a thing of it."

Korroc had his palm inside his own collar, going after the wounds under the plate. Golden warmth ran down the gaps in the gold-and-mithral and pooled and went in. He straightened, rolled the shoulder, and looked at the dent in the gravel with an expression of pure professional grievance.

"Went home," he said.

"They do," said Caleth. "It isn't dead. It's just not here. It'll wake up in the Abyss in about a year with a very poor opinion of dwarves."

"Aye, well." Korroc hefted the hammer. "It'll be a correct one."

Nageru let go of the horse's cheekstraps and stroked its neck twice and stepped back, and the horse, which had been about to bolt into the Worldwound alone in the dark, put its head down and began, insanely, to look for grass.

Caleth watched that for a second and filed it with the other things he had no name for.

---

They came down off the escarpment at Eagle Rock in the gray hour before the sun, and heard the fighting before they saw the village.

It carried up the cliff face in pieces — a shout, another shout, the flat unmistakable clap of iron on a shield boss, and under all of it the particular ragged noise of people who are not soldiers making noise on purpose because it is better than being quiet.

Caleth had them down the switchback faster than was safe. Thane took the wagon; the rest went ahead on foot, and Caleth ran with Radiance in one hand and the daylight running with him down a cliff path in the dark, and every step of the way he was doing the sum and hating it.

*Three minutes. Four. Whatever is happening down there is finished before we arrive.*

It was.

They came into Vilareth Ford in the first gray of dawn to find a fight that had ended perhaps ninety seconds earlier: men and women standing in the road with weapons still up and no one left to point them at, a tipped cart, a burning thatch someone was already beating out with a wet sack, two bodies in the mud that were not villagers, and the specific silence of people who had won and had not yet decided how they felt about it.

Kamilo Dann came out of it at a run, saw the light, and came straight for them the way a person comes toward a thing they have been hoping for.

She had been a prisoner in a rope pen when they first met her, what seemed a year and a country ago, with her wrists tied. She was a supply master now. She had a ledger board tucked under one arm even at this hour, and mud to the knee, and a bruise coming up on her cheekbone that she had not yet noticed.

"What was that?" Caleth said. He was looking past her, at the bodies.

Kamilo shrugged. It was a real shrug, tired and complete. "Grunhuld barbarians. We've had problems with them — trying to raid our supplies, trying to take people."

Caleth walked to the nearer of the two dead men and crouched.

His eye went over the body the way it went over everything, and it made him a full report he had not asked for. A big man. Kellid, tall, hide and mail scraps and a good axe he had made himself. Dead about two minutes of a spear in the side.

And along the forearms and the ridge of the shoulders, standing up out of the leather at an angle — spikes of dull bone, ivory-colored, hand-length, swept back.

Caleth's hand stopped an inch short of them.

Then he took hold of one, and turned it, and it turned.

*Ah.*

He crouched further down and read the fastening, because a fastening is a sentence about the man who tied it. The spike had been drilled at the base and pegged through boiled leather and whipped down with sinew, and the whipping had been done twice — once well, and once again later and worse, by somebody in a hurry or by somebody else entirely. The bone itself was old. It had been handled a great deal. The ridges of it were worn smooth at the places a hand would go if you were nervous and had something to fiddle with.

He pushed the leather aside with two fingers and looked at the shoulder underneath.

Skin. Ordinary skin, scarred the way a fighting man's shoulder is scarred, and nothing coming out of it at all.

*Not grown. Worn.*

Which was a different fact entirely, and a considerably stranger one, and he did not have anywhere to put it yet.

Behind him, Thane had come up and gone quiet, which with Thane meant he had arrived at something.

"That's what she was," he said. "Isn't it."

Caleth did not have to ask who.

"Jestak," he said.

"The siege captain." Thane crouched on the other side of the body, took hold of one of the bone spikes, and turned it exactly as Caleth had — arriving at the same place three seconds later by a different road, which was how the two of them had always worked. "Furs. Wild hair. And a pair of horns *bolted* onto her shoulder plates." He let go of the spike and let it swing. "I remember thinking somebody had gone to a lot of trouble to look like something they weren't."

He looked up.

"Same people?"

"Same people." Caleth had known it the moment Kamilo said *Grunhuld*, and had been sitting on it for ninety seconds without deciding what to do with it. "She never named the clan. But yes. And she wore these same horns on her armor."

Thane nodded slowly and said nothing else, which was the whole of his comment on the subject, and was worse than a speech.

Because Caleth remembered the rest of that room too, and it did not sit down quietly.

Jestak had radiated evil. Not faintly. Korroc had read it and said so; Caleth had read it himself and had not argued. Two paladins in one doorway with the same answer, and it had been the correct answer — his sight had told him the truth, cleanly, and the truth had been *evil*, and Thane Stonevein had put his hand out anyway on a hunch he had never adequately explained and had never stopped arguing with himself about since.

And every single thing she told them had been true. The chambers below. The Banner below. Joran Vhane.

Caleth looked down at the dead man in the mud, who had come here in the dark to steal grain and to take people, and who had gone to the trouble, first, of tying old bones to his coat.

*Both of those are true,* he thought. *He was raiding, and he was in costume, and neither of those facts does anything at all to the other one.*

And there was a question inside the second one that he could not answer from a corpse, because a man dresses up as a thing for exactly two reasons and they look identical from the outside.

*Either you want to be it,* Caleth thought, *or you are hoping it will not notice you.*

He put it in the ledger with the shape in the sky, and found that it sat in there considerably heavier.

He stood up.

"What have you done to discourage the raiding?" he said.

Kamilo's face did a small tired thing. "Everything we can think of. They just send more. We've taken a few alive — they don't talk, and the first chance they get, they fight. A couple have escaped. The rest are dead." She shifted the ledger board under her arm. "We know where their settlement is. We don't have the people to go into the Worldwound and do anything about it."

She looked at him then, straight on, and shook her head once, and said the thing that ended the conversation before Caleth could make it into a plan.

"Our first priority is keeping the supply route open to Drezen."

And she was *right*, and Caleth had the good grace to feel it. Every wagon that came up this road was arrows and grain and boots for a fortress a hundred and forty miles into hostile country, and one raid too many on this village and Drezen starved in the spring. She was not being small. She was being a quartermaster, which is a thing very few people are willing to be.

He turned to the others.

"We need to get Jesker back to Drezen," he said. "His being alive is worth something to that garrison, and he needs to start recovering somewhere with a roof."

Korroc nodded. "Aye."

Then Nageru stepped forward.

"I would ask leave to go to Drezen as well."

Caleth turned to look at him.

"I am sorry, my friends," Nageru said. "There is business I must tend to, and it will not keep."

That was all he offered, and Caleth was aware — with the part of him that never stopped being aware of such things — that he wanted to ask, and that the wanting was not admirable. A man who has spent his whole life holding his own cards close develops a considerable appetite for other people's.

He was still deciding not to ask when Nageru told him anyway.

"Korroc offered that man atonement," the aasimar said. "Yesterday afternoon. In the cavern, on his knees, in front of all of us." He looked at the wagon, where a priest of Erastil lay unconscious under a blanket in the dawn. "It has not been performed."

"It's been a day," Caleth said. "He has been unconscious for most of it."

"Yes. And in an hour he will be in a bed in that fortress, being cared for by people who know exactly what he did to their shrine, and he will stop being a man who was offered something and start being a patient." Nageru's voice did not change at all. "A patient is a thing that waits. Nobody is unkind to him. Nobody refuses him. It simply moves down the list every morning, because there is always something bleeding, and a day becomes a week, and a week becomes the spring."

He paused.

"I have watched that happen in a house of forty people who did nothing all day but be disciplined. That garrison has six hundred and a war."

Nobody said anything.

"Sosiel Vaenic can do it. He is in Drezen and he is a good man and he will do it properly." Nageru's hands were loose at his sides. "But he does not know it was offered. Somebody has to be there who was in that room, and say what was said in it, and stand up when it is done."

He inclined his head very slightly.

"So I would like to go and stand there."

Caleth looked at him for a moment.

He was conscious of a small ignoble sting somewhere under his ribs, and he examined it honestly, because that was the arrangement he had with himself. It was not that he disagreed. It was that Nageru had done a sum — a bed, a list, a shrine full of people who remembered — and had done it about a *man*, in a place where Caleth had not thought to look, while Caleth was being efficient about wagons.

*I have been moving that priest across a hundred miles of the Worldwound since yesterday afternoon and it did not once occur to me that we had left something undone in the middle of him.*

"Of course," he said. "Go."

Off to the side, Thane was leaning on the wagon wheel with his arms folded, and Caleth saw him hear all of it and saw him decline, deliberately, to say anything about it. It was not indifference. It had the shape of a thing already discussed once, some days ago, in words Caleth had not been given.

"You don't have to apologize," Caleth said.

"No," Nageru agreed. "But I am sorry. It is a poor time to be one man short."

"It is exactly the right time," said Korroc, from the wagon.

He had not spoken since Kamilo left. He was sitting on the tailboard with his hammer across his knees and his beard in a state, and he did not elaborate, and he did not look at anybody, and after a moment he got down and went to see to the horse.

So Caleth gathered up an unconscious priest, and an aasimar who had noticed something nobody else had, and folded the world.

The step took him through that half-second of nowhere that he had never entirely gotten used to and had never once admitted, and put the three of them in a cold stone corridor in Citadel Drezen with a startled crusader dropping a bucket ten feet away. He got Jesker Helton into hands that knew what to do with him. He gave the man's condition to a Shelynite chirurgeon in nine sentences.

Then he stood in the corridor with Nageru for a moment.

"I could stay for it," Caleth said.

He meant it, and he was also aware — the second layer, always the second layer — that Sosiel Vaenic wore Shelyn's symbol, and that Caleth had noticed it eleven months ago in a command tent and had never once said a word about it to the man, and that here was an entirely respectable reason to be standing in a room with him.

"You are needed at the Ford," Nageru said. "And it does not require an audience. It requires a witness. There is a difference, and one is enough."

Nageru bowed to him — the full one, the one that meant it — and turned and walked away down the corridor, barefoot on cold stone, and did not look back.

Caleth stood there for a moment.

Then he folded the world again and was back at Vilareth Ford, alone, before the sun had properly cleared the escarpment, and the whole thing had taken less than an hour, and nobody in the village had noticed he had been gone at all.

That was the part he thought about, later, on the road. The hour. He had crossed a hundred and forty miles of the Worldwound twice, and delivered a man from a lava chasm into a hospital bed, and been told the difference between an audience and a witness by a man with no shoes, and been back in time for breakfast, and the ordinary world had not so much as flinched.

*You could do a great deal of damage that way,* he thought, *if you were the wrong sort of person.*

He put that in the ledger too.

---

They went to Kamilo again that night, and asked her about the barbarians properly.

"They act like men," she said. She had a mug of something in both hands and had finally noticed the bruise and had done nothing about it. "But they've got the bone spikes coming through the skin. And they feel — " she made a small helpless motion — "wrong. Like they've got the Abyss in them somewhere. And I'll tell you now, they're ferocious fighters. Every one of them. Not brave. *Ferocious.* There's a difference and I've learned it."

"The one I looked at this morning had them tied on," Caleth said.

Kamilo stopped with the mug halfway up.

"Tied on."

"Drilled and pegged through the leather and whipped down with sinew. Old bone, well handled. There was nothing under it but a shoulder." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table. "I'm not telling you your business. I'm telling you what was on the body."

She sat with that for a moment, and then she laughed — one short breath of it, without much humor in it, the laugh of a woman being shown something she is going to be annoyed about later.

"Well," she said. "You'll not find a soul in this village who'd tell you different. Not one. And I'd have sworn to it myself this morning."

"You weren't lying to me."

"No. I was twenty yards off in the dark with a man coming at me, and I saw *bones sticking out of him*, and I did not stop to check the knots." She drank. "Saints. It's amazing anybody ever learns anything true about anything."

Caleth said nothing to that, because he agreed with it entirely and had nothing to add.

But he took the *ferocious* seriously, and said so.

"There is a difference," he said. "And a man who fights like that usually has a reason, and the reason is usually standing behind him."

"You're going out there, aren't you."

"We're going to go and look at it."

Kamilo laughed, once, without much in it, and drank.

In the morning she had three horses in the road.

Not the tall crusade chargers — small ones, thick-legged, shaggy, with the deep chests of animals bred to work in bad country. Caleth had asked for exactly that.

Korroc looked at them for a long moment.

"No," he said.

"It will save half a day."

"I've legs."

"You have excellent legs. They are eleven inches long."

Thane made a sound that he turned into a cough.

"You," said Korroc, rounding on him. "You do not like riding either."

Thane sighed and gave the horse a sideways glance. "But I will," said Thane. "Half a day is half a day."

It took a further ten minutes. In the end both dwarves got up onto the shaggy little horses looking like a pair of siege engines being transported by cart, and they grumbled about it for the first three hours, in shifts, with an organization that suggested prior arrangement, and Caleth rode ahead and let them, and did not point out that they had both stopped grumbling around midmorning, when the pace started telling and the miles started falling behind, and that neither of them had said a word about it since.

The country went bad by degrees, the way it always did. First the color left it. Then the shapes did. By afternoon they were riding through low broken hills the color of a bruise, under a sky that had gone the flat sick orange that meant nothing at all except that you were now well inside the Wound, and the horses' ears were flat and their heads were low, and nothing was hunting them, and it took work to remember that not being hunted was the normal condition of the world.

They saw the signs of the village before they saw the village. Smoke, in three thin threads. Cut stumps. A path worn by feet.

*Life,* Caleth thought, and was surprised by how strange the word felt out here, and how much of what came with it was dread.

"Looks like we need to go and talk to them," he said.

He said it lightly, which was a choice.

"Korroc?"

Korroc looked at the smoke for a while.

"Aye," he said.

---

They rode in slowly, at a walk, with their hands visible.

It was a village of tents pitched among old foundations. That was the first thing Caleth's eye gave him, unasked and unwelcome: the tents were *inside* something. There had been buildings here once, a proper steading, laid out by people who intended to stay — and the buildings were gone down to their footings, and hide tents had been put up in the squares where rooms had been, so that the whole settlement was a sort of ghost of a floor plan with cloth growing out of it.

The second thing his eye gave him was the people.

They backed away. All of them, everywhere, without exception. A woman gathering a cook pot straightened, saw them, and went backward three steps with the pot held against her chest. Two children stopped dead in the path and then were simply gone, into a tent, without a sound. A man mending a strap did not look up at all, which Caleth realized after a moment was a strategy.

And they were beaten. Not battered — some were, but that was not it. *Beaten.* Caleth's painter's eye, which his foster father had trained on faces before it was ever trained on anything else, went across twenty people in ninety seconds and did not find one whose shoulders were where shoulders belong. They were thin. They were haunted. They were quiet in a way that children are not quiet unless they have been taught.

He had come here expecting a war camp. He had assembled, on the ride, a fairly good speech for a war camp.

He put the speech away.

*This is not a raiding people. This is a raided people who have been sent out to raid.*

They stopped in what had been a square. Caleth dismounted, and picked, out of all of them, an older man near a tent flap — because the man had backed away only one step instead of four, and had not looked at the ground.

"We need to speak with your leadership," Caleth said.

He said it in Hallit, which he had learned out of a book in Kenabres when he was nineteen because a Kellid trader had once been rude to him in it and he had disliked not knowing exactly how rude, and which he had spoken aloud perhaps forty times since.

The man said nothing at all.

He raised one arm and pointed, and Caleth followed the line of it, and there it was in the middle of the tents: a stone hall.

The only building left standing in the whole steading. Squat, thick-walled, a peaked roof, no windows anywhere — just small vents up under the eaves for smoke. Fifty feet of ground cleared by axe all around it. Caleth's eye read the construction and gave its verdict without being asked: *this was built to be defended, and it was built well, and it is the oldest thing here by two hundred years, and everything else in this village was knocked down and this was not.*

They walked the horses toward it.

On the east side of the hall was a heap.

Caleth made himself look at it, because looking away is how the ugly thing wins, and he had made that rule for himself in a defiled hall in Citadel Drezen and had kept it since. So he looked, and catalogued, and did not flinch outwardly.

Broken weapons. Scraps of crusader mail. Cook-bones and refuse. And in among all of it, without any particular attempt at concealment, parts of people.

Things flapped and shrieked and scattered off the heap as they came near — scavengers, half-bald, diseased-looking, going up in a ragged cloud with a noise like a hundred rusty hinges.

Caleth watched the noise go up over the hall, and understood the entire defensive arrangement of the village in one second.

*They don't post sentries. They feed them.*

The three of them stopped in front of the double doors. Every person in sight was watching them. Nobody had followed.

Caleth glanced at Korroc.

Korroc glanced back and shrugged, in seventy pounds of gold-and-mithral plate, in the middle of the Worldwound, in front of a demon-worshipper's hall.

"Knock?"

Thane went up and knocked.

---

The doors came open a hand's width and an eye looked out, and a voice behind it said something in Hallit that Caleth understood and Thane did not.

Thane's shoulders did the small tightening that meant he was being spoken at in a language he had no purchase on, which Caleth knew he loathed above nearly all things.

The man repeated it in Common. "Who are you and what do you want?"

Thane hesitated a fraction. It was the fraction of a man who is about to say something that will get people killed and has not decided which people.

Caleth spoke over his shoulder. "We are Knights of the Fifth Crusade, and we are here to speak with your leader."

Thane recovered, glowered at the door with great sincerity, and took a step forward.

The door shut. Behind it, talking.

Caleth listened. The doorman's voice, low and unhappy, and clear enough through two inches of oak: *"They want to see Marhevok."*

*Marhevok.* He put the name away carefully.

The door opened. The man at it stood aside without a word. Further in stood a second man, who looked at them for a moment, turned, and simply started walking back into the dark of the hall.

Caleth went first. Thane came behind him and Korroc came last, and Korroc coming last meant Korroc's shield was between the party and the door, which had not been discussed and never was.

The entry hall stopped him for half a step.

It was painted.

Every wall, floor to roof beam, in old flaking pigment: landscapes. Forests, with the light coming down through them in bars. A lake with birds standing in the shallows. Deer. Grasslands going away to hills, and out in front of all of it, standing tall in hides with bows and spears, proud-looking Kellid men and women looking out at whoever came through the door.

It was not good painting. Caleth's eye said so at once and without malice — the perspective was wrong in three places and the hands were beyond the painter entirely. And it was *beautiful* anyway, and Caleth knew exactly why, and the knowing put something cold down the middle of him.

It was beautiful because it was accurate. Somebody had stood in that country and loved it and come inside and put it on a wall.

And that country did not exist. It had not existed for a hundred years. Outside those doors was a bruise-colored waste with a midden of human parts on the east side of it, and inside was a lake with birds standing in the shallows, and the people who had painted it were dust, and their descendants had been marched back here to live inside the picture.

Two stuffed animals stood in alcoves flanking the inner doors — a rearing bear on the west, a golden eagle mounted on a stand carved like a tree on the east. Both were old. Both had been repaired, more than once, badly and with great care, by different hands.

*They carried these,* Caleth thought. *Out of here, and then back again. Somebody carried a stuffed eagle a hundred miles into the Worldwound.*

He walked through.

The next room was a common hall: a fire pit in the middle, a few tattered bearskins, an old shelf sagging under a heap of weapons and hide armor. Four people in it, who stared and did not move. And on these walls, murals again — but these had been defaced.

Caleth's eyes went over the damage as he walked and read it in three seconds, because damage is a composition too.

They had shown men and women in some kind of ceremonial fighting. Formal stances. An audience painted in around the edges, watching. Rules — you could see the rules in the postures, in the way the fighters were placed, in the fact that somebody was clearly *judging.*

And the faces had been taken off them. Not the bodies. Not the audience. The heads of the combatants specifically, gouged out, in a room full of undamaged forest.

*Somebody in this house objects to the idea of a fight with a judge in it.*

He filed that, and it turned out to be the single most useful thing he did all day, and he did not know it yet.

They went down a hallway. The escort knocked at a double door, said "Wait," went in, and shut it.

Muffled arguing. Two voices, one of them much louder and getting louder.

The door opened. "Enter."

Caleth went in, and Thane and Korroc came after, and behind them, unhurried, six more men filed in from the hall and stood along the wall.

Nine in the room, then. Caleth counted without turning his head.

The room had a low ledge running around the whole of it, and on the ledge stood columns carved into the shapes of men and women — a whole gallery of them, looking down into the middle of the floor with their carved faces set in what could only be judgment. To the southeast, a raised dais with a stone throne on it under piled furs. To the west, a pool of still water with a thin rime of ice glittering across it, which in a windowless hall with a fire in the next room was not possible.

Except that there was something lying in the pool.

Caleth's eye went to it and stayed a half-second too long. Dragon-shaped, blue, the length of a cart horse, its head laid along the ice and its slit pupils tracking them across the room with no expression whatsoever.

*Not a dragon. Something in the family and further down it.* He put the sum together: the ice, the cold, the size, the intelligence in the eye. *Breath weapon. Cone. Cold. If this goes badly, it opens with that and it will catch us.*

And on the throne, a large man.

Not enormous. Tall, deep-chested, deeply tanned, with lank black hair and gray eyes and a mouth set in an expression of permanent mild contempt. Caleth's painter's eye did the thing it always did and gave him the man's whole composition at a stroke: a good-looking man once, gone thick, sitting with the deliberate slouch of somebody who has learned that comfort in front of others is a display of power.

"Who are you?" he said, in Hallit.

Caleth answered in the same.

"We are Knights of the Fifth Crusade, and we have come to speak with you about the raids on Vilareth Ford."

There was a small shift around the room. He had answered in their language and it had cost them something.

"What do you want to talk about?"

"We want them to stop."

"Now why would we do that?"

"Because it is in your best interest."

The room laughed. It was not a good laugh. It was the laugh of nine men who have learned exactly when their leader wants to hear one.

"In our best interest." Marhevok's voice went down into a register he clearly enjoyed. "Best interest is what is best for my tribe."

"No longer," Caleth said. "There is an army coming."

He paused there, and then — because the whole point of the sentence was going to be lost if two thirds of his own party could not hear it — he added: "My friends do not speak your language. May we continue in Common?"

Marhevok waved a hand in a lazy arc.

"Continue," he said, in Common.

Caleth inclined his head.

"We have freed Drezen," he said. "It is no longer held by demons. We intend to close the Worldwound and to rid this land of what lives in it. We would like to work with you in the days coming. If you refuse, we will destroy you if we must."

He heard himself say it. He heard the shape of it — the flat courteous final clause, *if we must* — and noted with some distaste that it had come out sounding entirely sincere, because it was.

Marhevok had gone still on the throne.

Then he said, low, and half to himself:

"So you weren't sent by her."

Caleth's attention came to a point so fine it hurt.

"You're too weak," Marhevok said. "You're not from my mistress."

*Mistress.* *Sent.* *A woman who sends people to check on him and he expects it.*

He kept his face exactly as it had been.

Marhevok looked past him, down the line, and his eyes went over Korroc in gold plate, and over Caleth, and settled on the smallest thing in the room.

"Beverick," he said. "Take the smallest one."

Later, Caleth would be able to reconstruct the next second and a half in perfect order, because he had reconstructed it many times.

Thane cursed and both daggers came into his hands, low and reversed, and he went down half a step into the stance he used when he had decided the room was going to be a fight.

A man by the wall — big, bearded, a little older than the rest — hesitated. It was a real hesitation, a full beat of it, and then he unslung an axe and took one step forward with his eyes somewhere in the middle distance.

And Caleth heard his own voice come out of his own mouth, and did not entirely recognize the man it belonged to.

"*Touch my friend and be obliterated.*"

Radiance came off his back and into both hands, and Yaniel's blade lit up in a windowless hall in the middle of the Worldwound like somebody had opened a door onto noon.

Every carved face on the ledge leaped out of the dark at once. Nine men flinched. The thing in the pool raised its head off the ice.

*Obliterated,* Caleth thought, in the middle of it, with a kind of clinical dismay. *That was not a word. That was a threat wearing a word.*

He had come here to prevent a fight. He had just made the room a promise.

"Oh," said Marhevok.

He was smiling. He was, Caleth saw, delighted.

"You challenge?"

And there it was. Caleth felt the whole board turn over and reset in front of him, and he read it fast, because he had perhaps two seconds.

*Nine armed men and a drake in a stone room with one door. If it starts here it starts badly. He wants a challenge. He has told me he wants a challenge. A challenge is a fight with rules, and a fight with rules is a fight I do not have to win.*

He did not think about the gouged faces on the mural in the next room. He had seen it. He had filed it. He was tired, and he was fast, and he did not go back for it.

That was the error, and he would find it later, and it would be a bad hour.

"I'll make a deal," Caleth said. "If you can hit me once in three attempts, we concede."

He watched Marhevok take it.

He watched, specifically, the exact moment when the man understood that the terms were insulting — three, not one; *attempts*, not exchanges; and no offer of anything in return, because Caleth had deliberately not said what happened if he lost, and Marhevok had been too pleased to ask.

"I accept," said Marhevok, and stood, and walked out.

---

The whole village came.

They formed a great ring outside the hall in the cleared ground, the villagers watching from further back, and they did it in near silence and with practiced speed, which told Caleth that this had happened often and that attendance was not optional.

Korroc came up beside him at the edge of the circle.

"Lad," he said, low.

"I know."

"If this goes wrong —"

"If this goes wrong, get your back to the hall wall and let me put something between you and the crowd." Caleth rolled his shoulders. "It won't go wrong. He has to touch me three times. He is not going to touch me once."

Korroc looked at him for a moment with an expression Caleth could not read at all.

"Aye," he said, and stepped back.

Marhevok took his place in the ring and raised both arms.

"These strangers have come to challenge!" he roared, and the whole village made a sound. "We will show them the power of the Grunhuld Wintersun!"

And then he changed.

Caleth had two years of demons behind him and he still had to make himself stand still.

The tall thick-bodied man swelled. There was no other word for it. The chest went out, the arms doubled, the neck went; the skin darkened through red into a deep arterial color; horns came up out of the brow with a sound like green wood splitting.

And along the forearms and the shoulders and down the spine, the bone came through.

Not tied. Not pegged. Not whipped down twice with sinew by a man in a hurry. It tore up out of him from underneath, wet and sudden, in a long ripple down the length of his body — ivory, hand-length, swept back — and it was, in every particular except the blood, the exact thing Caleth had held between two fingers in the mud at Vilareth Ford at dawn and turned, and found that it turned.

Caleth's whole understanding of the last two days reorganized itself in about a second and a half, and did not improve.

*They are all copying him.*

Every fighter in that ring with old bone drilled and lashed to his shoulders. A woman a hundred and forty miles east with a pair of horns bolted to her pauldrons, standing alone in a room among twenty-four dead, who had read as evil to two paladins in one doorway.

*And I still cannot tell whether it is worship or camouflage.*

He looked, fast, at the faces around the circle — the nearest ring of them, the ones close enough to read.

Not one of them was cheering.

They were watching their clanliege come apart into a demon in the middle of his own steading with the flat, braced, careful attention of people watching weather come over a hill.

*Camouflage,* Caleth thought. *It's camouflage. They dress as the thing that eats them so it will look past them, and they have been doing it so long that everyone downriver believes the whole clan is what he is.*

*And the crusade believes it too. And they're not wrong enough for it to matter, because he is standing right there.*

The face stayed. That was the horror of it. The face was the same man's face, wearing the same contempt, with a demon's leer laid over it like a coat of varnish.

*Not possessed,* Caleth thought. *There is no seam. It's him. It's all the way through him and it goes with his temper.*

The village made no sound at all.

Caleth considered several responses and selected the correct one, which was an insult.

He raised one hand and mimed a yawn.

Then he leveled Radiance, and the blade flared white, and he set his feet in the way the Spire had taught him and Kenabres had taught him better.

Marhevok came.

He came like a landslide with a grudge, and the first blow was a fist the size of Caleth's head coming down in a straight overhand at the crown of his skull, and it was fast — genuinely fast, faster than a body that size had any business being, and Caleth's whole plan for the afternoon lived or died in the next quarter-second.

He did not step back. He turned the glaive up under the descending arm and took the fist on the shaft's angle and *swept* it aside, letting the man's own weight carry it past his shoulder, and it went by his ear close enough to move his hair.

Then, before Marhevok had recovered, Caleth reached out and tapped him on the shoulder with the flat of Radiance.

There was a spell in the blade.

He had put it there that morning, out of habit, the way you load a crossbow you do not expect to fire — because a dwarf had cut channels into a saint's glaive on a forge floor a few weeks ago and an elf had seated the working like a weld, and the thing the working *did* was hold a spell against the day a blade found flesh. Caleth had been carrying it charged ever since and had not yet had cause to let one go.

The flat of the blade touched a demon-blooded chieftain's shoulder, and Caleth did not discharge it.

*Three attempts. He touches me or he does not. That was the whole of the offer and the offer is the point.*

He held up one finger.

The village made a noise. A small one. It got out of somebody before they could stop it.

Marhevok's face went through several things and arrived at fury.

He came again, both hands this time, an overhand smash meant to end it.

And Caleth reached down into the gold.

He spent a little of it now, and he *left himself behind.*

There was no flash. That was the worst of it, afterward — no light, no sound, nothing for anybody to point at. Caleth Aranor simply went on standing where he had been standing, with the glaive leveled and an expression of mild civic patience on his face, and at the same time was thirty feet away at the edge of the ring, watching himself do it.

Marhevok's fists came down through the top of the man's skull and out through his chest and closed on air.

There was a pause of perhaps a quarter-second in which nothing at all happened, and the demon-blooded chieftain of the Grunhuld-Wintersun looked down at his own hands standing in the middle of a half-elf's ribcage.

Then he swung again, hard, sideways, through the same body.

Air.

The sound the crowd made this time was bigger, and had something dangerous in it.

Thirty feet away, the real Caleth coughed lightly into his fist.

Every head in the ring came around.

He held up two fingers. Then, after a moment's consideration, a third.

Marhevok charged.

And as he ran, he *shimmered* — went smeared and wrong at the edges — and was suddenly behind Caleth with a fist already coming down.

Caleth had expected something. He had not expected that.

He was gone anyway. He took the step — that small cool half-second of nowhere — and the fist closed on the space where a half-elf had been standing, and the air went out of the whole village at once.

Caleth reassembled himself six feet to the left, set the glaive's butt on the ground, and leaned on it.

"Are you finished?" he said. "That's more than three."

He was pleased with himself. That was the honest account of it, and he gave it to himself later without mercy: he was tired, and he had been frightened in a stone room forty minutes earlier, and he had just made a demon-blooded chieftain look like a drunk swinging at a wasp in front of his entire people, and he was *pleased*.

Marhevok stood in the middle of the ring with his fists at his sides.

Then he threw his head back and screamed something.

It was not Hallit. It was not Common. It was not Abyssal, and Caleth knew Abyssal — had known it since a bookshop in Kenabres, had understood a demon perfectly well while it explained what it intended to eat — and this was not that, and not anything near it. It came up out of the man's chest in a rush of syllables with no shape Caleth's ear could get hold of.

He looked at the crowd.

They were looking at each other.

*They don't know it either.* Dozens of Kellids, in their own steading, hearing their own clanliege, and every one of them was looking at their neighbor with the same blank incomprehension Caleth could feel on his own face.

At the edge of the ring, the drake — which had come out of the hall to watch, and had settled in the dirt like a dog — lifted its head sharply and stared at Marhevok.

*It knows what that was.*

And then there was no more time to think about it, because Marhevok was charging, and he was not stopping, and the deal was over.

Korroc's voice came from the circle's edge, flat and carrying.

"He's broken it. We may have to fight all of them."

Caleth heard the shield come up and the hammer come off the belt.

And then the fist landed.

He got a hand up. It did nothing. The blow came in over his guard and hit him across the chest and shoulder and lifted him clean off the ground, and the world went white and then red, and he came down on his back six feet away with his ribs screaming and Radiance still, somehow, in his hand.

*One,* some idiot part of him reported. *He's had one.*

Then the cold came.

---

The drake breathed.

It caught both dwarves in a blossoming explosion of glittering white, and the ground under them went to ice in an instant, a slick sheet spreading out across the dirt of the ring, and Caleth heard Korroc make a sound he had heard perhaps three times.

Cold went into Korroc the way it always did — the way it had at the Ahari with a chimera's breath, the way it went into no one else — past the gold, past the mithral, past the stone in his blood, straight into the imbalance at the middle of him that Caleth had tried to ward twice and had twice watched the ward slide off with nothing to hold on to.

Caleth got to one knee.

Korroc was already healing himself. Golden warmth ran down him and steamed in the frost, and he set his boots on the ice and did not fall, and then he turned and put one gauntlet out toward the drake and let go two rays of fire that crossed the ring and struck it in the flank and the neck.

It shrieked. Ice-drakes do not enjoy that.

Thane cast something short and was not there anymore.

And Caleth, on one knee, with his ribs grinding and dozens of people around him deciding what they were going to do, did the thing he had been half-thinking about since a hall full of ghouls a year ago and had never had a reason to try.

He had been inverting spells his whole adult life. Fire into cold. Fire into acid. He took the recipe apart on the way to the table and put it back wrong, and it worked, and Aravashnial hated it, and it had killed a great many things.

But he had only ever done it to spells that were *his.*

He shut his eyes for one second in the middle of a fight, which was the most dangerous thing he did all day, and he built the working outward instead of inward — not a spell but a *rule*, a condition, hung in the air around himself like a held breath: *anything that comes in here is not what it was.*

The air around him shimmered.

He opened his eyes.

Marhevok was coming.

Caleth got his feet under him and thrust — and Radiance went in, holy light running the length of it, into the great red chest under the ribs; and came back, and went in again, and the second one made the man stagger.

Then Caleth stepped, and the world folded, and he came down beside Korroc on the ice with the ring's whole width between himself and the thing he had just stabbed twice.

It made no difference. Marhevok turned and charged the two of them together, headlong, all fury and no craft, and ran directly into a warhammer and a glaive at the same instant. The hammer took him across the hip. Radiance opened his side.

And still he got a hand free, and the fist came down on Caleth again.

Caleth reached down past the tired and past the ribs and spent a little more of what he had spent in the ring, and simply was not where the fist was. It hit the ice and cracked it in a star a yard across.

Behind them, the drake breathed again.

Caleth felt it come. He felt it hit the shimmer.

And the explosion of ice turned into a ball of fire.

It came apart on the way in and put itself back together wrong — the working caught it and rewrote it midair, and what arrived was flame. Korroc, who had gone rigid in expectation of the other thing, got his shield up out of pure reflex and caught most of it on the face of it, and what got past the shield went into a man with fire in his blood and did very nearly nothing at all.

Korroc lowered the shield. His beard was smoking. He looked at Caleth.

Then he turned, because Marhevok Grunhuld-Wintersun was still standing beside him.

The hammer came up over the dwarf's head and came down once, and Caleth heard bone go — a wet, deep, structural crack, the sound of a load-bearing thing failing.

Marhevok took one step.

The hammer came down again.

He fell on the ice, and the fall broke it further, and he did not move again.

---

The sound that went around the ring was not a cheer.

Caleth stood in the middle of it with Radiance dripping and his ribs on fire and a few dozen people around him, and he understood, in the two seconds before anything happened, that the entire afternoon now balanced on what these people did next.

What they did was drop everything.

Axes went into the dirt. Shields went down. A man near the front simply opened his hands and let a spear fall and stood there looking at it.

At the ring's edge, the drake got up. It looked at the body for a moment with its slit eyes. Then it spread its wings, and took two steps, and went up — and away west, low over the tents and out over the bruise-colored hills, and did not come back.

Caleth watched it go the whole way.

Beside him, Thane wiped a dagger and said, to nobody in particular:

"That's off to report to somebody."

Caleth did not argue with him, because he had been thinking the same thing since the wings came out and had been hoping to be talked out of it. A cold-blooded thing lying in a heated hall in an iced pool did not live in that room by accident; somebody had put it there, and it had come out to watch a duel, and it had looked up sharply at a language nobody knew, and now it had gone west without hurrying.

*Not fleeing. Leaving.*

He put it in the ledger with the shape in the sky, and the ledger was becoming genuinely crowded.

Then a man came out of the crowd.

It was the bearded one. Beverick — Beverach, Caleth would learn shortly, and would find that the difference mattered to him, because Marhevok had gotten his own man's name wrong for years and nobody had ever corrected him.

He walked to the middle of the circle. He did not look at Caleth.

He stopped in front of Korroc, and looked up at him, and said:

"You have defeated Marhevok. According to the laws of our people, you are now our leader."

Caleth stood there with a lit holy blade in his hands and blood on his tabard and a working still shimmering in the air around him, and watched the Kellids turn, all together, to look at the dwarf with the hammer.

He had done all of it. He had learned their language out of a book at nineteen. He had walked in first and spoken first and taken the terms and made the deal and won the duel three times over in front of the whole village, and he had done it *well*, and the arithmetic had been correct in every particular except the one that mattered.

And it was the hammer they went to.

*Be the kind one, Spireborn.*

A silver dragon on a cliff above a sea, seventeen chapters ago, in a dream he had never told anybody about.

*Even when the others choose the hammer.*

He had carried that around for almost a year and he had understood it as an instruction about the company. About Korroc's theology and Thane's grudge — *they will reach for the blunt answer and you will be the counterweight.* He had been rather proud of himself for it.

Today he had come into a village of frightened people, and read them correctly in ninety seconds, and then spent the whole of the next hour on the one man in it who was past reaching. He had threatened. He had bargained, and won the bargain three times over, and enjoyed the winning. And the thing in the chair had broken its word anyway, because that was what it was — and in all of it Caleth had not once looked at the people standing in the ring.

And Korroc had swung a hammer once at the very end because there was nothing else left, and now people were looking at him as if he were weather.

*She wasn't warning me about them.*

Caleth Aranor stood very still on cracked ice in the middle of the Worldwound, and did not say one word about it, and never would.

"First," said Korroc, stepping forward, "the raids stop."

Beverach's shoulders came down an inch.

"The raids were never what we wanted," he said. "We only did what our leader ordered."

"Good." Korroc looked around the ring — at the dropped axes, at the thin children, at the tents pitched in the footprint of a dead town. "Why are you people living here?"

"Generations ago, before the Worldwound, this was our tribe's home." Beverach's voice was steady enough. "We left. He made us come back."

Korroc nodded slowly.

"How many are you?"

"About a dozen warriors. A few dozen more who are not."

"Then if I am your leader," said Korroc, "this tribe is allied with Queen Galfrey and her army. How quickly can you get these people packed and moved to Vilareth Ford?"

Thane materialized at Korroc's elbow, close, his voice low enough that only the three of them got it.

"Should we not let them go back to wherever they were living before?"

It was a fair question. Caleth had already run it — Mendev, the refugee camps, four days' hard road, a village of demon-tainted Kellids arriving unannounced at a crusader border post — and had gotten a bad answer.

Korroc shook his head.

"No. We can make sure they're looked after." He tipped his chin at the crowd. "Look at them."

Nobody argued. Not Thane, and not — Caleth noticed, and would think about for two days — the barbarians. There was no protest at all. What went through the crowd was closer to eagerness, the ugly kind: the eagerness of people who know they are in a bad place and have not been permitted to say so out loud in years.

They scattered to their tents almost before Beverach had finished turning around.

---

The trouble came over the body.

Caleth had not expected it, which he later added to the day's account of himself.

They were building a pyre. He came around the corner of the hall and found six men laying Marhevok Grunhuld-Wintersun on stacked wood with what looked, unmistakably, like ceremony.

Something in Caleth's chest went very cold and very fast.

"*No.*"

The word came out with more force behind it than he had put in.

"He is evil," Caleth said, "and he does not deserve rites."

He meant it entirely. He could feel exactly where he was standing when he said it, and the ground was solid. This was a man who had marched his own people into the Abyss's back garden to live on a midden of human parts; who had ordered a stranger's throat cut in his own hall for the crime of being small; who had worn a demon's shape and enjoyed it. *There are things you do not sing over. The singing is a lie and the lie stays in the ground with them.*

Beverach came forward.

He was terrified. Caleth's eye gave him that in an instant and gave him no way to un-see it: the man's hands, the color of his face, the small backward flinch of a body that had learned what happens when you contradict an armed man in this village.

He did it anyway.

"He held a curse," Beverach said. "His family's curse. He had no children of his own." He swallowed. "If we burn him — if the body burns — we hoped it might go out of the line with him."

And then, because he was frightened and because frightened people over-explain, he told Caleth the rest of it, standing in the cleared ground with the wood already stacked.

He told it badly, out of order, the way a man tells a thing he has known his whole life and never once had to say to an outsider. An ancestor named Corag who had stayed when everyone else fled — who had been brave, who had been *commended* for it, whose family had been proud of him. The First Crusade coming and pushing the demons back and everyone saying he had been right. And then the Second Crusade failing, and the Grunhuld-Wintersuns finally running east into Mendev after all — and bringing something with them that they had not packed.

Children born wrong. Not all. Not most. But enough, and for long enough, that a family learned to hold its breath at every birth. A tail. A foot turned the wrong way. And sometimes worse than that, and Beverach did not describe the worse, and said only that the mothers were always quick.

And Marhevok, who had seemed to escape it. Who had been a good son and a barbarian like his parents and who had saved his own family from a pack of demons and been half-killed doing it — and who had found, after, that when his temper went, so did his shape.

"He was not always like this," Beverach said. "That is not — " He stopped. He started again. "His brothers tried to take him out of the seat. Three of them."

"What happened?"

"He killed them." Beverach looked at the woodpile. "We have all been very careful since."

Caleth stood there.

The gouged faces on the mural came back to him whole, and he understood what he had failed to do with them four hours ago, and it cost him something to hold his expression where it was.

But that was not the thing that turned him. He was honest enough with himself, that evening and for a long time afterward, to know that it was not the argument, because the argument had not actually changed a single fact. Marhevok was still what he had been. The heap on the east side of the hall was still there.

What turned him was the sight of a terrified man arguing with an armed stranger, in front of witnesses, on behalf of somebody he had hated for years — and doing it not for the dead man at all, but for a child who had not been born yet.

*He is not asking me to forgive him. He is asking me for a funeral because he thinks a funeral is medicine.*

And it probably was not. Caleth was almost certain it was not; curses did not work like that, and he could have said so, and been right.

*Knowing wasn't enough.*

"You're right," Caleth said. "I'm sorry. It's sensible to try to end the curse on your tribe."

He heard Beverach breathe out.

"Burn him," Caleth said. "But he gets no rites. No words. No song. You may light the fire and you may stand and watch it. That is all he gets from anybody, and I will stand and watch it with you."

Beverach nodded, and it seemed to be enough.

---

Caleth examined the body before they lit it, because he was still Caleth.

There was a symbol on a cord around the neck. He lifted it on the tip of a dagger and turned it in the light.

Baphomet. Unmistakably — the horned head, the labyrinth worked around it, a particular unpleasant asymmetry its maker put into it. Cast, not carved. Good work.

He turned it over.

*Jerribeth*, in a fine engraved hand.

"Beverach. Who is Jerribeth?"

The barbarian came and looked and his mouth tightened.

"An elf woman. Beautiful. She came here, twice, maybe three times. She gave him that." He hesitated. "She is the mistress he spoke of. He was waiting for her to send for him. He said she was going to make him a leader of her armies."

*A Templar, then. Or something wearing Templar colors.* Caleth turned the symbol over again. An elven woman, personally recruiting a demon-blooded barbarian chieftain in the middle of the Wounded Lands, and then leaving him out here raiding a supply road for a year without sending for him.

He set his sight on the little thing properly and felt out what was in it, and found it in about four seconds, and it was not a blessing or a protection or a weapon.

It was an eye.

Not for the wearer. For the giver. A pretty gift on a cord that made its owner easy to find and easier to watch, worn day and night by a man who had believed it made him important.

Caleth laughed. It came out of him before he could stop it, one short unlovely note.

"Something?" said Thane, behind him.

"He was on a leash and he thought it was a medal."

He walked over to the pool inside the hall — the drake's pool, still rimed with ice at the edges, still cold in a windowless room with no reason to be — and he held the symbol out over the black water for a moment, considering.

*I could keep it. It's a thread. Threads are useful. Aravashnial could —*

No. It ran the wrong way. Whatever it showed, it showed to *her*, and there was no version of carrying it that did not mean carrying an open window on his own back through the middle of a war.

He dropped it in.

It went down into the black with a small clean sound and did not come back up.

"Let her look at the bottom of a pool," Caleth said, "and wonder."

---

They found the two women in a side room at the back of the hall.

Chained to the wall by the wrists. Crusade-issue clothing, filthy, months gone. They pressed themselves back against the stone when the door opened and the light came in, and one of them put her face against her own shoulder and stayed like that.

Caleth crouched down, six feet away, and made himself smaller, and put Radiance flat on the floor behind him where they could see him put it.

"You don't have to be afraid of him any longer," he said. "He's dead. We're going to free you."

Thane went past him without a word and was already at the wall. The locks came open under his picks in the time it takes to say so — two of them, one after the other, with a small competent click each. He caught the chain before it could fall and clatter, and lowered it to the floor by hand, and stepped back out of their space immediately.

"Go out and talk to the people outside," Caleth said. "They'll tell you what's happening. It's a great deal, and most of it is good."

"You're safe now," said Korroc, from the doorway, filling it, in gold plate, with a warhammer.

Caleth watched the two women look at the dwarf and decide to believe him, and could not for the life of him have told anyone how they had arrived at it.

---

They spent the afternoon packing a village, and the night in it, and left in the morning as one column.

It took two days. Nothing came near them. A village moving together with a dozen armed men and three Knights of the Fifth Crusade was, Caleth reflected, the safest he had been in the Worldwound in a year, and there was probably a lesson in it that the crusade had been failing to learn for a century.

On the second afternoon they came up onto the shoulder of Eagle Rock, and Vilareth Ford opened up below them, and Caleth saw the village see them.

It was instant. The whole place sprang. Men came out of buildings at a run; a horn went; a line began forming across the road with a speed that would have pleased any sergeant alive.

Which was, of course, correct. A group of Kellids cresting a ridge above your supply depot is precisely one thing.

Caleth put his heels into the shaggy horse and rode out ahead of the column alone.

He drew Radiance and held it straight up over his head, and off the crossbar of the leaf-blade a pennant stood out flat in the wind off the escarpment as he came down the slope.

He was aware — he was extremely aware — of what he was doing with it. Yaniel's blade. A relic of the Fourth Crusade, recovered out of a cultist's hands, lit at the gates of Drezen. He had turned it into a signal flag, because a column that reads as an attack at six hundred yards gets a volley at three hundred, and a bright glaive with a pennant flying reads as *me* at any distance.

He put that away with the other things and rode down.

Kamilo Dann came out to meet him, and Caleth swung down off the horse, and she looked past him up the slope at dozens of people coming down it.

"What in the — " she said. "What is going on here?"

"Korroc is now the leader of the barbarians," Caleth said. "We've brought them here to live and to help."

Kamilo looked at him. She looked up the hill. She looked back at him.

"How many do you *have?*"

"A few dozen," said Caleth. "Not too many."

"Not too many." Her eyebrows went up and stayed up. "That will double the size of this camp."

Korroc arrived at Caleth's shoulder. "They'll help."

"Not all of them look like fighters."

"Not all of them are," Caleth said. "They need work. Give them work."

Kamilo Dann stood in the road for a moment with her ledger board under her arm and every problem in the world arriving over the hill at walking pace.

Then she sighed.

"All right," she said. "We can do this."

---

That night Korroc asked if they could stay, and Caleth said yes without asking why, and then spent four hours finding out.

The dwarf went to the crusaders first. That was the part Caleth had not predicted and should have. Korroc went around the Ford's own people — the soldiers, the teamsters, the woman who ran the smithy, the boy who ran messages — and he did not give a speech. He sat down with them in twos and threes and told them the story. Where the barbarians had come from. What had been done to them. What the raids had been and who had ordered them and what had happened to the man who had. He told it plainly and he did not soften the parts that were not soft, and Caleth, drifting past at intervals with something in his hands as though he were on his way elsewhere, heard him say *they were made to do it, and now they're not, and that's the whole of it* three separate times to three separate groups in almost exactly the same words.

Then he went to the barbarians.

He helped them pitch. In seventy pounds of plate, on his knees in the mud, holding lines and driving pegs for two hours. He told them what the Ford was and how it ran and where the water was. He told them that when the war was finished they would be free to go anywhere they wanted, and that until then they should stay here and be useful. He told them, bluntly, that there would be distrust, and that some of it would be unfair, and that they should expect it and eat it — *lie low if you need to, especially you fighters* — and that if anyone asked them to do a piece of work, they should do it.

And then he found Beverach.

Caleth was near enough to hear it, which he had arranged.

"You're in charge of the tribe while I'm gone," Korroc said.

Beverach's eyes went wide. "What? I'm not sure I — "

Korroc held up one gauntlet.

"You'll be my right hand. Lead them while I'm away."

Beverach was quiet for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, and Korroc put the hand on his arm and said something too low to carry, and the big bearded man nodded again, differently.

Caleth stood in the dark at the edge of the firelight with his arms folded and watched the whole thing.

He had, on the two-day walk, done the work. He had done it thoroughly, because it was what he was for. He had run the numbers on dozens of additional mouths at a depot victualed for half the number; on winter; on the eleven distinct ways a demon-tainted Kellid clan and a crusader supply post could produce a killing in the first month; on what a single knife fight over a stolen loaf would do to the road to Drezen. He had a list. The list had failure points on it, and he had ranked them, and he had been intending to hand it to Kamilo in the morning with recommendations.

Korroc had solved perhaps two-thirds of it by walking around a camp for four hours saying true things to frightened people.

*I could not have done that,* Caleth thought.

Not could-not in the sense of lacking the skill. He could have made the rounds. He could have chosen better words than Korroc's, probably; he was better with words than Korroc had ever been. And it would not have worked, and he knew exactly why it would not have worked, and the reason was that every person in this camp could tell, within about nine seconds, that Caleth Aranor was managing them.

Korroc was not managing anybody. Korroc had simply gone out and been the same man to strangers that he was to three friends, over and over, until it was late.

The fire popped. Somebody's child laughed — an actual laugh, out loud, the first Caleth had heard from any of them.

*Be the kind one, Spireborn. Even when the others choose the hammer.*

He stood there in the dark a while longer, and thought about a dragon who had looked at him across a crowd at Armasse and smiled at him as though she already knew something, and he wondered, not for the first time and with considerably more discomfort than before, exactly how much she had known.

Then he went and found a wall to sleep against, because they were leaving in the morning.

---

Kamilo caught them in the road at first light with a chest in her hands.

"I need a favor."

It was about eighteen inches by ten, banded, with a good lock and a wax seal over the hasp, and it was heavier than it looked. Caleth registered all of that in the process of taking it, along with the fact that the seal was undamaged and that the wax was not one he recognized.

"This was brought to me by a courier," Kamilo said. "I was ordered to get it to Drezen."

"Who do I give it to?"

"A Master Aravashnial."

Caleth's face did nothing at all.

"I'll make sure he gets it," he said.

He set it in the crook of his arm, and stood there for a moment.

He weighed it in both hands. He turned it once. He noted the balance and what the balance implied and how it shifted, and he noted precisely how much he wanted to look inside it, which was a great deal.

Then he gathered up two dwarves and a chest and folded the world.

---

Aravashnial took the chest off him in his own quarters and went entirely still.

"How did you get this?"

"It was given to us at the Ford. Kamilo Dann said a courier brought it to her."

The elf nodded slowly. He carried the chest to the table and set it down and put both hands flat on the lid for a moment, and did not open it, and did not say anything else about it at all.

Caleth waited three full seconds, which was one longer than was polite.

"Thank you," said Aravashnial. Then, without looking up: "Oh — Irabeth will want to speak with you as soon as possible."

Which was, Caleth reflected as they walked out, one of the most efficient dismissals he had ever received.

---

Irabeth Tirabade had four men around a table covered in lists, and she waved every one of them out of the room the moment she saw who had come in.

She heard the whole report standing up, with her knuckles on the table, and her eyebrows went up twice.

"So," she said at the end. "You've left the barbarians at the Ford. And you don't think that's going to be a problem."

"No," said Korroc. "It won't."

Irabeth looked at him for a while.

"All right," she said, and sighed, and looked at the ceiling. "Well. It won't be a problem unless it becomes a problem."

Caleth filed that as approximately the wisest thing anyone had said all week.

Then she came around the table and looked at the three of them, and her voice changed.

"I thank you for what you did. All of it. You brought Jesker Helton back, and you have no idea what that has done for this garrison."

---

They went to see him that afternoon, and he was packing.

He was on his feet, which Caleth had not expected for a week. He was thin and moved like a man who has been ill, and he had shaved, and he had a satchel open on the bed.

And he looked at them when they came in.

That was the difference, and Caleth had it before anyone spoke a word. In the cavern the man had not been able to hold anyone's eyes for more than a second and a half; Caleth had counted, because he counted. Jesker Helton stood up off the bed now and looked all three of them full in the face, one after another, and it cost him something, and he did it anyway.

Nageru was standing against the wall by the window with his hands behind his back, and had evidently been there some time.

"It's done, then," said Korroc.

"Two days ago." Jesker's voice was steady. "Father Vaenic performed it. It took most of a morning." He glanced at the wall by the window. "Your friend was there for all of it."

"He would be," said Korroc.

Caleth looked at Nageru, and Nageru looked back at him with an expression of such complete neutrality that it constituted a full report.

Korroc crossed the room and put out his hand, and Jesker took it, and Korroc closed his other hand over the top and stood there a moment.

"How is it?" the dwarf said.

Jesker thought about the question. Caleth watched him actually think about it, and revised his estimate of the man upward for the third time in five days.

"Clean," he said at last. "That's the honest word for it. Whatever was on me is off me, and I can tell, and I did not expect to be able to tell." He turned back to the satchel. "And it doesn't finish anything. That's the part I've been sitting with. A god forgives you in a morning. It took me a month to wreck that shrine and it will take longer than a morning to be a man those people can look at."

He folded a shirt with more attention than the shirt required.

"So I'm going home. My own country, the temple I came out of. Not to hide." He said that part firmly, as though he had been arguing it with himself and had won. "There's work there and people who knew me before, and I intend to be useful in front of them for a long time, and then I intend to come back and be useful here. I've left someone in charge of finishing the cleaning of the shrine. It'll be done properly."

"Aye," said Korroc. "That's the right shape of it."

"It's the only shape of it. Absolution's a beginning." Jesker pulled the satchel's strap through its buckle. "A man can be forgiven and still owe."

Caleth stood very still.

He was aware of Thane, on his left, going a particular kind of motionless — the kind Caleth had learned to notice and had never learned to read — and he did not turn his head, because turning his head would have been a question.

Jesker gave them gifts before they left, and would not be talked out of it. A bow — a good one, a beautiful one, with a working in it that Caleth's eye read off it from six feet away — and a quiver of arrows, the symbols of his god.

Erastil's symbol is a bow. Caleth held the thing across his palms and thought about that: a man giving away the shape of his own god on his way out of a city his hands had wrecked while something else was driving them, and giving it away not as a payment but as a thing he would rather have working than hanging on a wall.

And he thought about a slab of smoky crystal on a ledge two hundred feet down a chasm in the Weeping Hills, with a dead priestess in it, and a bow laid along her side that four men had looked at and decided did not belong to them.

*Two bows in one week. One we wouldn't take, and one we couldn't refuse.*

---

That evening Aravashnial sent for them.

He had cleared a working table, which was itself alarming. On the cleared space, on a square of dark cloth, sat a single potion.

They all recognized it. It had come out of Staunton Vhane's quarters the better part of a month ago, among a great deal of other loot, and it had sat in a bag ever since because it looked like eight hundred other things that looked like it: a squat glass vial, a cure of some kind, unremarkable.

"Do you know what this is?" Aravashnial said.

"No," said Caleth.

"Study it."

Caleth picked it up.

He turned it to the lamp, and let his sight down into it, and the first thing it gave him was *cure*, plainly and immediately, the way a well-lit sign gives you a word.

He held that for a moment, and did not accept it.

*Upstairs I looked at a banner and saw a bedsheet, and it was a bedsheet. That was true. This is a different kind of true.*

"It looks like a cure potion at first," he said slowly. "But it isn't."

Aravashnial's mouth moved very slightly.

"No," he said. "It is not."

He took it back and held it up, and the lamplight went through it and threw a small trembling coin of light onto the ceiling.

"It is made with a philosopher's stone. There is quicksilver at the center of such a stone; the quicksilver is what the stone is *for.* This is what comes out of it." He turned the vial. "Poured onto a dead body, this acts as a true resurrection."

Nobody said anything.

Caleth was aware of Thane, on his left, going entirely motionless in a particular way.

"It is one of the more difficult things in the world to make," Aravashnial went on, "and once it is opened it will lose its potency within a day. Which means it cannot be prepared in advance, or carried opened, or used carefully. It will be used in a hurry by whoever happens to be holding it, or it will not be used at all."

He set it down on the dark cloth.

"I suggest you keep it," he said. "Korroc does an admirable job of keeping everyone in this company on their feet. But what happens if he falls?"

Korroc snorted.

Aravashnial smiled — a small, dry, entirely unrepentant thing — and let it pass, and began putting away his lamp, and that was the end of the conversation.

---

Caleth carried it out.

Nobody had discussed who would. They had simply all looked at him.

He stood in the corridor outside for a while with the vial in his hand and the cold of the glass coming up into his fingers, and did what he always did, which was to run it forward.

A single undo. One. Not a healing and not a rescue — an *undo*, the only one any of them were ever likely to hold.

He ran the futures the way he had run futures since he was fifteen years old under a strange old teacher in a strange old room in Kenabres. And every road he sent out came back with the same shape at the end of it: four men, and a bad hour, somewhere ahead, and a decision made by whoever could still stand up.

He could not tell which of them it would be.

That was the part he could not get out in front of, and he tried, standing in a cold corridor in a fortress his friends' fathers had built, for rather a long time.

And under it, unbidden and unwelcome and entirely useless, the other thought — the one that had arrived the moment Aravashnial said *poured onto a dead body*, and had been waiting politely ever since for him to have a free moment.

*There are two people this could never have been spent on.*

*I don't even know where they are.*

He put the vial in the innermost pocket of his coat, against the folded blueprints, over his heart.

The cold of it went on being noticeable for a long time.
