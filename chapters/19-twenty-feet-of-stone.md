# Twenty Feet of Stone

*Chapter Nineteen — The Tunnel and the Tomb*

---

There were four kinds of paper on that table and Thane had sorted them before Aravashnial said a word.

It was not a decision. It was the way his eyes worked and had worked since he was nine years old and his mother had put him in front of a strongbox and told him to tell her three true things about it before he touched the lock. So while an elf stood over a table with both palms flat on it and did not speak, Thane Stonevein read the table.

Ledgers, bound, iron at the corners: kept records, official, made to survive. Loose sheets in a hand he recognized as Aravashnial's own: notes, recent, made this week. A drift of thin copied pages with the ink still black rather than brown: transcriptions, made from something the elf could not carry up here. And the maps, which were the oldest things in the room and were pinned down under an ink bottle and two books and a stone off the floor and somebody's abandoned cup, because when Aravashnial needed a weight he took whatever was nearest and had never once in his life gone to fetch a proper one.

Four kinds. Four separate sources. Nothing on that table had been assembled to make a case; it had been *gathered*, from four directions, by somebody who did not trust any one of them.

That was the part that frightened him. A man who is guessing brings one source. A man who is certain brings four.

"I have found something concerning your fathers."

The door had shut behind Irabeth about six seconds ago. The latch was still settling in Thane's ears.

Beside him Korroc had stopped moving entirely, in the specific way his cousin stopped, which was not stillness but the arrest of a very large thing that had been going somewhere.

"Say it," Thane said.

"I intend to." Aravashnial did not look up from the table. "I intend to say all of it, in an order, and I would ask you not to get ahead of me, because you will get ahead of me and you will guess wrong, and I have spent four days making certain that I am not guessing."

"Four days."

"Six, if we are counting the ones where I did not know what I was looking at."

He reached into a stack and pulled out a thin weathered book and set it down.

"This came out of the Blackwing Library. A crusader accounting from Kenabres — companies formed, orders received, patrols sent into the Worldwound, casualties, men missing. Most of it is precisely what it appears to be, which is the most tedious document in the world."

He opened it to a page already marked with a scrap of paper, and Thane saw that the scrap was one of the transcriptions, folded twice, and that it had been folded twice a long time before today.

"Several decades ago," Aravashnial said, "a company of forty dwarven crusaders left Kenabres. Their stated purpose was to investigate reports of ruins believed to be connected to one of the old dwarven sky cities."

Thane's hands did not move. He was aware of them not moving.

"Eight returned."

Nobody said anything.

"The account they gave was that the company encountered a demon encampment and were overwhelmed. Thirty-two presumed dead. Eight survivors home."

He turned the book around on the table so that it faced them, and put one finger on it.

"One of the eight was Staunton Vhane."

*There it is,* Thane thought, with a calm so complete that he understood at once it was the shock and not the calm.

"Among the thirty-two listed as dead were two names."

Aravashnial's finger came down the page.

"Thorek Stonevein."

Down one line.

"And Borin Stonevein."

Thane looked at the page. He did not look at his cousin, and his cousin did not look at him, and the two of them stood there four feet apart with the same forty-year-old grief lying on a table in a stranger's handwriting.

His father's name was written in a clerk's hand. That was the thing that got in. Not the fact of it — he had grown up in the fact of it — but the *hand*. Some man in an office in Kenabres, four decades gone, entering a name in a column at the end of a working day, and then the next name, and then going home to his supper.

"Which is what the crusade believes to this hour," Aravashnial said. "It is also, in the parts that matter, false."

He reached for another book.

This one was different. Black leather, iron corners, the pages yellowed all the way through to the gutter.

"This is a prison ledger from Drezen."

He opened it.

"And this is where the official account stops being true."

Thane heard Caleth shift his weight, once, behind him. Nageru made no sound at all.

Aravashnial set the two books side by side.

"The company did encounter demons." He looked up. "But it was not an accident."

He pulled a page from beneath a ledger and laid it beside the books. It was a transcription. Thane's eyes went down it and caught the shape of the original underneath the elf's clean lettering: a hand that had gone at the parchment sideways.

"They knew the company was coming. They knew its route. They knew, within a few men, how many were in it."

Another page.

"The expedition was a trap."

His expression did not change much. It did not need to.

"Staunton Vhane arranged it."

*I asked for him alive.*

The thought arrived whole and would not be got rid of. Three weeks ago in a map room with a dead traitor cooling on the flagstones, Thane had said *I might still want to ask Staunton questions*, and had been overruled by his own blood, and had swallowed it and gone on down the stair. He had told himself since that it was a small thing. That the man had had nothing left worth having.

The man had had this.

He would have had all of it, and Thane could have got it out of him — not with hooks and not with fire, but with time and a chair and the patience of somebody who had spent a season listening to Kenabres gutters for a living. Three days. He'd have needed three days.

And a hammer had happened instead.

He put it away. He put it, deliberately, in the place where he put things he would take out later when there was nobody about, and he found that his voice worked.

"Go on."

Aravashnial turned another page.

"The eight who returned to Kenabres believed they had escaped." His eyes dropped. "According to these accounts, they were permitted to."

Korroc made a low sound.

"The purpose was simple and it was well done. Survivors return with a believable account. The dead are mourned. The company is written off as another loss to the Worldwound, and no one goes looking, because there is nothing to look for." The elf's mouth thinned. "It is efficient. I dislike admiring it."

He looked directly at the two of them.

"But most of those thirty-two men were not killed."

He tapped the black ledger.

"They were captured."

Another page.

"They were brought here."

He let it sit exactly long enough.

"To Drezen."

Thane became aware that he had put one hand flat on the edge of the table. He had no memory of doing it. He took it off.

Aravashnial pushed the open prison ledger across the wood toward the two dwarves.

"I found both of your fathers' names in it."

His finger stopped beside the first entry.

"Thorek Stonevein."

Then the second.

"Borin Stonevein."

He looked up.

"Your fathers survived the ambush."

---

Thane read the page.

He read it the way he read a floor. Not for the names — the names were a hand around his throat and he set them aside for later, the way you set aside a wound until the fighting is over — but for the *ledger*. Column widths. Ruling lines. The hand, and whether it was one hand or several. Whether the entries ran continuous or had been added to.

"Two hands," he said.

Aravashnial's head came up sharply.

"Yes."

"Changes there." Thane put a fingertip an inch above the page without touching it, three-quarters of the way down. "Different ink and a different way of making a *g*. Second clerk took over and kept the same ruling because the book was already ruled."

"Yes." Something almost like pleasure went across the elf's face and was suppressed. "It took me most of an afternoon to be sure of that, and I was not sure until I found a page where they overlap."

"So it's a working book. Kept over years by whoever was on the duty." Thane heard his own voice going flat and level and did not fight it, because flat and level was the only road through this room. "Not a thing composed after the fact to look right."

"No."

"You checked that on purpose."

"I checked everything on purpose." Aravashnial straightened. "I have been aware since the first hour that I was going to have to stand in front of the two of you and say a great many things, and that if I said one of them carelessly I would be handing you a hope with a hole in it. I would rather have taken another week."

Thane looked at him.

Four days of an elf's time, in a fortress that wanted every hour anyone had, spent making certain that two dwarves would not be handed a maybe.

"Right," he said. "Keep going."

---

"They were brought here with the others. They were interrogated — Kenabres, the crusade, supply routes, defenses. The usual catechism." Aravashnial turned several pages. "Then they were put to work."

Something touched his expression that in a less careful man would have been amusement.

"That appears to have been the first mistake."

He looked at the two of them.

"Your fathers helped build Drezen."

Korroc's breath went out of him.

Thane did not move at all.

"They knew this stone," he said.

"Better than the people holding them." Aravashnial tapped the ledger. "Whoever was responsible for the prisoners either did not know that, or did not consider it worth knowing. They had taken two dwarves who understood the Citadel, imprisoned them inside it, and set them to labor on it."

He shook his head slightly.

"The records become considerably more interesting after that."

He began laying out loose sheets, one at a time, like a man dealing a hand.

"Missing food."

Another.

"Stolen tools."

Another.

"Altercations with guards."

Another.

"Unauthorized movement between work details."

Another.

"Damaged equipment."

Another.

"Fighting."

He looked down at the growing spread.

"Quite a lot of fighting, actually."

His finger touched one of the entries. "Thorek appears repeatedly."

Another. "So does Borin."

He glanced up.

"Neither of them seems to have been particularly good at being a prisoner."

And Thane Stonevein, standing in a council room in a citadel three hundred miles from anywhere he had ever been happy, laughed. "And they wonder where I got it from."

Nobody said anything about it.

"Aye," he said, to the table. "That'd be them."

---

Aravashnial reached across and pulled an old map free from beneath two books.

"At first I took these for what they appear to be. Acts of resistance. Men with nothing left making trouble because trouble was the only thing they still owned." He unfolded the map with the particular care of a man handling something that has decided to become fragile. "Then I found this."

Thane came around the table.

He could not have said afterward whether he had asked leave. He stood at the elf's elbow and looked down at a map so old the edges had gone soft and brown as autumn leaf, and his eyes did the thing they did.

Contour first. Then the built work. Then the lines that belonged to neither.

"This predates the fall of Drezen."

"It does. It's a survey." Thane's finger hovered. "That's a water plan."

"It is."

Aravashnial traced a faded line running from the Citadel out toward what had once been the river.

"The original fortress drew from the Ahari through a tunnel beneath the eastern side. When the river receded, the tunnel ceased to be useful, and it was abandoned. Sealed at the citadel end and forgotten at the other."

His finger followed the line inward.

"When the demons expanded the lower levels, they built over and around a great deal of the old construction. They did not survey. They cut where it suited them."

He tapped a point on the map, and then a second one, very close to the first.

"The prison came within approximately twenty feet of the old water tunnel."

The room went quiet in a way that Thane felt in his back teeth.

"The demons did not know it was there," Aravashnial said.

A pause.

"Your fathers did."

He reached for the disciplinary records again, and his voice was almost gentle.

"The stolen tools suddenly made considerably more sense."

He laid several of the records beside the map. Then he put one finger on the prison, and drew it slowly across, and stopped it on the old watercourse.

"They weren't merely stealing from their captors."

He traced the distance between them.

"They were digging."

Thane looked at the gap between the two lines on the map. It was, on that scale, about the width of his thumbnail.

"Twenty feet."

"Twenty feet of stone and earth," said Aravashnial, "between a prison full of dwarves and open country."

And for the first time since they had come into that room, the elf almost smiled.

"Someone in Drezen apparently believed that constituted a secure prison."

---

Thane had a great many questions, and he asked them in the order that would hurt least, which was not the order he wanted them in.

"How long?"

"I cannot determine it exactly." The faint expression had gone. Aravashnial reached for another ledger. "The records were written by people who did not know the tunnel existed, so none of them understood what a missing tool or an extra hour on a work detail actually meant. They recorded symptoms and diagnosed nothing."

"Then give me the range."

The elf considered him for a moment.

"The first entry I can plausibly attach to it and the last are separated by rather more than a year."

Thane took that and turned it over and found that he could not hold it for long.

A year. Better than a year of it. Twenty feet, in the dark, in shifts, with tools stolen one at a time and hidden and stolen again, by men who were beaten for it when they were caught and went back to it when they could stand. Not a night's daring. Not a charge at a wall. A year of it — the way you build anything, the way his father had built everything, an inch and an inch and an inch, on the flat unglamorous faith that inches add.

*You'd have hated every hour of it,* he thought at a dwarf forty years dead. *You never could stand slow work. Ma used to say it.*

He found that his eyes had gone hot, and he stood very still until they stopped.

"Until the prison erupted," Aravashnial said.

He opened the ledger and settled a finger on a dated entry.

"The official Drezen record calls it a riot."

He glanced at the map.

"I do not believe it was."

Another report came forward. "Cells were opened. Guards attacked. Weapons and tools taken. Prisoners moved into the corridors in several groups."

Another beside it. "Some fought toward the upper levels."

Another. "Others moved in the opposite direction."

His finger went back to the forgotten watercourse.

"Toward here."

He looked at them.

"It was an escape."

Then, more quietly: "And it had been planned for some time."

Thane was already there. He had been there since the word *groups.*

"They split," he said.

"Yes."

"Because a hole twenty feet long and a dwarf wide doesn't take forty men in five minutes." He heard his voice getting very flat. "So some of them went the wrong way loud."

Aravashnial's eyes came up and held his.

"Some of the prisoners stayed behind," he said. "Whether by choice, or because they could not reach the tunnel, I cannot determine. Several held the lower corridors while the others went out."

A pause.

"Many of those men died."

*Of course they did,* Thane thought. *That was the job. That was somebody's job and somebody took it.*

And he thought, and did not say, and would not say to anyone in that room or any other: *and I do not know which of the two of them took it, and I never will, and I have wanted to be the sort of man who did not need to know that, and I am not.*

---

Aravashnial reached for a much thinner ledger.

The room seemed quieter as he opened it, which was nonsense; nothing had changed. Thane noticed himself thinking it anyway, and noted, distantly, that he was not as steady as he had been managing to sound.

"The Citadel kept records of the bodies," the elf said. "Those recovered from the prison. Those found in the lower corridors. Those killed in the tunnel. Those recaptured afterward."

He hesitated. It was the only time he did.

"The bodies were disposed of in the refuse pit beneath the Citadel."

His mouth tightened.

"The one containing the green slime."

Thane had walked past that pit. Three weeks ago, on the way to somewhere else, with a timetable in his head. He had smelled it and read the room and moved on.

"I checked every name," Aravashnial said. He turned a page. "Then I checked them again."

He looked at Thane.

"Thorek Stonevein is not among them."

Then at Korroc.

"Neither is Borin Stonevein."

Thane closed his eyes for exactly as long as one breath took, and then opened them again.

"Say the rest."

Aravashnial pulled one final sheet from beneath the map.

"The demons pursued the escapees into the old watercourse. Parts of the tunnel were collapsed. Other sections were deliberately filled — which is why nobody has found it since, and why it does not appear on any plan drawn after that year."

His finger followed the old waterway out beyond the fortress, past the walls, past the edge of everything anyone had drawn.

"But not before prisoners reached the riverbed."

And he stopped talking.

---

The elf let the silence stand for a while, and then he did the thing that Thane would remember longest about that morning, which was that he laid his own case out against himself.

He touched the prison ledger.

"I can prove that your fathers survived the ambush."

He touched the disciplinary records.

"I can prove they were alive in Drezen, and that they were involved in repeated acts of resistance while they were here."

He touched the old map.

"And I am reasonably certain — not certain; *reasonably* certain — that they were involved in planning the escape."

Then, last, he laid two fingers on the list of the dead.

"What I cannot prove is what happened after they left the Citadel."

He paused.

"But their bodies were never recovered."

His eyes moved between them.

"And neither man was recorded as recaptured."

Thane made himself take it apart in front of the man who had given it to him, because that was the only way he knew to hold a thing without dropping it.

"Recapture lists get kept badly," he said.

"They do."

"And a body in a hole with a slime in it doesn't always keep a name."

"It does not." Aravashnial met his eyes without flinching. "I have thought about that a great deal. It is the strongest objection to everything I have told you and I am not going to pretend it away. I will tell you only this. They kept those lists to account for property, and a prisoner was property, and a clerk who cannot account for property is a clerk who answers for it. That is a poor kind of care and it is a very reliable one." He tapped the thin ledger. "This book is careless about nearly everything and rigorous about exactly one thing, which is whether a man was still owed to somebody."

Thane looked at the ledger.

"Aye," he said. "That'll do."

---

Aravashnial looked down at the old waterway on the map.

"That," he said, "is why I asked Irabeth and Aron to move the eastern bridge repairs up the list."

He tapped the dried riverbed beyond Drezen.

"Because if you intend to find out where your fathers went—"

He looked back at them.

"—this is where their trail begins."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Thane discovered he had been standing with his weight on his back foot for a long time, the way you do at a doorway you have not decided to go through, and he shifted it forward.

"How many of them got out?"

"I do not know."

"Best guess."

"I do not guess."

"Guess anyway."

Aravashnial looked at him for a long moment.

"Fewer than half of those who tried," he said. "More than a handful. I would not put a number in front of Irabeth and I will not put one in front of you."

"Right."

Thane looked at the map a while longer. Then he reached out and turned it, very slightly, to square it with the edge of the table, and took his hand away.

Beside him, Korroc had not spoken in a quarter of an hour. Thane knew what that meant and did not look, because there are things you do not do to a man in front of an audience, and one of them is check on him.

It was Nageru who broke it.

"Master Aravashnial," he said. "How long have you known you would say this today?"

The elf considered the question, and then answered it honestly, which was one of the reasons Thane had come round to him over the past months.

"I knew four days ago that I would say it eventually. I have spent since then deciding whether I could say it in a way that would leave them standing." He gathered a loose page and squared it against another. "I am aware that I may have failed at that. I am also aware that the alternative was to know this and let them go on not knowing it, and that I would then not have been able to look at either of them again."

"That is the correct answer," Nageru said.

Thane cleared his throat.

"Aravashnial."

The elf looked up.

Thane had had a whole sentence and it had gone somewhere. He stood there and could not find it, and refused, on principle, to hand over a worse one instead.

"Aye," he said finally. "Well."

Aravashnial inclined his head about a quarter of an inch, which was, Thane understood, the elf receiving the entire speech he had not managed to make.

"Yes," he said. "Quite."

---

The eastern bridge site was off-limits until Aron's people had it surveyed. Irabeth had said so at the table that morning in the flat voice she used for things she considered settled, and Aron had said it again on the stairs in fewer words, and Thane and Korroc walked out there together as the light was going and neither of them mentioned it.

It was a mile and a half beyond the eastern wall, down where the ground shelved off toward the old channel. The Ahari had shifted its bed in some year nobody had written down, and what was left was a broad dry scar full of shattered rock and thin gray scrub, with the stubs of the old bridge's piers standing out of it like the last four teeth in a jaw.

Korroc looked at the piers for a long moment and then rolled his shoulders.

"Right," he said. "Stand back a bit."

He went down onto one knee at the base of the nearest pier, put both gauntlets flat on the ground, and prayed.

Thane had watched his cousin do this perhaps a dozen times and it had never once stopped being strange. There was no flourish in it. Korroc said the words the way he said everything, out loud and unceremoniously, in a workshop voice — and then the ground under his hands *moved.*

Not violently. That was the wrongness of it. Stone flowed like poured wax and stopped being flowed the instant it arrived, and the broken lip of the pier grew a shoulder for a beam to sit on, and a scatter of rubble ten feet away drew itself together into a course of block with the joints already true. It made almost no sound at all. Thane's ears kept expecting one and kept not getting it, and that was the part that put his hair up.

Korroc did it four times. By the fourth he was gray around the mouth.

"That'll save them a week," he said, getting up in stages. "Maybe two. Aron'll not thank me."

"Aron'll not notice."

"Aron notices everything."

"Aye." Thane looked at the new stone. "He'll not thank you."

Korroc snorted.

He stood a while looking down the dry channel, east, where it ran out past the last of the light. Thane knew what he was looking at, because it was the same thing, and it was not visible from here or from anywhere.

"You coming?" Korroc said at last.

"No."

His cousin nodded as though he had asked purely for the record.

"Be careful," he said. "And don't do anything you shouldn't."

"That's two separate instructions."

"Aye, well. Pick one."

And he went, and the sound of a dwarf in plate walking away over broken ground carried a very long time, and then it did not, and Thane Stonevein was alone in a dry riverbed in the Worldwound with the dark coming down.

---

He worked it anyway. Of course he worked it.

The old tunnel came out somewhere along this channel — that was all anyone could say, and *somewhere along this channel* covered better than a mile in one direction and rather more in the other. Forty years of weather. Two floods that he could see the marks of and probably a dozen he could not. A demon garrison that had spent seventy years quarrying this bank for the citadel's repairs and throwing the spoil back down here when they were done.

He went at it in a grid, because his mother had taught him that a man who searches by instinct searches the same ground four times and calls it thoroughness.

*Listen first.*

He stood in the middle of the channel and shut his eyes and gave it a long count. Wind through the scrub. Something small in the rocks that stopped when he stopped. Far off behind him, the sound of Drezen at evening — a hammer somewhere, and the low collective noise of three hundred people who had all decided to eat at once.

No water. That was worth having. A collapsed culvert with forty years of runoff in it talks, and this did not.

*Look second.*

He went over the bank in sections while the light lasted, hunting the things a filled tunnel does to the ground above it long after everyone has forgotten it is there: a line of settlement, a place where the scrub grew in a strip because the fill drained differently, a slump. He found four candidates and killed three of them himself inside twenty minutes. The fourth held out until the light was nearly gone and then turned out to be the edge of a quarry cut.

*Touch third.*

He got down and went along the base of the bank on his hands and one knee, working his fingertips into the joints of the broken rock, feeling for the specific coldness that comes up out of a void behind a wall.

The bank was cold everywhere. It was that kind of country.

He found nothing.

He had known he would find nothing. That was the honest truth of it, and he had known it walking out here — one man, one evening, a mile and a half of ruined bank, and no better idea than *look.* Aron's engineers would open this ground properly over the next month with fifty men and a plan, and the odds were better than even that they would find the mouth of it, and the odds were better than even after that that it would be forty feet of packed fill and nothing else.

He worked until it was full dark, and then he sat down on a rock and did not go back.

---

He thought about the twenty feet.

Not about his father — he found he could not get at his father directly, that every road toward the man closed off before he arrived, which had been true for forty years and was somehow worse now that there was more road. But the twenty feet he could hold.

A dwarf takes a tool off a work detail. One tool. He does it in front of guards who count tools, and he pays for it later when the count comes up short, and he does it again the next week.

He gets back to a cell in the dark. He works out where he is — and he *can* work it out, because he set this stone, because thirty years earlier he stood on this ground in the free air with a plan in his hand, and there is no lock in the world so cruel as a room a man helped build.

Then he starts.

He hides the spoil in his clothing and in the latrine and in his food and in the seams of the wall. He works the hours nobody watches. He puts the face of it back every night so a guard with a lantern sees nothing. He teaches it to men who have never dug anything and cannot be told the whole plan, because a man who does not know cannot give it away. He is caught fighting. He is caught out of his detail. He is written up in a ledger by a clerk who thinks he is a nuisance, and the clerk is *right*, and that is the entire point of him.

And every night he lies down twenty feet from open country and does not know if he will live to spend it.

For better than a year.

Thane sat in the dark on a rock in the Worldwound and thought: *I could not have done that.*

It arrived with the total certainty of a thing that is true. He could pick any lock ever made. He could go through a fortress at night and leave no mark on the plate. He could stand in a doorway and hold it against six things that wanted through. He had never in his life been able to do a thing slowly on faith that it would come out.

He had wanted his father to be a hero, when he was young, in the ordinary way. Then for forty years he had wanted his father to have been *avenged*, which is a different appetite wearing the same coat, and he had built the whole of himself around it: the contract work, the listening, the flat cold cataloguing of every Templar name he could put a knife through. *Find the man who did it.* He had said it to himself in a hundred rooms.

He had found the man. Three weeks ago he had watched Radiance go into him, and had asked for him alive and been refused, and had spent the twenty-one days since discovering that a settled account is not the same thing as a full one.

And now here was the truth, and it was not vengeance-shaped at all.

His father had not been a hero and had not been avenged. His father had been a *nuisance in a ledger* for better than a year, and had dug twenty feet with a stolen tool, and had got out.

Thane put his hands over his face for a moment, in the dark, where there was nobody.

Then he took them down, and got up, and walked the last two hundred yards of bank he had not covered, because it was there and he had not covered it.

He found nothing on that stretch either. He had expected nothing. He went anyway, and that, he thought on the walk back to the walls, was probably the most he had inherited from anybody.

---

Irabeth sent for them in the morning, and the officer's court was in an uproar.

Thane read it before they were through the arch. Two supply wagons in the yard that had not been there at dusk and had come in loaded, judging by the tracks — deep going in, shallow going to the picket. Fresh horse-lines. A clot of soldiers by the west wall in surcoats that were far too clean, standing the way men stand when they have arrived somewhere and have not yet been told where to put anything. Sunbursts on half of them. Sarenrae's ankh on two.

"Reinforcements," Korroc said.

"Aye." Thane counted the horse-lines again as they went past. "And they came up the road in the dark, which means they were told to hurry."

The war room had a crowd in it. Irabeth was at the head of the table, and she looked up as the four of them came in and did something with her mouth that was very nearly a grin.

"Finally. You're here."

"We were sent for eleven minutes ago," Caleth said.

"Then you dawdled." She picked up a folded sheet, and Thane saw the seal, and the seal was the Queen's.

"After we took Drezen I sent messengers south," Irabeth said. "We have a response."

She read it out. She did not read it in a herald's voice, which Thane appreciated; she read it the way you read a letter.

*"To the Heroes of Drezen —*

*"Words cannot convey my gratitude for what you have done. I wish that I could say the worst is over, but as you must surely know, the liberation of Drezen is but the first of many steps. You are poised to serve in a way that no others among the crusaders can match. Use Drezen as a base of operations. Set out to explore the Wounded Lands to the south and west for anything else we can use against the demons. You, being on the front lines, doubtless have already isolated several possible leads to investigate, but do not hesitate to consult those I have sent north with you. Sosiel, Aron, Irabeth, and the rest know much of the region's history and legends.*

*"Word of your success and Drezen's liberation has already started to spread, and already I see the results in the faces of my soldiers. They fight with renewed morale. Our increased tenacity along the southern borders should keep the eye of Deskari turned away from you, affording you time to explore and investigate behind enemy lines.*

*"With your aid, I now feel that victory is finally something we can hope for.*

*"— Galfrey."*

Irabeth folded it.

"Along with the letter came a contingent of paladins and clerics, and workers, and supplies." She let that land on the room, and Thane watched several people's shoulders come down an inch. "Horgus is already fighting with the quartermaster and I have decided not to intervene."

*The first of many steps,* Thane thought.

He turned it over. It was the kind of sentence queens wrote, and it was also, he suspected, true, and he had noticed some time ago that the two were not usually the same thing.

"But it doesn't end our problems," Irabeth said. "Sosiel has brought something to me."

Sosiel Vaenic straightened up from where he had been leaning on the map table, and Thane looked at him properly for the first time that morning and revised his estimate of the day.

The man had not slept.

"A priest went out into the city about a fortnight ago," Sosiel said. "An Erastilian. He found a house in the eastern quarter with a sound roof and a stone foundation, and he made a shrine of it — cleaned the whole block by himself, more or less, and got a few of the local worshippers coming in." A pause. "He has been missing five days. The shrine has been desecrated."

"Desecrated how?"

"Thoroughly."

Irabeth put both hands on the table.

"Ordinarily I would not put this in front of the four of you," she said. "It is a city matter, and I have a Warden and a High Priest for city matters. But morale among our Erastilians has fallen through the floor, and it is spreading, and I would rather it were spreading for a reason I understood." Her jaw set. "Jesker Helton is the missing priest. Go and see what you can find."

---

They went to get their kit, and they were nearly out of the gate when the messenger caught them.

He came at a run, and Thane's hand was on his mother's blade before he had finished registering the boy's tabard, which was a thing he did not much care for about himself and had stopped apologizing for.

"Sirs. I've been looking for you all morning."

He had four envelopes.

He handed them out one at a time, and it took him a moment, because he had to check each one, and Thane stood in the gate arch of Citadel Drezen with something turning over slowly in his chest.

Then the boy put one in his hand.

The direction was in Dagna's hand.

Thane had seen that handwriting on the inside of every parcel that had ever come to him from Kenabres or from Nerosyan, and on a list of chores tacked to a door for eleven years, and once — once, and he had not thought about it in a decade — on the flyleaf of a book she had given him when he was forty and had been told he was too old for presents. Upright. Small. Bearing down hard on the downstrokes.

He looked round at the other three.

Korroc had one from the same hand and was staring at it.

Caleth had two. One heavy, one thin and rather badly folded.

Nageru had two as well, and had gone very still, which was not unusual, and had not opened either, which was.

Nobody said anything.

Thane walked six paces out of the gateway, put his back against the wall out of the traffic, and broke the seal.

---

*Thane,*

*Yes, you get your own letter.*

*I know Korroc has one as well, and before you decide you can simply read his and save yourself the trouble, you cannot.*

*This one is yours.*

He had to stop after that and look at the far wall for a moment.

*Drezen has been retaken. I have written those words more times in the last few days than I can count, and they still don't seem real.*

*But there is something stranger still.*

*You are there.*

*You and Korroc are walking the streets where we once walked.*

*I wrote to Korroc about what Drezen was to us, and I won't repeat all of it here. You have heard those stories often enough. You know Drezen was our home. You know your father and Korroc's father helped build the citadel. You know that was where the four of us met.*

*But there is something I want you to know that perhaps I have not said often enough.*

*Your mother loved that city.*

*She loved Drezen in a way I never quite did. I could always find something wrong with it. Too much noise. Too many soldiers. Too much mud when it rained. Your mother could stand in that same mud, look at those enormous walls, and somehow see everything that was good about the place.*

*She was like that.*

*When we fled, I think leaving Drezen hurt her almost as much as anything we left behind.*

*She always believed we would go back.*

*For a while, so did I.*

*Then years became decades.*

*Your father and Korroc's father disappeared.*

*And eventually your mother was gone too.*

Thane read that line, and then read it again, and then stood in the gate arch of Citadel Drezen with a piece of paper in his hands and did not move for some while.

He had been standing in this fortress for three weeks. He had walked up out of the prison rows with a demon's fingerprints still on the inside of him, and he had taken down a black banner and raised a red one, and every stone of the place had had his father in it, and not once — not one single time in three weeks — had he thought about his mother being here.

She was the one who had loved it.

*Disappeared,* he thought, a beat later, and something else went through him entirely.

Aunt Dagna had used that word his whole life. Not *died.* Not *fell.* He had assumed for forty years that it was an old woman being careful with two boys — softening a thing that does not soften.

An elf with a ledger had proved her right, and she did not know it yet.

He kept reading.

*Somewhere along the way, there were only the three of us.*

*You, Korroc, and me.*

*I don't remember exactly when you stopped being the boy who was always at my table and became another son I worried about.*

*Perhaps there wasn't a moment.*

*Perhaps that is simply how families work.*

*So understand something, Thane Stonevein.*

*When I hear stories coming north about what happened at Drezen, I worry about Korroc.*

*And I worry about you.*

*Equally.*

*I know what you are like. You will find the darkest corner, the locked door, the hidden passage, or the dangerous thing everyone else had enough sense to leave alone.*

*And Korroc will follow because somebody has to make certain you come back.*

*Some things apparently never change.*

Thane's mouth moved.

It was not a smile. It was the thing his face did instead of one, and there was nobody watching, and it was as close as he had come in about a month.

*But this time, you did come back.*

*You went to the city your parents were driven from and helped reclaim it.*

*I wish your mother could see you now.*

*I have been thinking about her ever since the news arrived. I keep imagining what I would say if she were sitting across from me.*

*I think I would tell her that her boy grew into a good man.*

*A troublesome one.*

*A stubborn one.*

*One with entirely too many knives.*

*But a good man.*

*I think she would be proud of you.*

*No.*

*I know she would.*

*Your father would be too.*

He had to stop for a bit there as well.

*There is something I asked Korroc to do, and I want you there with him.*

*Go walk through Drezen together. Find some of the old stone. Somewhere away from the fighting and the repairs. Put your hands against it.*

*Korroc will understand that part better than you. Try not to make fun of him for talking to a wall.*

*Then remember them.*

*Your mother. Your father. Korroc's father.*

*Remember that they stood where you are standing now. Remember that long before either of you carried weapons or marched beneath crusader banners, four young dwarves met in that city and somehow became a family.*

*You and Korroc are what remains of that.*

*Well, the two of you and one old dwarf in Nerosyan who fully intends to have some very unpleasant words with both of you about your apparent inability to stay out of danger.*

*There has been enough loss in Drezen. Do not make me bury either of my boys because you decided reclaiming the city wasn't dangerous enough.*

*Watch Korroc's back. Let him watch yours.*

*And come home. Both of you.*

*Your mother should be the one writing this letter.*

*I wish more than I can say that she could.*

*But since she cannot, I will tell you what I know she would have wanted you to hear.*

*She would be proud of you, Thane.*

*And so am I.*

*Dagna*

---

He read it twice.

The second time he read it the way he read everything, which he was not proud of and could not stop: the paper was Nerosyan stock and good; the ink had been bought recently; she had written it in one sitting without stopping to think, because the hand did not change anywhere and the line spacing never tightened.

She had known exactly what she was going to say before she picked up the pen.

He folded it along its own creases. Then he unfolded it and folded it smaller, and put it inside his chain shirt against his ribs, where a letter with his father's handwriting on it had ridden for two years.

Then he looked up.

Korroc had not moved at all. He was standing eight feet away with his letter held in both gauntlets like a man holding something that had been handed to him at a funeral, and his beard was doing that thing it did.

Nageru had opened the thicker of his two and was reading it a second time. Thane watched him get to the end of it and go back to a place about a third of the way down and read that part again, and then fold the whole thing into quarters and put it away inside his robe. His face had not changed by any amount a stranger would have measured. Thane was not a stranger, and he saw the aasimar's shoulders come down about a finger's width and stay down.

Caleth had read the heavy one, and had been reading it for some time.

Then he opened the thin badly-folded one.

Thane, who had spent a year learning to read Caleth Aranor and had got perhaps half of it, watched the half-elf's whole face come apart and reassemble in about two seconds. It was not grief and it was not joy. It was something with more parts to it than either, and it went through him, and when it was over he stood looking at the page with his thumb moving very slightly on the edge of it.

Thane looked away, because it was not his.

*Klarah,* he thought, from the size of the hand, and from the fact that the folding had been done by somebody who had never folded a letter before.

That accounted for one of them.

It did not account for what he had seen on the half-elf's face over the *first* one, which had been something Thane had no name for at all and had seen exactly once before, in a room with a false goddess in it. He filed it. He had a shelf for things about Caleth. It was not a small shelf and none of it had ever gone bad on him yet.

Nobody said a word.

That was the part Thane thought about later. Four crusaders standing inside the gate arch of a citadel they had taken, with the traffic going past and somebody shouting about barrels, and not one of them said anything at all. They put the letters away. They shouldered their kit.

And then they walked out into Drezen.

---

The eastern quarter was a long way through the city, and they took the long way through it.

Nobody said that was what they were doing. Korroc simply turned left where he ought to have turned right, at a corner with nothing to recommend it, and Thane went with him, and Caleth and Nageru — who had both been given to understand something at the gate without a word being spoken — dropped back about ten paces and stayed there.

It was a ruin. It had been a ruin for seventy-five years, and Thane had walked it twice already and had read it both times as ground: sightlines, choke points, which streets the enemy would use if they came back over the wall.

He made himself walk it as a *town.*

That was harder than it sounded.

Doorsteps worn in the middle. That was the first thing that got through — a run of six houses with the stone hollowed at the threshold by feet, over and over, decades of somebody's feet, and the roofs gone and the walls open to the sky and the doorsteps still worn where the door had been. A bakehouse with the oven still in it and the flue built up the outside wall in good brick. A street where every second lintel had a shallow niche cut over it, and no two niches the same, and nothing in any of them.

Not a fortress. Not a battlefield. Not the ruin he had grown up hearing about.

Somebody's whole ordinary life, in mud, complaining about the mud.

Korroc stopped at the corner of a building four streets short of the shrine.

It was nothing. That was what Thane noticed. It was the corner of some warehouse or hall, three courses of the original work still standing where the later stuff had come down off it, and it had no sign on it and no history that anybody would ever write, and nobody had been near it in seventy-five years except to walk past.

But it was old work. Thane could see that from ten feet. Cut square, joints true, the load running down through it the way his people set it running.

Korroc took his gauntlet off.

He put his bare palm flat on the stone, and stood there, and did not say anything for long enough that Thane began to wonder if he was going to.

Then he said, out loud, in a perfectly ordinary voice:

"Dagna remembers you."

Thane looked at the street.

"She says to tell you some of us survived," Korroc said. "She says to tell you the others were never forgotten. And she says—"

His voice did something in the middle of it and he waited until it stopped.

"—she says the sons of the men who helped build your walls have come home."

He stood there another moment.

"So. That's done."

Thane thought about *try not to make fun of him for talking to a wall,* and about the fact that his aunt had written that line knowing exactly which of the two of them would need to be told, and had been right.

He did not make fun of him.

He walked up and put his own hand on the stone beside his cousin's, which was not a thing he had planned to do, and which he did before he could arrange an opinion about it.

It was cold. It was just cold stone in a ruined street.

*You set this,* he thought. *Or you didn't. It's the same wall either way.*

And then, a moment later, the thought he had been keeping off since yesterday morning, which came in on its own the way they do:

*And then they brought you back and made you work on it in irons.*

He took his hand off.

"Right," he said.

"Aye."

They went on to the shrine.

---

Thane worked the neighborhood first, because you do not walk into a room until you know who has been in it.

It took him most of two hours, and he liked almost none of what he got.

There were not many Erastilians in Drezen. The Old Deity's people are farm people, and there had not been a farm within forty miles of this city in three generations, and the ones here were mostly soldiers who had come north out of a village somewhere and never quite left it behind. He found eleven of them. He talked to nine.

He did it the way he had done it in Kenabres for money: standing up, sideways, in the middle of something else, never in a row. He carried a crate for a woman for thirty yards and got four minutes out of it. He stood in a line for water. He let a man complain at him about the quartermaster for a quarter of an hour and got the whole thing at the end of it, the way you always do.

What he got:

Jesker Helton was young, and handsome, and had come north because of a genuine scholarly interest in the *old* Sarkorian worship of Erastil — the pre-crusade shape of it, the local one, the thing that had grown up in this country before Mendev ever came. Everyone said so. Two of them said it fondly, and one said it the way you say a thing that has begun to worry you.

The consecration had been going badly. That came up from every single person Thane asked, and none of them had thought it worth mentioning until he asked twice. Holy symbols lost. Holy symbols damaged. Mud on the prayer books — not once; repeatedly, and nobody could say how. And during a sermon three weeks ago, the longbow hung over the altar had come off its pegs and hit the stone and broken.

Jesker had taken all of it in stride. He had a phrase for it that two separate people repeated to Thane in almost the same words, which meant he had used it more than once and had liked it.

*The shrine spent a great many years in demonic hands. A few unquiet echoes are bound to remain.*

Thane wrote that one down in his head and underlined it.

Then there was the other thing, and it took him three conversations to get at, because nobody wanted to say it.

He got it at last out of a supply corporal in her forties who had gone quiet when he circled near it, and had then decided — Thane watched her decide — that she would rather say it to a stranger with a Drezen knight's badge than go on carrying it.

"He was kind to me," she said.

"Aye."

"He was. He was kind and he was funny and he asked after my sister." She was looking at the wall. "And when I walked away from it I felt like I wanted a bath, and I couldn't have told you one single word he'd said that would explain it. Not one." She looked at him. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"Aye," said Thane. "I do."

"Because I've said it out loud twice now, and both times it came out sounding like I'm accusing a good man of something."

"You're not accusing him of anything." Thane heard his own voice come out level and quiet and about ten degrees warmer than he had intended. "You're telling me what a room felt like. That's a fact about the room. It isn't an accusation about anybody."

She let out a breath.

"He wasn't like that when he came," she said. "That's the thing. He wasn't. And these last weeks he'd go dark for days — wouldn't speak, wouldn't eat — and then he'd be bright as a lamp again, and you'd think you'd imagined it." She rubbed her face. "And he lost his mother's ring. Her wedding ring; he wore it on a cord. He was cut up about that for a week. I keep thinking about it and I can't say why."

Thane could have said why. He did not.

He thanked her, and moved on, and stood in an empty street for a minute afterward doing nothing at all.

*Two men,* he thought. *You've got two men in one set of clothes, and everybody here has been apologizing to themselves about it for a month.*

---

The shrine was a humble wooden chapel on a stone foundation, two rooms, and Thane read it from the doorway before he let anybody in.

"Wait."

They waited. Even Caleth, who had learned.

Blood first — animal, from the smell, and thrown rather than poured, in arcs across the floor and up the walls at about the height of a man's shoulder. Not much of it, spread thin to look like a great deal, which told him something about intent.

The Erastil statue had been broken up. Not toppled: *broken*, with something heavy, and then the pieces had been broken again.

And on every wall, scratched into the wood, line after line of Abyssal.

Nageru went very still just inside the door.

"Blasphemies," Caleth said, reading. "Insults. Nothing that means anything — it's a man shouting."

Thane was not reading the words. He was reading the height.

"Look at the top line," he said.

Caleth looked. "What about it?"

"Reach." Thane put his own arm up against the wall by way of demonstration and got nowhere near it. "Man's height with his arm up. Every one of them. Nothing higher, nothing on the beams." He walked the length of the wall with his eyes going along the bottom. "And nothing lower than his knee, and no marks where somebody stood on anything."

"So it was done by one man standing on the floor."

"Aye." Thane crouched. "And done with this."

He did not touch it. It was lying at the base of the wall where it had been dropped: a bent iron nail, the point gone blunt and rolled over from being dragged through wood for hours.

"That's a nail out of this building," he said. "He pulled it out of somewhere and he went round the whole room with it, and it took him half a night at least, and he did it alone."

Korroc's voice was low. "You're sure alone?"

"Floor's damp under the blood, and it's a wooden floor over stone." Thane pointed without looking up. "One set of boots in and out of the mess, going everywhere, back and forth, over its own marks a dozen times. Same boot. Nobody else came in here after the blood went down." He straightened. "And nobody forced the door. It was unlocked, or he had the key. He had the key. He lived here."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Then he did it himself," Korroc said.

"His hands did it." Thane heard his own voice change and could not stop it in time. "That's not the same sentence."

He turned away from the wall and went to the altar to give himself somewhere else to look.

Across the front of it, smeared in blood by a finger, in Common:

*JUST AS DELAMERE NOW WEEPS TEARS OF FIRE, SO SHALL THOSE WHO FOLLOW GO TO THE PYRE!*

"Delamere," Caleth said, and something went across his face — the click of a man finding a card in a deck he had not known he was holding. "That's a name. That's — saint's mercy, that's an *old* name. Delamere was a Sarkorian priestess of Erastil. Before the Wound. Well before."

"Known for what?"

"Being wrong, mostly."

There was a low wall of rubble where the statue had been. Nageru had gone to it while they talked and had crouched there, and now he rose with a book in both hands.

It was bound in green leather, and it was filthy, and it had been read to pieces.

"*The Stag King's Bride*," he said.

He handed it to Caleth, who opened it, and read, and got about a page in before his eyebrows went up.

---

It took the half-elf twenty minutes, and he summarized it standing in the wreck with the door open behind him for the light.

"She taught that cities were the greatest blight humanity ever conceived," Caleth said. "Any settlement of more than fifty-three people was inherently evil."

Thane blinked. "Fifty-three."

"Fifty-three."

"Why fifty-three?"

"I was afraid you'd ask." Caleth turned a page and his mouth did something pained. "Because a family is five. And six families of five stand around that family — grandparents, cousins, nieces and nephews, thirty in the ring — and the arithmetic is not arithmetic, it is a diagram. Which comes to fifty-three, with something fudged in the middle that I frankly cannot follow. And therefore the *fifty-fourth* person in any settlement stands outside the family entirely." He looked up. "And is therefore likely to be a traitor to it."

Korroc stared at him. "That's not sense."

"No."

"That's not even wrong in an interesting way."

"No," Caleth agreed. "It was enormously popular, though. It got hold in the small towns of northeastern Sarkoris and never quite let go, and when she died they buried her in a considerable tomb, and ever since then the families who followed her have each been assured a place in it." He turned to the last pages. "Which is here. The Weeping Hills. Geothermal country — vents, hot springs, that sort of thing. South of us, and not far."

Thane looked over his shoulder.

There was a message written on the last leaf in ink. It was recent — the ink still black where the print around it had gone brown — and it was in a young man's careful hand.

*Delamere has the right of it — Drezen is too big for its own good. A trip to her tomb to search for more of her teachings may be in order.*

Nobody said anything.

Thane read it three times.

"That's the same hand as the ledger of the shrine's stores," he said. "I saw it in the back room."

"Then it's his."

"Aye." Thane straightened up. "And it's not *him.*"

Caleth looked at him.

"Read the sentence." Thane put a finger under it. "*A trip to her tomb to search for more of her teachings may be in order.* That's how a man writes when he's making himself a note about a job of work. That's a scholar. That's the same fellow who came north because he wanted to know what Erastil looked like in this country before we got here." He tapped the altar behind him without turning. "And that, on the front of the altar, is somebody screaming."

"Two voices."

"Two voices, and one of them's been getting louder for a month, and everybody in this city noticed and every one of them talked themselves out of it." He looked at the smashed statue. "*A few unquiet echoes are bound to remain.* He gave them a phrase for it. They took it, because it fit, and then they stopped looking."

He heard how that came out.

"Aye," he said, before anyone else could. "I know."

---

"Of course the tomb would be in this area," Caleth muttered on the way back, to nobody.

Getting out of Drezen took the rest of the day and most of Thane's patience.

Horgus Gwerm was at a trestle table in the cold hall with three clerks and a wall of columns, and he heard the whole request out with his chin on one hand, and then supplied every single thing they asked for inside twenty minutes and made two of them better. Thane, who had spent the past months expecting to have to fight that man for a length of rope, took his list and found he had nothing whatever to complain about, which put him in a worse mood than being refused would have.

Then they went up to the eastern tower.

Aravashnial listened to all of it with two fingers on his chin, and Thane watched the elf's face go through the tomb, the priest, the possession and the fifty-fourth person, and settle.

"The Weeping Hills," he said. "Yes. You want to go back to Vilareth Ford."

Thane, who had already looked at the map, said: "What?"

"Vilareth Ford. The first village we came to after Kenabres." Aravashnial pulled a chart across the table and turned it. "West of the ford is a formation called Eagle Rock. It is the only place along that escarpment where you can get a wagon up the cliffs, and from the top you can travel inland to the Weeping Hills without going around."

"That's days out of our way." Thane put one finger on Drezen and drew it, hard, in a straight line. "That's a *week* out of our way. There's a direct route right there."

"There is."

"So why are we walking backward to the ford?"

Aravashnial did not become irritated, which was itself an answer.

"You could take that route," he said. "It goes through the middle of some very untamed country. The last anyone could tell me, it was the actual border of the Wound — genuinely unheld, on both sides — and it is full of swarms of things you will not enjoy. Not demons especially. Swarms." He looked up. "Take the road you like. I would suggest going round by the ford and then the shortest line inland from there. And Caleth can put you at the ford in a step and save you the entire march."

Thane opened his mouth.

"I can teleport us," Caleth said, studying the chart. "Not with horses and a wagon."

"The Queen has a camp in that area." Aravashnial waved a hand. "Supplies, staging, troops moving north. You can get what you need there."

And there it was.

"Horgus's requisitions," Thane said, "are not going to be worth what they're worth in this building."

Nageru, who had not said anything for an hour: "I am sure we can get what we need. The Queen has given us a mission."

"You say that now." Thane turned. "While we're standing in a fortress with our own quartermaster forty feet down the stair. Can you guarantee it?"

Nageru considered it. He always considered it. That was the maddening thing about arguing with the man — he never once gave you the satisfaction of a fast answer you could take apart.

"No," he said. "but I think it is our best route."

Thane looked at him a moment.

Then he rolled the chart up and handed it back to the elf, harder than he needed to.

"Fine."

He was aware, going down the tower stairs, that he was not actually angry about the ford. He was angry because he had come off a riverbed the night before with nothing in his hands, and had spent the day reading a man's ruin off a wall, and now four people were going to step sideways across sixty miles of the world in a half-second, and there would be *no ground between here and there for him to read.*

He did not say that to anybody. It would have sounded like exactly what it was.

---

An hour later they stood on the outskirts of Vilareth Ford in the late afternoon, and walked in.

The first person they met was a survivor from the tiefling camp they had broken a year ago — a woman named Kamilo Dann, who had been standing in a rope pen with her wrists tied when Thane first saw her, and who came running across the road now with her hands up and her face doing three things at once.

"Oh! Hello, heroes! What are you doing back in the Ford?"

"We have a mission west of here," Caleth said. "We'll need supplies. Who can we talk to?"

"I can help you with that." She was already turning, already organizing, and Thane revised his estimate of her upward. "Anything for the Queen's heroes."

Caleth smiled and nodded, and Thane put his thumbs in his belt and looked at the middle distance.

The evening was very bad.

It was not bad in any way he could complain about out loud, which was what made it bad. There was food, and it was decent. There were people, and they wanted to talk. They wanted to talk a great deal — about Drezen, about the banner, about whether it was true the citadel had a demon-forge in it, about a marilith, about the Queen, about whether Iomedae had appeared to anybody personally and if so what she had said. A man asked Korroc twice whether the four of them had really taken the fortress alone while the army watched, and Korroc said no twice, patiently, and the man went off looking as though he had been done out of something.

Thane got asked how many demons he had killed.

He said he had never counted, which was true, and the boy who had asked looked at him as though he had confessed to not knowing his own name.

He found a wall at the edge of the firelight around the second hour and stood against it, and Nageru arrived beside him at some point without appearing to have crossed any ground, which was a thing the man did.

They stood there together a while, watching Caleth be gracious at four people at once.

"You do not like this," Nageru said.

"No."

"Why not?"

Thane thought about how to put it.

"I spent three months in Kenabres," he said, "being nobody in particular, in places where being nobody in particular was the whole of the job. It's a craft. You get good at it." He nodded at the fire. "I'm no good at this. And they've decided I'm something, and now if I go and stand in a market three towns from here, somebody'll know my face."

"That is a real cost."

"Aye. It is." He shrugged. "Won't get it back, either."

Nageru was quiet a moment.

"My order sent word," he said.

Thane did not turn his head. "Aye. I saw."

"They are pleased about Drezen." A pause. "They also gave me something else to do."

"Now?"

"Eventually." Nageru's voice did not change at all. "It has waited seventy-five years. It can wait for the priest."

Thane grunted.

He wanted, with a suddenness that surprised him, to ask what it was. He did not. He had a rule about that, learned young and reinforced often: a man who volunteers half a thing has decided exactly how much of it he is giving you, and if you go after the rest you get less of him later.

"If you need hands for it," he said instead, "say so."

"I will."

They slept eventually. In the morning there was a horse and a wagon in the road for the dwarves and the supplies, and Kamilo Dann standing beside them at an hour Thane had not expected her to be up.

"Good luck in your journeys!"

Caleth swung up onto the riding horse and smiled down at her. "Luck? Do we need luck?"

She laughed. Then she stopped laughing.

"Anyone heading west needs luck," she said. "You should know there are demon patrols through that country. They come close, some weeks. And there are reports that some of them have taken up in Eagle's Rock itself."

Caleth's face did not change by very much. "Thank you for the warning."

Thane climbed up onto the wagon seat beside his cousin and took the reins and thought: *she told us. Whatever happens today, somebody told us, and I heard it.*

He would come back to that.

---

They went west into the Wound, and by the middle of the morning there was nothing behind them that anybody had built.

Thane had spent a year now looking at this country and had still not got used to the way it declined to be a landscape. It did not look burned. Burned land is honest; it tells you what happened and how long ago. This looked like ground that had been *argued with.* The rock came up through the soil at angles that no fault had made. The scrub grew in colors that had no business on a plant, and grew thickest where nothing should grow at all, and there were long bare runs of hardpan through the middle of it with the same faint scalloping across them, mile after mile, like the inside of something's throat.

He drove and read it and got nothing back.

Several hours out they saw the towers.

Five of them, up on the lip of the escarpment, standing black against a white sky — ruined stubs, uneven, broken off at different heights. Broken fingers jutting up out of a fist. And below them the cliff face itself came round in a great curve and folded, and there was the head, and the hooked beak, and the hollow that was the eye.

"Eagle Rock," Caleth said, from the horse.

"Aye," said Korroc. "Somebody wasn't stretching."

Thane pulled the wagon up a half-mile short.

"I'm going ahead."

Nobody argued. He went off the seat and into the ground the way he did — down into a fold, along it, up behind a run of broken rock — and inside forty seconds he was somewhere the wagon could not see him, and inside two minutes he was a long way off.

He gave it the better part of an hour.

There were ruins on the far side of the rock formation: an old fort, small, pre-Wound work, thoroughly ruined and thoroughly picked over. He worked the whole approach. He read the ground for tracks and found the usual — old, weather-ruined, nothing he could put a date on inside a season. He worked the fort's broken wall line from the outside and then went in and did the two standing rooms. He listened for a long count in three separate places.

He found nothing.

He came back and swung up onto the seat and said so.

"There are ruins on the other side of that formation. An old fort. I didn't see anything else."

They rolled forward, heading for the gap.

Afterward he would take that hour apart a great many times and never once find the place where he had been careless. He had worked it correctly. He had used every part of the grammar his mother had put in his hands. He had gone slow and he had gone quiet and he had come back and reported precisely what he had, which was nothing.

*A thing that flies doesn't leave you anything to read.*

He would arrive at that eventually and it would not help much.

---

They were close to the gap when Thane stood up on the wagon seat.

"What is that buzzing."

Everything stopped. Korroc's head came round; Caleth reined in. The sound was low and rising and it was not one sound, it was a great many small ones stacked together, and it was getting nearer at a rate that meant it was in the air.

The horses started to go.

"*Off the wagon—*"

It came over the top of the rock formation forty feet up and dropped at them.

Thane's mind took it in pieces on the way down, because that was what his mind did and there was nothing to be done about it: wings, insectoid. A humanoid torso above and a thing like a grasshopper's haunches below, folded and enormous. Long arms. And behind it all, curling up over the whole body, a scorpion's tail with the barb turned forward.

"*Derakni!*" Korroc bellowed.

It cast before it reached them.

There was no gesture Thane could see. The air in the middle of the group simply *filled* — four separate places at once, the space between wagon and horse and rider all going gray and granular in the space of a breath — and then the gray resolved into wasps.

Not wasps. Wasp-sized, and wasp-shaped, and there were thousands of them in each one.

Nageru went forward and under, moving out of the wagon's lee and toward the thing in the air, and his arm came back and the javelin left his hand — and it was not a javelin by the time it was ten feet gone. It was a bolt of white lightning, and it hit the derakni square, and the crack of it slapped off the cliff face and came back.

The demon did not flinch.

It did not even change course. It came on through the flash as though it had walked through a doorway.

Thane saw Nageru's face register it. Not surprise. Recalculation — the same half-second the man had spent on a specter's chest in the prison rows, the moment where a tool comes up short and gets put down.

Then the swarms arrived.

Thane had been bitten in his life. He had taken a cave lizard's teeth through the thigh and a glaive through the middle of him and a streak of acid across the shoulder, and he had a working relationship with all of it. This was not that. This was every square inch of him at once — inside his collar, inside his sleeves, in his beard, at his eyes, a hundred small hot stitches a second, and the noise of it filling his skull so that he could not hear whether anybody was shouting.

He got an arm across his face and went down off the wagon seat because standing up in it was how you got taken off it.

Somewhere to his right, the world folded — that cold small nothing he had felt a hundred times now — and Caleth and Korroc and the riding horse were simply *gone* out of the middle of it and standing forty feet clear.

Which left two of them in it.

The derakni came round in a long flat curve and swept across the ground at Nageru, and Thane got his eyes open long enough to see the aasimar go under most of it — a duck and a turn, the body doing in a half-second what most men need a plan for — and one claw came across his back and opened his robe and the skin under it.

Nageru turned as it went by, and his hand was already back.

The second javelin was not lightning. It was cold iron, plain and gray and heavy, and it went into the demon's flank and stuck there.

"*Down!*" Caleth's voice, from forty feet away. "Thane — *down!*"

Thane did not ask what for. He dropped and rolled and kept rolling.

The fireball went off above him.

He felt the whole shape of it go over — the pressure first, then the heat, a great flat orange bloom filling the entire space where the swarms were and where he had been standing a half-second before. Grit and burning insects rained down on his back. He came up on one knee with his ears ringing and his eyebrows intact and a great deal of the middle distance on fire.

The swarms came down out of the air in sheets. All four of them, at once, in a dry rustling fall like grain out of a split sack, and then there was nothing in the air but smoke.

Thane got his short bow off his back.

He hated the bow. He had always hated the bow. It was in his kit because a man who works alone in strange buildings occasionally needs to make a noise thirty feet from where he is standing, and because his mother had made him learn it and had been right, and he had never once in his life been glad of it.

*Wrong distance. Everything today is the wrong distance.*

He put two arrows into the derakni anyway, which was two more than he expected, and beside him Korroc's hands came up and two rays of fire crossed the gap and burned into the thing's shoulder.

It answered by coming for him.

He got a bad look at it on the way in — the front of the torso where the arrow shafts stood out, the tail cocked over, the head — and then the claws were on him. He turned the first with the bow-stave, which cost him the bow, and the second went into his upper arm and drew down and out and took a strip.

He heard himself grunt. He hated that. His cousin did it too and they had both got it from the same place.

Then it was past him and up, and Nageru had moved again, and the third javelin was in the air.

It hit. The demon's climb faltered.

"Caleth!" Korroc roared.

The half-elf had not moved from where the fold had put him. He had one hand out and his eyes on the thing in the sky, and he was speaking, and Thane — who had been standing next to that particular sound for a year now — heard the shape of it and knew it was not a killing spell.

The word landed.

The derakni came apart. Not into pieces. It *unfastened* — a smear of the world going sideways around it, wings and torso and tail sliding out of true and then out of sight — and the noise it had been making stopped in the middle of a beat, and there was nothing over the rocks at all.

Silence. Wind. Something small still burning in the scrub.

Korroc lowered his hands slowly.

"Did you just send it back to hell?"

Caleth nodded.

Thane sat down on the ground and looked at his arm.

He was thinking, in the flat cold way he thought about everything, that the thing was not dead. It was standing somewhere in its own country right now, four javelins and two arrows the worse, entirely intact, with the faces of four crusaders in its head and nothing whatever to do for the rest of eternity.

He put it in the ledger. It was a long ledger by now.

"Right," he said, getting up. "Somebody go and find the horses."

---

They found the horses. It took a while; two of them had gone the better part of a mile before their nerve gave out, and Nageru went after the wagon on foot and brought it back in less time than Thane thought was decent.

It was late afternoon before they reached the chasm.

Thane had been expecting a hill. That was the honest truth of it. *The Weeping Hills*, and a tomb in them, and he had built a picture of a mound with a door in it, the way you do without deciding to.

The ground simply stopped.

They came over a low rise and there was a gap two hundred feet across running away southwest for as far as he could see in either direction, and the near lip was crumbling, and the far lip was a hundred yards off, and it went *down.*

The four of them stood at the edge and looked into it.

"Two hundred feet," Thane said. "At least."

Nageru nodded. "And it is at the bottom, I expect."

Caleth had gone along the rim twenty paces and come back. "I don't see a path down. Anywhere."

Then he looked over properly, and stopped.

At the bottom of the chasm, a river of molten rock moved.

It came out of the rifts in the chasm floor in slow cascades — Thane could see three of them from here, seams in the rock weeping fire the way a wall weeps water — and it gathered and ran southwest in a broad sullen band, and the heat coming up off it hit him in the face at two hundred feet and made his eyes water.

Thane looked along the rim.

"Rocky area over there," he said, and pointed back, away from the chasm.

Nageru looked at him.

Thane looked back. "I don't want the horses to get eaten."

So they saw to the horses first, because that is what you do. They took the two out of the traces and led all three in among a spill of broken rock two hundred yards back from the edge and did what they could to make them look like more rock, and put the wagons in a separate hollow, and Korroc muttered something over the lot of it that Thane did not ask about.

Then they went to the edge.

Nageru looked down once, took two steps, and went over the lip.

He did it the way another man steps off a curb. Thane got to the edge in time to see him already twelve feet down, moving from hold to hold on a face Thane would have called unclimbable, one hand and then one foot, no hurry in it anywhere.

"Show-off," Thane said, with feeling.

Caleth was already at work with pitons and a hammer, driving a line into the rim.

Thane looked at the rope, and at the two hundred feet, and then reached to his belt and put two fingers on the silver scale.

It was warm. It was always warm.

Beside him Korroc did the same, and the two of them looked at each other, and stepped off the edge together.

The world let go of them.

Thane had used the scale six or seven times now and it had never once stopped being the strangest thing that had ever happened to him. There was no falling. There was simply a decision, made somewhere behind his sternum, that down was a direction he was going at a walking pace, and the cliff face began to go up past his shoulder, and Terendelev's silver came off the plate at his belt in a faint cold light that the rising heat could not touch.

*Get them out,* the scale said, the way it always did. It was not a voice. It was the shape of somebody's last thought, still going.

Down and down. And hotter.

That came on gradually and then all at once. At fifty feet it was a warm day. At a hundred it was standing too near a fire. At a hundred and fifty the air stopped being air and became a thing that pushed back, and Thane's beard began to crisp at the ends of it, and he put his forearm over his mouth and breathed through his sleeve and did not stop going down.

At a hundred and eighty he saw the cave.

"There," he called down. "Right of the fall. Behind the shoulder."

They came down onto the floor of the chasm about sixty feet from a river of molten stone, and the whole world was orange.

Korroc got there last, and landed, and immediately began muttering — hands out, both of them, the words rolling in that unhurried workshop cadence — and something came down over the four of them like a curtain going between Thane and the fire.

It did not make it cool. It made it survivable, which was a different and much more welcome thing.

"Ten minutes on that," Korroc said. "So don't dawdle."

"Never do," said Thane, and went in first.

---

The tunnel ran forty feet and opened out.

The cavern was enormous and it was lit entirely by what was moving through it. The lava came in at the far end and crossed the floor in a lazy band, and the floor on either side of it was broken open — gaping pits, a dozen of them, some of them wide enough to lose a wagon in, all of them dropping away into a red that Thane declined to think about. The roof went up thirty feet and was cracked from end to end, and the cracks breathed. He could see the gas coming out of them in slow shimmers.

The heat in here was worse than outside. Korroc's curtain took the edge off and the edge was all it took.

And out in the middle of it, on a small ledge with a fifty-foot pit between it and the entrance, there was a slab of smoky crystal.

Thane looked at it and had to work to make his eyes accept what they were being given, because there was a shape inside the crystal. A human shape. Not a carving — the crystal held it the way ice holds a leaf, deep and blurred and about two-thirds visible, and it was lying on its back with its hands crossed.

And sitting on top of the crystal, with his boots hanging over the edge of it, was a man.

Nobody had to say who.

Thane read him from sixty feet in the orange light and did not like one single thing he read. The armor was crusader work and had been good, and the front of it was caked with vomit — days of it, layered, dried and gone over again. The hands hung between the knees. And the eyes, when the head came up, were the eyes of a man who has not slept in five days and has stopped wanting to.

Jesker Helton got to his feet.

He faced them across the pit, and he spoke, and his voice came out of him like something being dragged over gravel.

"I've only brought shame upon my family, my god, and all the people who loved and trusted me."

"*Jesker—*" Caleth's voice went out across the gap, and Thane heard him put everything he had into the shape of it.

"You've come here to save me, but you're too late." The priest's head turned, slowly, toward the ledge's edge and the red beneath it. "I'm done with this failure of a life, and now that you're here to witness my end—"

He stopped talking.

He did not finish the sentence. He stood there on the crystal with his weight already going forward.

Nageru was shouting. Thane could hear him shouting — *stop, brother, be still* — and he could hear Caleth going at it from the other side with everything a paladin had, and both of them were doing exactly the right thing and neither of them was doing any good at all.

Thane did not shout.

He was looking at the man's feet.

Because a man who has decided to die makes a decision, and a decision goes into the body, and Thane Stonevein had watched more people decide things than most inquisitors ever would. He knew what it looked like in the shoulders. He knew what it looked like in the hands. And what he was looking at across fifty feet of red air was a man whose *feet were arguing with his knees* — the weight going forward and the heels resisting it, the whole frame at odds with itself, like a door being pushed from both sides at once.

That was the first thing.

The second thing came up out of somewhere much further down and arrived whole, and it was not a deduction at all. It was recognition.

He knew what that looked like. He had worn it.

Three weeks ago in a black room under Citadel Drezen, something had gone into his mouth and his nose and the corners of his eyes and had taken the whole of him, and he had stood there in his own body with his own hands and watched them go high-low at his cousin with his mother's blade and his father's knife. He had been *in there.* That was the part he had never said to anyone. Nobody had asked, and he had come back and wiped two clean blades and put them away, and Korroc had tied a thread back into his chest and said *there, that's better,* and Thane had said *aye, that's better,* and that had been the whole of it.

Twenty-one days ago.

Nobody had ever spoken of it since. He had made sure of that in the way he was good at, which was by leaving no door in himself where the subject could get in.

He said it low, under the shouting, to the three men beside him and nobody else.

"Something's wrong with him."

Korroc's head came round.

"He doesn't look like he's in control of his own actions."

There was a very short silence, on their side of the cavern, in the middle of two other men shouting.

"You're sure," Caleth said.

"Aye."

The half-elf did not ask him how he knew.

Thane noticed that. He noticed it the way you notice a lock that opens on the first try, and he put it somewhere and did not look at it, because Caleth Aranor had already had a wand out of his sleeve and was speaking, and there was work to do.

The spell went across the cavern and closed on Jesker Helton like a hand.

He stopped mid-lean. Every part of him — the shoulders, the hanging arms, the argument between the heels and the knees — went out all at once, and he stood on top of a dead woman's coffin as still as the coffin.

Korroc had his eyes narrowed and his head tilted, doing the thing he did.

"Heat's taken him," he called. "Bad. That's all I've got from here. He's a man who's been cooking for five days and I don't see anything else in him."

"Keep looking," Thane said.

And then something came out of Jesker Helton.

---

It rose.

That was the only word for it. It came up out of the priest's chest and shoulders the way steam comes off a horse, and it gathered above him, and it took a shape, and the shape had wings.

Thane's first thought was that somebody had made it out of two things and had not cared how they went together.

It had a demon's face — fanged, flat, inhuman, the expression on it set in a permanent contempt that did not seem to be about anything in particular; it was simply the shape the face had been left in. It had wings, and it had a body, and all of that was of a piece.

And bulging out of the back of the skull, pushing through, was a second face.

That one was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing Thane had ever seen, and it was wearing an expression of such complete and undiluted horror that he had to make himself go on looking at it.

Beside him Nageru said something in a language Thane did not know, very quietly, and did not translate it.

"*Shachath,*" Caleth said.

Thane got his short bow up — it had a stave with a claw-mark in it now, and he had strung it anyway — and put two arrows across the cavern at the thing.

They both went wide.

He cursed in Dwarven, comprehensively, and reached for the next two.

Caleth was already moving. Thane saw him draw on something down inside himself, the way he did, the small gathering-in that always came before he spent more than he had — and then the rays came off his hand, three of them, and they should have been fire and they were not. They crossed the cavern white and cold, hoarfrost trailing behind them in that furnace of a room, and all three went home.

Korroc's arm came back.

The axe he threw was new. He had turned it up somewhere in the citadel's stores and had been quietly pleased about it for a week, and Thane watched it go end over end across fifty feet of open air and hit the shachath so hard that the thing folded around it — and then the axe came off it and came *back*, turning, and dropped into Korroc's hand as though it had been on a string.

"Hah," said Korroc.

Then Jesker jerked.

Thane's head snapped round. The priest had come out of the hold — the whole body starting all at once, the way a man starts out of a dream — and his legs were not ready for it. He slid off the smoky crystal and hit the ledge and went down.

The shachath turned in the air.

And the room went off in colors.

Thane had no other way to describe it, then or later. It came out of the demon in a burst and it filled a third of the cavern — shards of light ricocheting off the walls and the pits and each other, every color that exists and two that Thane was fairly sure did not, and it was not light, because it went through him instead of past him.

He set his teeth and refused it. He was good at that. Whatever it was, it had no business in there, and he told it so in the only way he knew, and it slid off him.

Korroc grunted and stood. Nageru did not appear to have noticed it at all.

Caleth was not so lucky.

Thane saw him take the full weight of it and stagger — and then not recover. The half-elf's next step came down wrong, and the one after that, and Thane watched a man who had crossed rooms by *folding the world* suddenly moving as though the air had gone to syrup, dragging one foot after another, his own body arriving late to every instruction.

"Caleth—"

"I'm here." Through his teeth. "I'm *slow.* Not down."

The shachath swept past the ledge and its tail came round and caught Jesker Helton across the back.

The priest went off the edge.

He got one hand on a rock as he fell and grabbed hold with his entire life. Thane saw it happen and could not get anywhere near it — fifty feet of open pit between him and the ledge, and a man hanging over red by four fingers.

Thane put two more arrows into the air.

The first missed. The second did not, and it was one of the ones he kept for demons — cold iron, and a working laid into the head by a priest in Kenabres two years ago — and it went into the shachath's flank and the thing *screamed*, which none of the rest of it had made it do.

"Korroc!" Caleth had a wand out and was dragging himself two steps to reach him. "Hold still—"

He put his hand on Korroc's shoulder and the dwarf gone.

He came back into the world above the ledge, on the far side of the cavern. Korroc landed and went straight down onto one knee at the edge.

Nageru's hands came up and caught fire.

Thane saw it from thirty feet: the white-gold running up the aasimar's forearms and pooling in his fists, the smite gathering, the whole of the man's law and conviction arriving in his hands at once.

And then Nageru looked at the fifty feet of empty air between himself and the demon.

He was too far. He had been too far all fight. Every javelin he had thrown had gone at the edge of his reach, and now he had a fistful of holy fire and nothing within thirty feet of him to put it in.

Thane watched him solve it in about a second and a half.

Nageru reached down without looking, took up a javelin, and — Thane could not have said how, and Caleth explained it to him twice afterward and he was no wiser — *put the fire into it.* The white-gold went out of the aasimar's knuckles and ran down the shaft, and he threw, and eight feet of consecrated iron went across the cavern and hit the shachath in the middle of the back.

It shrieked and dropped ten feet before it caught itself.

On the far ledge, Korroc reached down into the stone.

Thane knew what that looked like now. His cousin's whole body changed — settled, rooted, went from a dwarf standing on rock to a piece of the rock that happened to be standing up — and he put one arm down over the lip and got a fistful of Jesker Helton's wrist and hauled him bodily back onto solid ground, one-handed, in a suit of gold-and-mithral plate, without moving his feet.

Then he stopped.

Thane saw it happen at thirty feet. Korroc had the priest half across his knees and had been looking him over the way he looked over anybody he pulled off a floor — and then his cousin's chin came up, and something went across his face, and his eyes narrowed hard.

Korroc had opened his senses on the man.

And whatever was coming off Jesker Helton, it was not heat stroke.

"*Korroc?*" Thane called.

His cousin did not answer him. That was answer enough.

---

Then the shachath said a word, and was not there.

"*Ach* — of course," Thane said. "Of *course.*"

He got his eyes moving. That was the whole of the trade and he had learned it the hard way over a year: an invisible thing is not a thing you look for, it is a thing you *predict.* He worked the room in his head — where it had been, what it wanted, who was hurt and who was isolated and where it would go if it were sensible.

Caleth got there first.

A patch of the air forty feet up and to the left burst into a glittering cloud, a fine gold dust hanging in the heat.

There was nothing in it.

"Not there," Caleth said, hoarse.

Nageru turned in a slow circle with his hands open, and Thane watched a man who could hear a berbalang through a stone wall find absolutely nothing in a cavern full of moving lava and hissing gas, and stop, and stand very still with his face perfectly blank, which for Nageru was the loudest sound he ever made.

Then Korroc got up off his knees and put one hand out and *purged* it.

Thane never learned the shape of that working and never wanted to. There was a soundless sweep across the whole chamber, and the world came clean, and the shachath was standing directly in front of Korroc Stonevein with a longsword already up over its head.

Korroc's swearing went across the cavern in old Dwarven with a great deal of feeling in it, and his shield came up.

The claws came first — two of them, both turned on the enameled hammer and anvil, both shrieking off. Then the longsword came down twice, and the plate held and the man inside it did not, and Thane saw his cousin rock back a full step.

"*Caleth—!*"

The half-elf had the wand out already and was moving toward him at that terrible slowed pace, hand outstretched.

Thane got there first. He put his own hand into Caleth's before the man had finished reaching, and the world folded around him.

Cold. A half-second of nowhere.

And then his boots came down on stone, and there was a demon eight feet in front of him.

Something in Thane Stonevein came loose that had been wound tight since the derakni came over the rocks.

*There it is.*

He had spent the entire day at the wrong distance. He had shot a bow he despised at a thing in the sky and hit it twice out of six, and he had watched a demon he could not reach take his cousin's shield apart, and every single thing he was actually good at had been sixty feet away from where it was needed.

Not any more.

He went in.

Mother's blade came out into his right hand on the way and he did not reach for his father's knife at all. His left hand went to the fourth sheath on his belt — the new one, the one he had cut and stitched himself in the Purity Forge with Korroc watching sideways and pretending not to — and his fingers went through the cross-grip and closed, and the punch dagger came up out of the leather with the blade standing forward between his knuckles like something the hand had grown.

The shachath was still committed to Korroc's shield.

Thane came in under its wing on the blind side and put mother's blade into the seam where the wing root met the back, and while it was screaming about that he stepped straight through into its chest — no arc, no wind-up, nothing to see coming — and drove his whole weight forward from the hips.

The punch dagger went in to the grip.

He felt it hit something that was not muscle, and he twisted his fist, and the demon came off the ground.

*That,* he thought, with a savage and entirely uncomplicated joy, *is what it's for.*

Nageru arrived.

He came across the whole fifty-foot pit in a single step that Thane's eyes did not accept — a step that started on one side and finished on the other, with no ground crossed in between — and he came down on the shachath's far side with his fists already burning gold, and the first punch broke something in it that made a noise.

And Korroc came up off his shield.

His cousin reached down into the rock of the chasm floor a second time, and pulled, and hit the thing with a warhammer that had thirty feet of mountain behind it.

The shachath was surrounded by three of them and it knew it.

So it left.

---

It went out of the world and came back on the far side of the cavern, forty feet away, standing directly behind Caleth Aranor.

Thane's stomach went. Caleth was slowed. Caleth was still moving like a man underwater, alone on the entrance side of a fifty-foot pit, with his back to a demon and no one within reach of him.

It attacked before it had finished arriving.

And Caleth did not do what a slowed man does.

Thane watched it from across the cavern and he would take it out and look at it a hundred times afterward, because it was the best thing he had ever seen the half-elf do, and it was done at the worst moment of his day.

Caleth did not turn to face it. He did not block. He simply gave it his shoulder and *moved* — a small drifting side-step that had nothing hurried in it, the whole body going out of the line of the attack by about four inches. Three of the strikes went through the space he had been occupying. The fourth caught him across the ribs and he took it and did not stop moving, and when the drift finished he was standing outside the demon's reach with the glaive already coming up.

And Radiance lit.

Thane had carried that weapon out of a ritual chamber wrapped in a cloth. He had held it once in the underdark and felt it acknowledge him, faint, like a hearth two rooms away. He had watched it choose the man now holding it, and had watched it flare on a tower top when a red banner came down the side of Citadel Drezen.

He had never seen it do this.

The white went up the whole length of the leaf-blade at once and did not stop at the edge. It came off the steel into the air, and it filled that end of a cavern lit by molten rock and *beat the lava*, and every shadow in the room reversed itself.

Caleth Aranor said something Thane could not hear.

Then he stepped in and put the glaive through the shachath's chest.

The demon screamed with both its faces.

Thane heard it — the contemptuous one and the beautiful one, together, two different sounds coming out of one throat — and then the light went into it the way water goes into sand, and there was nothing left in the cavern but four crusaders, a priest, and the noise of the fire.

---

Korroc was already turning before it finished falling.

He went to Jesker Helton at a run in full plate, and Thane and Nageru got there a half-second behind him, and the three of them came down around a man lying on hot stone.

Korroc put two fingers up and murmured something short, and looked at him, and looked for a long moment.

Then he sat back on his heels.

"He's been touched by a demon, sure enough." He said it plainly and immediately, which Thane noted and was grateful for, because his cousin had a habit of getting to the reassurance first and this time he did not. "The evil coming off him — that's the possession. It's the mark of the thing that was in him. It isn't him."

"You're certain," said Nageru.

"I'm certain."

Thane looked down at the priest.

The man was in a bad way. Not wounded, particularly — the tail-strike had cracked something and the fall had done the rest — but the heat had been working on him for five days, and Thane could see the shape of that in his lips and his eyes and the way his breathing came in short and wrong.

Thane stood up and looked around the cavern.

And then frowned, because something was not right, and he could not immediately say what.

He took two steps toward the smoky crystal.

*There.*

The heat stopped.

It was not gradual. There was a line, and on one side of it the air was a furnace, and on the other side of it — the area close to the crystal slab — it was merely very hot. He put his hand out and moved it back and forth across the boundary twice, and felt it go from unbearable to survivable and back.

He did not know what did it. He did not need to.

"Over here," he said.

He went back, got Jesker Helton under the arms, and hauled him bodily across the ledge and over the line and set him down inside it.

The priest's whole body changed. Thane felt it happen in his hands — the awful rigid brace of a man being cooked coming out of him all at once, the shoulders going down.

Korroc came in after them and knelt down in front of him.

He did not say anything for a moment. He put one broad hand on the man's shoulder and left it there.

"Listen to me," he said. "There are spells of atonement that can help you, friend. Just because a demon has warped your mind doesn't mean you're beyond redemption."

Jesker Helton looked at him.

"Are you going to be all right?"

The priest was shaking. He looked like a man who had been taken apart and put back together by somebody working from a description. He did not say anything.

Then he nodded.

Thane stood three feet away with his hands at his sides and watched his cousin do that, and it went into him sideways and much further down than he had any defense prepared for.

*Twenty-one days.*

He had come back into himself in a black room with two clean blades in his hands, and he had wiped them both, and everybody had let him. That had been the arrangement, and it had been a good arrangement, and he had built it himself out of silence in about four seconds flat, because the alternative was to stand in front of three men and say *it was in me and I could see out.*

And here was a man he had never met, five days ridden and half-cooked and covered in his own sick, being told by a stranger in gold plate that there were spells for it. That it was not him. That he was not beyond anything.

Nobody had said that to Thane Stonevein.

He was aware, standing there, of exactly how unjust that was — because nobody had said it to him for the excellent reason that he had never told anybody there was anything to say it about. He had made himself unaskable. He had done it deliberately and he had done it well, and he was extremely good at it, and it had worked perfectly.

*Say something,* he thought.

He did not.

He was three feet from his cousin, in a cavern, with two other men who had watched a demon come out of his mouth and had not once brought it up.

*You could just say it.*

And Thane Stonevein — who had gone through a locked door in a fortress at midnight and stood alone in a dry riverbed at the wrong end of the world and put his fist through a demon's chest an hour ago — found that he could not open that one, and that his mother's grammar had nothing at all to say about it.

*Later,* he told himself.

He knew exactly what kind of word that was. He had used it three weeks ago at a row of cages, and he had gone back for those, in the end.

He let it stand.

---

Nageru had gone to the crystal.

He was standing over the slab with his hands loose, looking down through the smoke of it at the shape inside.

"Delamere," he said.

Thane came and stood beside him. Up close you could see more: a woman, on her back, hands crossed on her breast. A shape at the throat that might have been a torc. A band on one finger. Something laid along her side that had the length and taper of a bow.

He looked at it the way he looked at everything, and hated himself a little for it, and said it anyway because saying it was the honest thing.

"She's wearing magic items."

Nageru nodded, unsurprised. "Yes."

"There's a bow in there with her."

"Yes." Nageru did not look up. "I do not think we touch the body."

Thane frowned.

He thought about a woman who had taught that any village of more than fifty-three people was a blight, and who had been buried in a tomb so large her followers had been queuing for it for centuries, and about a young scholar in Drezen with an interest in the old ways who had read her book to pieces and written a note in the back of it in his own careful hand.

He thought about how badly the crusade needed a bow with a working in it.

"Say the rest of it," he said.

"We are not followers of Erastil." Nageru's voice did not change. "It is not our tomb, and she is not our dead, and there is no one here to ask. Somebody may come who has the right to decide it. It should be them."

Korroc's voice came from behind them. "I agree."

Thane stood there a second.

Three weeks ago he had been in a room where he had known he was right and had been overruled by a commander with a stake in it, and he had said *you're the commander* in a voice that had fooled nobody, and he had carried that around for twenty-one days like a stone in a boot.

This was not that. He turned it over and confirmed it was not that. This was a ruling given for reasons, by a man with no interest in the outcome, in a room where nobody had rank on anybody.

"Aye," he said. "All right."

He took a last look at the shape in the crystal.

*Nobody's going to come,* he thought. *Not for years. Maybe never.*

He found he did not mind that as much as he had expected to, and left it where it lay.

---

They all turned and looked across the cavern at Caleth, who was still on the other side of the pit and still moving like a man in deep water and had, Thane noticed, sat down.

He brought them back one at a time with the wand. Crossing himself, touching one of them and both going back. It took a while. When it was Thane's turn the world folded and unfolded and he came out standing next to a half-elf who looked gray around the eyes and was pretending not to be.

"You all right?"

"It's wearing off." Caleth flexed one hand and watched it obey him about a half-beat late. "Slowly. Which is the joke."

They got Jesker Helton up between them. Caleth put a working over the man that soaked him head to foot in cold water, and the priest gasped and shuddered and came a little further back into his own face than he had been all afternoon.

Then they went out through the tunnel and into the bottom of the chasm, and stood in the red light at the foot of two hundred feet of cliff.

Nageru looked up. Then he stepped, and was gone, and Thane's eye caught him again a half-second later standing on the rim two hundred feet overhead, looking down.

"Every time," Thane said.

Caleth grinned at him — actually grinned, for the first time that day — and reached down into the gold, and took hold of the four of them, and the world came apart and put itself together.

They were on the rim.

The sky had gone the color of a bruise while they were down there. West, over the Wound, the last of it was going out.

---

They collected the horses out of the rocks and the wagons out of the hollow, and Thane checked the traces twice because he did not trust anything he had not personally seen for the last three hours.

"I want to be further away from this area before we rest," Caleth said.

Nobody argued.

They went an hour east — a slow hour, with a hurt man in the wagon and the ground bad — and found another spill of rock and put the wagons in the lee of it and made the sort of camp you make when nobody intends to sleep.

They ate. They rested an hour, the four of them going down into that odd shallow stillness they had learned to fall into since the wardstone, the one that gave you back a piece of yourself if you were willing to hold still for it. Korroc spent his hour on Jesker Helton — working the cracked ribs, then the heat, then the exhaustion, laying his hands on and murmuring and going back and doing it again — and by the end of it the priest was asleep, properly asleep, for what Thane suspected was the first time in five days.

Then Caleth stood up, walked over to Nageru, and cast something on him.

Thane watched the aasimar's skin take on a faint roughness, like bark in the firelight.

Nageru looked down at his own forearm. Then at Caleth.

"We're about to be a target," Caleth said.

He drew a wand.

Thane got up off the wagon step. "Now what are you doing?"

"The horses can't see. The ground's bad, we've a hurt man, and I am not making that camp until dawn six hours from a lava chasm." Caleth touched the wand to the leaf of the glaive and spoke.

Light bloomed off the point of Radiance.

Not the holy white. This was daylight — flat, ordinary, midsummer-noon daylight, standing in a globe over the half-elf's head and throwing hard black shadows out across fifty feet of the Worldwound in every direction.

Thane looked at it for a long moment.

Every instinct he had, every hour of a life spent being nobody in particular in the dark, stood up on its hind legs and objected.

"That'll be visible for six miles," he said.

"Yes."

"Everything out there will see us."

"Yes."

"And know exactly where we are, and how many, and which of us is carrying the light."

"Yes," Caleth said. "And we'll see them coming, and we're four Knights of the Fifth Crusade with a hurt priest, and I would rather be attacked in the light than ambushed in the dark. Would you like to take a vote on it?"

Thane thought about it honestly, which took perhaps four seconds.

"No," he said. "You're right."

Caleth looked genuinely thrown by that, which Thane enjoyed more than he let on.

---

They went on into the night.

Caleth and Nageru walked out in front, on foot, ahead of Caleth's riderless horse, with the daylight standing over them and going with them across broken ground — a slow bright island moving east through the middle of a country that had nothing else in it.

Thane drove the wagon behind them.

Korroc rode in the bed of it with his back against the boards and his hammer across his knees, watching over Jesker Helton, who slept.

It was, Thane thought, precisely the arrangement his aunt had described. *Korroc will follow because somebody has to make certain you come back.* Except that Korroc was not following him tonight; Korroc was sitting up in the dark with a stranger, because that was the shape the man came in.

He drove and watched the light ahead of him and thought about twenty feet.

Not the tunnel, this time. The distance. A demon in the sky and a bow he hated. Fifty feet of pit with a man he could not reach on the other side of it. His whole day had been about being the wrong distance from things — and it had ended with his fist against a demon's ribs, which was the only place he had ever been any good.

And there was one distance left, and it was three feet across a cavern floor, and he had not crossed it.

Ahead of him, Nageru said something to Caleth.

Thane did not catch it. He caught the shape of it — the aasimar's voice going out low and even under the noise of the wheels — and then Caleth said something back that had a question in it, and Nageru answered.

"'There is always another step.'"

The words came back to Thane clearly on the night air.

"Is that yours?"

"No," said Nageru. "It was written to me. I have been thinking about it since the gate."

Thane sat on the wagon seat with the reins in his hands and let that go past him.

He did not know where it had come from. He did not ask, then or later. It had not been said to him and he had no business in it, and he had a rule about that.

But he had it now, and it would not go.

*There is always another step.*

His father had taken about four thousand of them, an inch at a time, with a stolen tool, in the dark, in the belly of a place he had helped build, for better than a year — and had never once, on any single one of those nights, known whether the next one would be the last.

And had gone out the other end into a dry riverbed and disappeared into a country that had not been mapped since.

And a woman in Nerosyan had spent forty years saying *disappeared* instead of *died*, and had been right the entire time, and did not know it yet.

*I'll write to her,* Thane thought. *When we're back. Somebody has to tell her, and it had better not be Aravashnial with the ledgers, and it had better not be Korroc on his own.*

He turned that over and found it was the first plan he had made all day that had nothing in it about a door.

Then, because his hands wanted something to do and because the road was straight and because there was nobody to see, he shifted the reins into his left hand, and took out the rag.

He had already done this. He had done it in the cavern, on the ledge, with the shachath's smoke not fully gone out of the air — mother's blade first, then his father's knife, then the punch dagger, which was the first thing that weapon had ever been wiped of and which he had felt in his hands as a small and private occasion.

He did it again anyway.

Mother's blade came out. He ran the cloth down its whole length, both faces, the way he had been taught before he could shave, and it came away perfectly clean, because there had been nothing on it the first time and there was nothing on it now.

*No Stonevein has ever sheathed a wet blade.*

He put it away. He took out his father's knife and did the same. Then the punch dagger.

Nobody saw him do it. That was rather the point.

Somewhere out east, past the light, past a hundred miles of ruined country, there was a fortress with his father's stone in it and a mile and a half of dry riverbed nobody had opened yet.

The wagon went on toward it through the dark, behind a light that anything could see.
