# He Wouldn't Have to Ask

*Interlude Two — The Work After Victory · The Purity Forge*

---

Five days after the Sword of Valor rose over Drezen, Irabeth Tirabade admitted that she had made a mistake.

Korroc knew because she said so, out loud, in front of nine people. That by itself was enough to make him put down what he was holding and look up.

They were gathered round the long table that had become the nearest thing Drezen had to a council chamber. The room had belonged to somebody important once. Korroc knew that the way he knew most things about buildings — it was too large, the chairs had been carved by a man paid by the hour, and the fireplace was wide enough to roast an ox in a fortress that had not yet turned up enough dry wood to boil a kettle properly. Somebody had built that hearth to be looked at. Korroc had been finding a great deal of that in this citadel and had stopped being surprised by it.

Maps covered most of the table. Lists covered the rest.

Lists of soldiers. Lists of wounded. Lists of prisoners. Lists of stores. Lists of buildings that could still be used, buildings that wanted repair, wells that wanted clearing, walls that wanted reinforcing, doors that wanted replacing, roofs that leaked, and roofs that had given up being roofs some while ago and were now simply weather with opinions.

Irabeth stood at the head of it with both hands braced flat on the wood.

"I made a mistake."

Nobody said anything.

Korroc looked at Thane. Thane looked back. Neither of them moved a muscle in his face.

Irabeth's eyes narrowed anyway. "I can see the two of you thinking."

"We've said nothing," Thane pointed out.

"You're dwarves. You don't need to."

Anevia covered a smile with one hand and did not do it especially well.

Irabeth straightened up. "I thought taking Drezen would be the difficult part."

"It was reasonably difficult," Caleth said.

"Thank you, Caleth."

"You're welcome."

"The problem," Irabeth went on, "is that an army is an army. A citadel is—" she looked down at the mountain of paper in front of her, and something in the set of her shoulders shifted "—apparently everything."

Aron made a low sound that in a less careful man would have been a laugh.

Irabeth pointed at him without looking. "You are about to become part of the problem."

The sound stopped.

Korroc leaned back in a chair built for a taller man and folded his arms. Now this might be worth staying for.

Irabeth came round the table.

"We have better than three hundred people in these walls and more coming up the road every week. Soldiers need commanders. Civilians need protecting. The walls need repair. Supplies need counting. Patrols need setting. Prisoners need watching. People need somewhere to pray, somewhere to sleep, somewhere to eat, and preferably somewhere to do all three without the roof coming in on them."

"An ambitious standard," Horgus observed.

Irabeth ignored him with the ease of long practice.

"I cannot command the army, run the city, hold the citadel, walk every wall, count every sack of flour, chase every rumour, and personally settle every argument about which god gets which abandoned chapel."

Sosiel raised one hand about halfway.

"There have been several."

"I know."

"One became quite passionate."

"I know."

"The followers of Cayden Cailean—"

"Sosiel."

He stopped. "Yes?"

"You're High Priest."

There was a pause of a particular quality, the kind Korroc associated with a hammer coming down somewhere out of sight.

Sosiel blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"High Priest of Drezen. Not of one faith. Of all of them."

The young man's face went from surprise into open alarm at a speed Korroc found genuinely admirable.

Irabeth spoke over the objection before it could get its boots on. "You are already doing the work. I am giving it a name, that is all. Paradise Hill has the old cathedral. Restore it. Make it fit for anyone who needs somewhere to worship, provided their god is not actively trying to eat the rest of us."

Sosiel considered that for a moment with the air of a man examining a contract.

"That is a remarkably broad theological standard."

"It's the one I've got."

His mouth twitched. "Very well."

Korroc, who had spent an hour on his knees in another god's chapel that same morning and had come out of it feeling better than he had in a week, thought that was about the most sensible thing he had heard said in a council room in a year.

Irabeth had already turned. "Aron."

Aron sighed through his nose. "I knew laughing was a mistake."

"Warden."

That took the slouch out of him.

Irabeth put two fingers on the map of the citadel. "You know ground. You know fortification. You have spent five days telling anyone who will hold still which of these walls are going to fall down."

"Because several of them are."

"Then stop telling me and go and fix them."

Aron looked at the map for a long moment, and Korroc watched the pieces go together behind his eyes: captain of the guard on one side, engineer on the other, and the whole reclaimed shell of Drezen sitting in the middle wanting both. It was a strange pairing to anybody who had not watched the man read a battlefield for a year. It was no pairing at all to anybody who had.

At last he nodded. "Warden."

"Warden," Irabeth confirmed, and it was done.

She turned again. "Horgus."

"No."

"You don't yet know what I am going to say."

"I have known you long enough to recognise that tone."

"Treasurer."

Horgus Gwerm stared at her. Then at the lists. Then at the stacked ledgers. Then back at her.

"Absolutely not."

"Good. You start this afternoon."

"This city," said Horgus, with the wounded precision of a man being asked to build a cathedral out of soup, "has no treasury."

"It has you."

"That is not remotely the same thing."

"It has warehouses."

"Half empty."

"Caravans."

"Late."

"Three hundred people."

"Who consume food at a rate I would describe as alarming."

Irabeth smiled. "There. You're already doing it."

Horgus stared at her for several seconds, and Korroc watched a man discover in real time that he had been outflanked by his own competence.

Then he reached out and gathered three ledgers off the table and tucked them under one arm.

"I want authority over requisitions."

"You have it."

"And no officer removes so much as a barrel without a written record."

"Agreed."

"And if one more paladin tells me that the gods will provide—"

"I will personally send him to you."

Horgus adjusted the ledgers. "Then perhaps civilisation has a chance."

Anevia laughed out loud.

Irabeth looked at her wife.

Anevia lifted both hands off the table. "Don't look at me. I volunteered."

That part Korroc already knew, and he had the bruised dignity to prove it. Anevia had been walking out into Drezen almost from the hour the gates shut behind the last of the fleeing cultists. Some days it was crusader kit. Some days a labourer's coat and a labourer's stoop. Once Korroc had held a conversation with a grey-haired washerwoman for the better part of a minute — a good conversation, he had thought, about the state of the wells — before something in the way she said *aye* landed wrong and he had turned round and looked properly and found her grinning at him under the scarf.

He had not lived it down. He did not expect to.

"Spymaster," Irabeth said.

Anevia nodded. "Somebody needs to know what's moving through those streets before it arrives at this table. Cultists. Demon-work. Old loyalties, new grudges, the men who were here before we came and never left." She shrugged. "And people talk when they're sure nobody important is listening."

Horgus sniffed. "That would explain several conversations I have had recently."

Anevia smiled at him with great sweetness.

Korroc decided not to ask.

Irabeth's eyes went down the table to Aravashnial.

The elf had installed himself at the far end with one hand resting on his staff, three books open in front of him and a drift of notes that seemed to have got larger since Korroc last looked at it, which Korroc suspected was not entirely a trick of the light.

"You have already established yourself in the eastern tower."

"It has several structural advantages."

"You mean you claimed it."

"I *evaluated* it."

"Riftwarden."

Aravashnial went still.

Only for a moment — but Korroc had been watching that elf across a year of ruins and rifts and had seen him hold a conversation with vrocks coming through a hole in the world without so much as adjusting his cuffs. He noticed the stillness the way he would have noticed a bell stopping.

Then Aravashnial inclined his head. "Commander."

"The Riftwardens in Kenabres are effectively gone," Irabeth said. "If Drezen is going to stand at the edge of the Worldwound, I want one person answerable for its magical defences. Wards. Teleportation. Planar breaches. Anything else I do not understand and would rather not learn about by having it detonate."

"A sensible policy."

"And rebuild the Riftwardens."

Something crossed the elf's face then. It was small, and it was gone quickly, and Korroc very nearly missed it.

He knew enough to guess at the shape of it. The Blackwing Library had gone into the ground with the rest of Kenabres's heart, and most of the order with it, and whatever was left of them was scattered or wounded or dead. Korroc had watched this same elf go down on his knees in the wreck of that library and stay there.

Aravashnial looked at the map of Drezen for a while — a broken city, in a poisoned country, a very long way from anywhere safe.

Then he nodded. "I will."

Finally Irabeth turned to the four of them.

Caleth straightened. Nageru waited. Thane's thumbs stayed hooked in his belt. Korroc kept his arms folded.

"And before any of you ask," Irabeth said, "no."

Thane frowned. "We haven't asked anything."

"Again. Dwarves."

Korroc grunted.

She pointed down the length of them. "You are not getting offices."

Caleth raised an eyebrow. "Was that under consideration?"

"For about six minutes."

"What happened?"

"I imagined Thane attending administrative meetings."

Thane looked faintly appalled, which for Thane was roughly a scream.

"I imagined Korroc responsible for approving requisitions."

"Depends what they're requisitioning," Korroc said.

"I imagined Nageru settling a dispute between two merchants."

Nageru considered this seriously, as he considered everything. "I could settle it."

"That is what worries me."

Then she looked at Caleth. Caleth waited.

Irabeth shook her head. "No."

"I haven't said anything."

"I know."

Anevia was laughing openly now.

Irabeth's face softened. Only a little; she rationed it.

"I need the four of you able to move. Today that may mean clearing rubble. Tomorrow it may mean riding fifty miles into the Worldwound. The day after it may mean killing something none of us knew existed until breakfast."

"That does seem to happen," Caleth admitted.

"I am not tying any of you to a desk."

Korroc looked round the table.

At Aron, already pulling a wall survey toward himself. At Sosiel, still visibly recalculating his entire life. At Horgus with three ledgers under his arm and something in his face that Korroc had never once seen there. At Anevia, who was about to become four different people before supper. At Aravashnial, looking at a map of a city he intended to make into a stronghold for an order that had almost ceased to exist.

Then at Irabeth Tirabade, who had begun the morning by telling nine people she had been wrong.

A city had taken shape in the space between one sentence and the next.

Not much of one. Broken walls, mud to the ankle, a prison full of people who had been on the other side a fortnight ago, three hundred exhausted crusaders and about four times as much work as there were hands to do it.

But there was a Warden now. A Treasurer. A High Priest. A Spymaster. A Riftwarden.

And at the head of it, whether she had asked for the title or not, Irabeth.

Korroc nodded once. "Aye."

She looked at him. "Aye what?"

"Aye, Commander."

For once, she did not correct him.

---

Joran Vhane was brought before the council two mornings later.

He came in without chains. Korroc noticed that first, and was surprised by how much it mattered to him.

There were guards to either side, naturally. Aron had no intention of being a fool about it. But the old dwarf's wrists were free, and he walked in on his own legs, and that was not nothing.

He looked smaller above ground.

Korroc had noticed it before and had not worked out why until now. Down beside the forge, in the heat and the iron and the red light, Joran *fit* — he was the right size for that room, the way a tool is the right size for the hand that has worn a shine into it. Put him in a stone hall with daylight coming in through tall windows and a table of officers in front of him, and he seemed to have been left out in the weather.

His beard had been washed. That helped. Not much.

Irabeth sat at the head of the table with Aron beside her. Sosiel and Aravashnial had the opposite side along with two captains Korroc knew by sight and not by name.

The four of them had been invited to remain. Korroc suspected this was chiefly because he would have remained regardless and Irabeth had decided to save them both the argument.

Joran looked at him once on the way in.

Korroc nodded.

Joran looked away.

Irabeth did not waste any time.

"Joran Vhane."

"Aye."

"You understand why you are here."

"Aye."

"You served the force occupying Drezen."

"Aye."

"You worked the forge beneath this citadel."

"Aye."

"You aided Staunton Vhane."

Joran's jaw worked. "He was my brother."

"That was not my question."

The pause went on a long time. Korroc counted his own breathing through it.

"Aye."

Irabeth nodded once, and wrote nothing, and moved on.

---

It went that way most of the morning.

Numbers. Companies. Storehouses. Who had commanded what after Aponavicius marched south. How many cultists had stayed. How supplies came into Drezen and by which roads. Which commanders had reported to Staunton and which had answered somewhere else entirely.

Aron did most of the detailed work, and Korroc came to enjoy watching him do it the way he enjoyed watching any craftsman.

Where the patrols changed over. Which doors had been kept barred against Drezen's own soldiers. Which tunnels moved supplies. Which stretches of wall had gone unrepaired because Aponavicius had written them off as expendable, and how long ago, and who had told him so.

Joran answered when he knew and said he did not when he did not, and did not decorate either.

Korroc found himself watching Irabeth more than Joran.

She never asked the same question twice.

Aron did. Not straight away — that would have been obvious and Aron Kir had never been obvious in his life. Ten minutes later. Half an hour later. From a different corner of the map, in different words, with the pieces in a different order.

The words changed every time.

The answers had better not.

Joran's did not.

Eventually Irabeth sat back in her chair.

"Why were you here?"

Joran frowned. "I've answered that."

"You have told us what you did. I asked why you were here."

That landed somewhere different. Korroc saw it go in.

Joran's eyes dropped to the table. "Staunton asked me to come."

"From where?"

"Does it matter?"

"At the moment, everything matters."

Joran rubbed one thumb along the side of the other hand, an old unconscious motion, a man checking a callus that had been there forty years.

"My brother needed a smith."

"There were smiths in Drezen."

"Not for that forge."

Aravashnial's head came up.

Joran noticed it. "The forge doesn't accept everyone."

"*Accept,*" Aravashnial repeated.

"Aye."

"Explain that."

Joran shifted in his chair. "Others tried. Some couldn't make it work. Some—" He stopped.

"Some?" said Aron.

"Didn't survive."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Korroc thought about four days at a rail watching an elf and a half-elf and this dwarf argue over an Abyssal book, and about how casually all three of them had leaned over that pit.

"Staunton sent for me," Joran went on. "When I got here, they tested me."

"How?" said Irabeth.

"A holy sword."

Korroc's arms tightened across his chest before he had decided anything about it.

Joran saw it. He did not look away. "Not one I knew. Taken off a crusader somewhere. They put it in front of me and told me to work it."

"Using the forge," Caleth said.

"Aye."

"And you succeeded."

"Aye."

"Why?"

Joran's mouth twisted. "If I knew *that,* lad, I'd have charged them more."

Nobody laughed. Korroc noted that Joran had not expected anybody to.

He looked back to Irabeth. "I passed their test. So I stayed."

Irabeth let the silence sit there until it had been thoroughly heard.

Then Aravashnial said, "The elixirs."

And Joran's face changed.

Korroc caught it at once, because he had been waiting all morning for something to get through the flat and this did. It was not fear. It was older than fear and further down.

Irabeth had seen it too. "What do you know about them?"

Joran said nothing.

"Joran."

The old dwarf swallowed. "Eustoyriax brought them."

Caleth's eyes sharpened. "The demon from below."

"Aye. Came to Drezen bearing gifts. Said they were from the Ivory Sanctum."

Aravashnial's hand stopped over his notes. "Ivory Sanctum."

"Aye."

"Where is it?"

"I don't know."

"Who holds it?"

Joran hesitated. "Vang."

Something went out of the room.

"Xanthir Vang," said Aravashnial, and every trace of warmth had left his voice.

"Aye."

Korroc looked at the elf. His face had barely moved. His grip on the table edge had gone white to the knuckle.

Irabeth went on. "What were the gifts?"

"Five vials."

"Containing?"

"I don't know."

"You worked for them."

"I worked metal."

"You saw Staunton use one."

Joran looked at her for a long moment. Then he sat slowly back in the chair.

"Aye."

That one word came out with the whole morning's weight behind it.

"They said the draught could wake something in a person. Power that was already there. Hidden." A pause. "My brother volunteered."

Nobody interrupted.

Joran's gaze had gone somewhere well past the table and the windows and the walls.

"Aponavicius didn't trust them. Didn't trust Eustoyriax, didn't trust Vang, didn't trust anything she hadn't put a knife into herself." His mouth moved without humour. "Staunton wanted to prove himself."

Korroc had grown up with enough brothers about the place to hear everything that was folded inside that sentence.

"He drank it."

Joran's hands closed on nothing.

"For three days I thought he was dying."

The council room went out of the old dwarf's eyes entirely.

"He burned with fever. Couldn't keep water down. His muscles locked up so hard I thought bones would go. There were hours he'd scream until the voice went out of him, and then keep on trying after there was no sound left to make."

He stopped.

Sosiel looked at the table.

"I tried spells." Joran's mouth tightened. "Back when Droskar still answered me."

Korroc said nothing at all. It cost him something.

"Nothing touched it. Not healing, not restoration, nothing. Whatever was happening to him wasn't a sickness."

"And after three days?" Irabeth asked, and she asked it quietly.

Joran looked up. "He woke."

"Recovered?"

"No." The answer came too fast, and he shook his head as though to take the speed off it. "He *woke.*"

And there it was — a brother remembering a brother. Not the traitor. Not the enemy commander. Not the man from their fathers' table with seventy-five years of blood behind him.

Just Staunton, on a cot, coming back.

"He was stronger after. Faster. Things that ought to have hurt him didn't. He could do things I'd never seen him do."

Joran frowned down at his own hands, which had lately stopped doing a thing they used to do.

"It was like somebody had opened a door inside him that had always been there and he'd never known about it."

Korroc glanced at Caleth. Caleth was already looking at Nageru. Neither of them said one word, and Korroc understood exactly why, because he had been standing in the wardstone's fire himself and knew precisely what that description was describing.

Irabeth did not ask for a name for it. Korroc was grateful. Joran did not have one to give, and the ones in that room who did were not going to hand it over across a prisoner's testimony.

"What happened to the other four?" Aron asked.

Joran breathed out through his nose. "They tested them."

"On whom?"

"A dragon."

Aron's quill stopped. "What kind?"

"I don't remember."

"You don't remember a dragon."

"I was interested in whether it died. Not its family history."

Thane coughed into his fist. Korroc strongly suspected what it was covering.

"A xacarba servant took another," Joran said.

"And?"

"Died."

"How?"

"Badly."

Aron waited, quill up, saying nothing, which Korroc had learned was the most effective thing that man did.

Joran stared at him. "You want the details?"

"I want to know what we are dealing with."

Joran looked at the tabletop. "The dragon tore itself open."

The room went very quiet.

"The xacarba lasted longer."

Nobody asked.

After a while Aron said, "The third?"

"Soltengrebbe."

Korroc's eyes narrowed. "The chimera."

"Aye."

Korroc remembered ice in his lungs on a channel bank in the Ahari. He remembered three heads and the cold going into him where no ward would hold, and severing the thread to his own cousin to stay upright, and the damned thing refusing and refusing and refusing to die.

*Enhanced. Fortified past its nature. In the same way the stone affected you.*

"She survived," Joran said.

Nageru's head tilted a fraction. "She."

Joran shrugged. "Chimera."

"You said *she.*"

"Because she was."

Nageru accepted that with a small nod, and Korroc thought — not for the first time — that the aasimar collected things nobody else in the room would have bothered to pick up.

"And the fifth vial?" Irabeth asked.

Joran took a long breath.

"Aponavicius."

There it was.

Korroc felt the whole room arrive at the size of it in the same half-second.

"She drank it?" said Caleth.

"After Soltengrebbe lived through hers."

"And survived it herself."

Joran looked at him. "Aye."

Nobody wrote anything for several seconds. Then every quill in the room moved at once.

Korroc watched Aravashnial write *Ivory Sanctum* and draw a line under it. Then draw another.

---

Irabeth moved on. "Arueshalae."

Joran blinked. "What?"

"The succubus."

Recognition came in. "Aye."

"Who is she?"

"A succubus."

Aron gave him a look. "Thank you."

Joran ignored him entirely. "Some sort of heretic."

"Heretic to whom?" Sosiel asked.

"The Abyss."

That took hold of everyone at the table at once, and Korroc watched Sosiel Vaenic in particular go very thoughtful indeed.

Joran shrugged at all of them. "That's what they called her."

"Why was she imprisoned?" said Irabeth.

"Aponavicius ordered it. She was to be held till a place was ready for her in the Rasping Rifts."

Aravashnial's head came up sharply. "An oubliette?"

"Aye."

Korroc did not know what a Rasping Rift was. He knew from the elf's face that he did not want it explained over breakfast.

"Why there?" Caleth asked.

"Didn't ask."

"How did she get out?"

Joran snorted — the first genuinely unguarded sound he had made all morning. "If I knew that, my brother would have slept better."

"Explain."

Joran rubbed both hands down his face. "He was terrified after she vanished. Drezen was his. She went out of his prison. He knew what Aponavicius would do when she found out."

"Did she find out?" Irabeth asked.

"Not before she marched."

"And Staunton?"

"Sent people after her. Everywhere. Out into the Wounded Lands. Kept saying there was still a chance, because she'd lost some of what she had."

"What had she lost?" Aravashnial asked.

"Teleporting. And going — not solid."

"Ethereal."

"Aye. That."

Caleth frowned. "He wanted her back before Aponavicius learned she had ever gone."

"Aye." Joran leaned back. "Didn't work out."

"No," said Irabeth. "It didn't."

---

The questioning went on another hour, and by the end of it Joran Vhane looked like a man who had been hollowed out with a spoon and the shell left sitting in the chair.

Irabeth closed the ledger in front of her. "That will do."

Joran nodded. A guard stepped forward.

Then Joran said, "Commander."

Irabeth paused.

He looked, instantly, like a man who wished he had let it go.

"What is it?"

Joran's fingers closed on something inside his coat, and Korroc knew what it was before the worn corner came into view, because he had watched Thane nearly set fire to it in a room with Staunton cooling on the flagstones.

The prayer book. Droskar's.

Joran did not take it out.

"Would I be permitted—"

He stopped. Irabeth waited, and did him the courtesy of waiting without expression.

"—to visit the chapel."

Sosiel's face moved. Irabeth's did not.

"Which chapel?"

Joran looked across the room at Korroc.

"The Father's."

Every eye in the chamber found Korroc at once. He grunted, which committed him to nothing and was the best he could manage on short notice.

Irabeth looked at the dwarf in front of her. Then at Korroc. Then back.

"Only under escort."

Joran nodded fast.

"Korroc, or one of the others, stays with you the whole time."

"Aye."

"If you are asked to leave, you leave."

"Aye."

"If you attempt to escape—"

"I know."

Irabeth's eyes went hard. "I prefer to finish my own sentences."

Joran lowered his head. "Aye, Commander."

She waved the guards back.

Korroc stood up. "So am I escorting him, or are you?"

The guards looked at Irabeth. Irabeth looked tired.

"You."

Nageru rose beside him without being asked. "I will come."

Joran glanced at the aasimar — at the man who had offered him a door in the middle of a fight and had then broken his brother's ribs with a fist.

Nageru gave him a small nod.

That settled it.

---

Joran stopped outside the chapel door.

Not slowed. Stopped.

Korroc was three paces in before he noticed and had to turn round. The old dwarf stood in the corridor with one hand inside his coat, and Nageru waited beside him, unhurried, looking at nothing in particular.

The chapel of Torag was cleaner now. Not clean; that would take weeks and a mason. But clean enough that the stone had begun to remember what it was meant to look like.

The statue had been scrubbed down to grey. The benches were back in rough rows. Broken pieces had been gathered along the north wall and sorted by size, because Korroc could not help himself. A new cloth hung across the worst of the crack behind the altar until somebody could cut proper stone for it.

Korroc looked from the room to the dwarf in the doorway.

"Well?"

"I don't know."

"Know what?"

"If I should go in."

Korroc looked around the chapel and back. "Door works."

Joran gave him a flat sour look. "That isn't what I meant."

"I know."

Joran's hand came out of his coat and the book came with it.

Old. Worn to softness. Dark leather gone shiny at the corners from decades of a thumb finding the same place. Droskar's mark pressed into the cover — near-kin to Torag's hammer and anvil and wrong in every proportion that mattered, the whole design bent toward *toil* where the Father's bent toward *craft.*

Joran looked at it in his own hand.

"He doesn't answer."

Korroc said nothing.

"Hasn't for a while now."

Still nothing. Korroc had learned that much, at least, in three weeks of getting it wrong.

"I keep carrying it."

"Aye."

"I don't know why."

Korroc shrugged. "You've carried it a long while."

Joran looked up at him. "That's all?"

"What do you want me to say?"

"That I should burn it."

"No."

"Throw it away."

"No."

"That Torag won't have me through that door while I'm holding it."

Korroc's brows came down hard. "Don't put words in the Father's mouth."

Joran actually drew back half a step.

Korroc pointed past him into the chapel.

"I've seen worse things than an old book carried through that door. I've seen worse things *carrying* it."

Joran looked at the statue for a while.

"Deskari gave Staunton power."

"Aye."

"Droskar gave me work."

Korroc waited.

"Neither of them gave us anything worth the having."

There it was. Small, and bitter, and about forty years late, and true.

Joran turned his head. "I've seen Torag answer you."

Korroc shifted his weight. "Aye."

"That's the only reason I'm listening to any of this."

"Fair."

Joran looked faintly annoyed at having that accepted so readily, which Korroc thought was probably a good sign about something.

Then Nageru walked past the pair of them into the chapel, picked up a bucket, came back, and put a rag into Joran's hand.

"West wall."

Joran stared at him.

Nageru pointed. "West wall."

Joran looked at the rag. Then at Korroc.

Korroc shrugged. "Needs doing."

And for the first time since a bone claw had gone into his back and taken something out of him that nobody could name, something that was nearly amusement went across Joran Vhane's face.

He put the book back inside his coat, over the heart, where it had ridden for forty years.

Then he crossed the threshold.

No thunder. No hammer out of heaven. No voice from the statue, and no sudden warmth in the stone, and no sign of any kind that anything anywhere had taken the least notice.

Joran Vhane walked into the chapel of Torag with the Dark Smith's prayer book against his chest, and knelt down at the west wall, and began scrubbing.

Korroc decided that was quite enough theology for one afternoon, and went to find a second bucket.

---

The next morning there were three dwarves at the Purity Forge, and Korroc had been looking forward to saying so since he woke up.

He didn't. Thane would have made a face, and the face would have been correct, and Korroc would have had to live with having earned it. Some jokes are better kept in the pocket where they cannot be assessed.

The forge no longer pushed at him.

That was the thing he kept noticing, three days on. For a week this pit had leaned on his senses like a hand on the back of his neck — heat with an *intention* in it, heat that seemed to be paying attention to who had come down the stair. Now there was only heat. Enormous, honest, ordinary heat, doing what heat did because fuel burned and metal took it up and gave it back. A man could work in it. A man could sweat in it and swear at it and think about nothing but the piece in his tongs.

Korroc liked it considerably better.

His armor stood on the work stand beside him with the breastplate and shoulder pieces off, and he had been at one of the old enchantment channels since first light, opening room for what he meant to add.

*Armor of the Pious.*

He had not got used to the name and did not expect to. He had worn borrowed harness and he had worn his own, and this was the third thing, the one that had looked at him for four days and made up its mind. Working on it felt faintly like trimming a friend's beard. He kept catching himself going slower than the metal required, the way you do with a thing that might have an opinion.

Torag's hammer and anvil sat in the gold over the heart, clean as if they had been struck there at the making. He had stopped putting his hand over it every time he passed. Mostly.

Farther down the forge Joran worked under guard, straightening a stack of dented crusader helms — good, dull, necessary work, and he did it well. Thane had claimed the neighboring anvil for something else entirely.

Korroc had seen the blank first thing that morning and had been waiting ever since.

Short. Thick. Nothing like anything his cousin carried.

"What're you making?"

Thane did not look up. "Knife."

Korroc considered the piece of steel. "Odd knife."

"Aye."

"Very odd knife."

"Aye."

Korroc waited. The file went on going. He had grown up with this and could outlast it on a good day, and it was early yet.

"Going to make me ask?"

"You already did."

Korroc grunted, which in the Stonevein dialect meant *fair*, and Thane finally held the piece up to the light.

The blade was only a few inches long, broad at the base and tapering hard. The tang did not run back the way a tang should. Instead the beginnings of a grip were taking shape crosswise behind it, set to sit across a closed fist.

"Punch dagger."

"Aye."

That made a great deal of sense, and Korroc did not say so, because it also made him tired.

Thane's fighting had always been close. Too close, in Korroc's private opinion, held for eleven years and offered to nobody. His mother's blade and his father's parrying knife served him well and served him at a certain distance — and there were nights, and there had been one very recently in a room full of murdered gods, when even a short blade wanted more room than Thane Stonevein intended to give it.

A punch dagger wanted none at all.

"You replacing one of the others?"

Thane looked personally insulted. "No."

"Didn't think so."

"Just another option."

Korroc nodded and went back to his channel. There were men who collected coins. There were men who collected grievances. His cousin collected ways to put steel into things at conversational range, and had never once been talked out of a single one of them.

He watched sideways for a while, the way you watch a craftsman you rate. Thane's hands were good. They had always been good — the same hands that could find a wire in a doorframe by feel could hold a file dead flat across a bevel for a hundred strokes without wandering, and the two facts were the same fact wearing different coats.

*If you're going to be a knife, son, be a true one.*

Helja Stonevein had put that in her boy's hand with his first dagger, and Korroc had heard her say it, and he thought about it now watching the boy in question make a knife out of nothing on the other side of a forge in the citadel their fathers had drawn on paper.

He kept that in his pocket too.

---

A while later Caleth and Nageru came down the stairs.

Korroc looked up. "No."

Caleth stopped on the last step. "I haven't asked anything."

"Doesn't matter."

Nageru kept walking, past both of them, and settled against the wall with the unhurried air of a man who had come to watch something and had correctly guessed how long it would take.

Caleth looked offended, which he did well; there was a whole formal architecture to it. "I've brought work."

"Worse."

Radiance was slung across his back.

That got Thane. He set down the file. "What are you doing to it?"

"Hopefully nothing disastrous."

"That's not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to reassure. It was meant to be accurate."

Caleth unslung the glaive and laid it along the cleared section of the bench, and even in forge light the thing carried its own weather — that pale steady white running the length of the leaf-blade, brighter at the edge, the way it had been since a banner went up over this fortress and it had decided to have an opinion about that.

Thane wiped his hands on his apron and came over. "What do you want?"

Caleth told him.

Korroc listened while he worked. So, he noticed, did Joran, who had not stopped hammering but had stopped hammering in any particular rhythm.

The idea was not unreasonable. That was the trouble with it. Unreasonable things could be dismissed with a word and a return to your own bench. Reasonable things became work.

Thane picked up Radiance. Turned it. Sighted along the haft and down the blade with one eye closed, the way you check a stave for a wind in the grain, and then started asking questions.

What exactly did Caleth want the weapon to hold? How should the effect come out of it — all at once, or bled? Would it draw from the weapon or from the man? Had Radiance ever taken a working like this before, or refused one?

Caleth answered what he could, which was most of it, and admitted what he couldn't, which was the interesting part.

Thane grunted at last. "I think we can do it."

Korroc looked over. "We?"

"You've two hands."

"So do you."

"Aye. Mine are occupied."

And that was that. Thane laid the cloth over the unfinished punch dagger — not tossed, laid, squared at the corners — and moved his tools down the bench to Radiance.

---

The first attempt failed before noon. The second failed not long after.

The third made Radiance flare bright enough to turn every shadow in the forge sharp and black as cut paper, and then threw the working straight back out with a crack that put Caleth back half a step and set Korroc's teeth on edge.

Korroc looked at the glaive. The glaive looked innocent.

"Temperamental thing."

Caleth frowned. "It isn't temperamental."

"It just threw your spell across the room."

"It rejected an incompatible structure."

Korroc looked at Thane. "Temperamental."

"Aye."

Caleth glared at the pair of them with the particular patience of a man who has been outnumbered by dwarves before and has learned exactly how much it is worth spending on it. Against the wall, Nageru's face did nothing at all, which from Nageru was practically applause.

Joran had stopped hammering. Only for a moment. Then he went on.

Thane tried again. And again. Each time the steel held — the channels sat where he had cut them, the balance stayed true, the physical work was sound. Each time the binding let go.

By midafternoon his cousin was annoyed. By late afternoon he was personally offended, which was a different animal entirely, and Korroc had been able to tell the two apart since they were forty years younger and considerably stupider.

"You're going to break your teeth if you keep grinding them like that."

"Nothing wrong with the work."

"Didn't say there was."

"The channels are right."

"Aye."

"The balance is right."

"Aye."

"The structure is *right.*"

"Aye. So what's wrong?"

Thane stopped filing. He looked up, slowly, at Caleth.

Caleth looked back.

"Oh," said Caleth.

"Aye."

Caleth folded his arms. "My casting."

"Not strong enough."

"I can cast."

"Didn't say you couldn't."

"I have been casting since before you could pick a lock, Master Stonevein."

"And this thing doesn't care." Thane tapped the flat of the blade with one finger, not hard, the way you'd knock on a door you weren't sure about. "You're trying to talk a weapon older than everyone in this room put together into taking a second working on top of the one it's already carrying. It's got a latch already. You're asking it to let you fit another, and it's holding the frame shut while you do it."

Caleth looked at the glaive for a long moment.

Korroc considered the problem, found it entirely outside his craft, and shrugged. "Ask the elf."

Caleth turned. "Aravashnial?"

"Know another one?"

"He'll want to know exactly what we're doing."

"Aye."

"He'll ask questions."

"Probably."

"*Many* questions."

"Almost certainly."

Caleth sighed — a long, formal, put-upon sound, and Korroc enjoyed every part of it — then took up Radiance and headed for the stair. "Back eventually."

Nageru watched him go. "So. Perhaps tomorrow."

Korroc laughed out loud, and the sound went up the pit and came back to him, and he found he did not mind that at all.

---

Thane watched his cousin's half-elf disappear up the steps, waited exactly as long as courtesy required, and pulled the cloth straight back off his own project.

Korroc stared at him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You're staring."

"Just admiring a man who can set down one piece of work and take up another without so much as a moment of mourning."

"I didn't abandon anything." Thane put the punch dagger back on the anvil and reached for his hammer. "Radiance is waiting on a wizard." The hammer came up. "This isn't."

The first strikes narrowed the shoulders of the blade, and after that came the long part, which was the part Korroc loved and could not explain to anyone who did not already know: heating, drawing, checking, grinding, heating again. The work that has no story in it. The work that is only the difference between a thing that is nearly right and a thing that is right.

He went back to his armor. But every so often he looked over, and every time he looked the dagger had become more of itself.

Thane was making it compact and heavy, weighted for force rather than reach, the cross-grip sitting behind the knuckles so the blade came forward through the fist. Every strike would be a thrust. There was no flourish anywhere on it. No ornament, no filework, not so much as a chamfer that wasn't earning its keep.

Of course there wasn't. Thane Stonevein was building a tool whose entire purpose was to put several inches of steel through something that had made the mistake of thinking distance meant safety, and decoration would have been a lie about what it was for.

Korroc approved so thoroughly he nearly said so.

---

Across the forge, Joran was watching them.

Nageru noticed. Of course he did. The aasimar had been against that wall for two hours without appearing to look directly at anything, and Korroc had long ago stopped mistaking that for inattention.

"You have been watching them."

Joran looked over. "Who?"

Nageru moved his chin toward the cousins.

Joran's face closed like a shutter. "I've eyes."

"That was not an answer."

Joran went back to the helm. Hammer. Turn. Hammer. The rhythm of a man who has decided the conversation is over and intends to build a wall out of noise.

Then, three strikes later: "I've never seen it done that way."

Thane looked up from the dagger. "Done what way?"

Joran seemed irritated to find himself inside his own sentence. "Work."

Korroc frowned.

Joran pointed with his hammer. "You." Then at Thane. "Him."

"What about us?"

"You don't ask."

Thane looked at Korroc. Korroc looked at Thane.

Joran made a sound of pure exasperation, the first unguarded noise Korroc had heard out of him. "If he's holding something and needs another hand, you take it. If you're running a line wrong, he moves the piece under you. If he reaches for a tool, you've already put it where his hand's going."

Korroc shrugged. "That's working."

"*No.*"

It came out harder than any of them expected. It came out of somewhere with pressure behind it.

The forge went quiet. Even the guard by the stair shifted.

"That's working *together,*" Joran said.

He stared down at the helm on his anvil. Nobody interrupted him. Korroc had learned some time ago that when a man has finally started, the kindest thing you can do is be furniture.

"I worked this forge for decades."

Hammer.

"If they gave me a thing, I did it."

Hammer.

"If I couldn't do it, I'd failed."

He turned the helm on the horn, sighted along the crown, set it back down.

"If I needed another pair of hands, I found a way to do it without them. Every time. That's what the work *was.*" A pause. "Staunton came down, sometimes."

The hammer stopped moving. His eyes stayed on the metal.

"Once I had a breastplate on the cradle near two days. Couldn't get it to take evenly. Every time I brought the left side in, the right went out of true on me."

Korroc knew that exact misery. He had fought a cuirass like that for a solid week when he was twenty-six and had finally discovered that the apprentice on the bellows had been drunk since Oathday. He kept that to himself as well. This was not a story that wanted company.

"Staunton stood there." Joran lifted his chin toward the rail above the pit, where a man could look down at another man working. "Watched me fight it."

His mouth thinned to nothing.

"He never picked up a hammer."

Nobody spoke. Up on the rail, the guard had stopped pretending to look elsewhere.

Joran nodded at Thane. "He wouldn't even have to ask you."

Korroc looked at his cousin.

Thane shrugged. "Course not."

Joran stared at him for a long moment. "That's what I mean."

The quiet came back and sat with them.

Then Korroc reached for a set of tongs.

Thane put them into his hand without looking up.

Joran watched it happen. Korroc, who had felt his cousin's grip settle into his palm before he had finished reaching, pretended with all his strength not to notice that he had been watched.

Thane went back to the punch dagger.

---

Caleth returned an hour later. With Aravashnial.

Thane glanced at the stair. "That was quicker than expected."

Caleth's expression suggested there had been negotiations, and that terms had been agreed, and that he was not going to enjoy any of them.

Aravashnial came down the last steps with the careful tread of a man entering a workshop where people intended to touch magic with hammers.

"I have been informed," he said, "that you have a plan."

Thane nodded. "I do."

"I have also been informed that it is failing."

"I said it was *encountering resistance,*" Caleth said.

"You said it threw you backward."

"Half a step."

Aravashnial turned to Thane. "Show me."

Thane laid the dagger aside a second time. This time, Korroc noticed, he looked at it before he covered it.

"You'd better be worth it," he muttered at the glaive.

Caleth heard. "So reassuring."

Then Thane showed him everything.

Not simplified. Not translated into elf. The whole of it — the physical channels and where he'd run them and why; the anchor points; the flow through the haft; the release; and the exact place, every time, where Caleth's working came apart against whatever Radiance was already carrying.

Aravashnial asked six questions. Thane answered five.

Over the sixth they argued for eleven minutes.

Korroc worked on his armor. Joran repaired another helm. Nageru watched both conversations — the argument and the two dwarves not participating in it — with the air of a man finding them equally worth his time.

Eventually Aravashnial said, "You are wrong."

"I'm not."

"Your third binding point is inverted."

"It's inverted *because* the weapon's carrying the load."

"No. It is inverted because you are thinking like a smith."

"I *am* a smith."

"Yes." The elf's mouth moved fractionally. "I had noticed."

Thane's eyes narrowed to something Korroc had last seen aimed at a locked door that was winning.

Aravashnial picked up a stub of chalk. "May I?"

Thane gestured at the slate, sharply, granting nothing.

The elf drew three lines.

Thane looked at them for a while. Then took the chalk and drew two more.

Aravashnial added one.

They both stopped.

Korroc waited, holding a piece of gold-plated mithral he had entirely forgotten about.

Thane said one old Dwarven word, low and with feeling.

"Precisely," said Aravashnial, and he looked as pleased as Korroc had ever seen him, which was saying something for an elf who had spent the last four days visibly delighted by a forge that had been eating holy relics for seventy years.

Then they started again.

This time Caleth did not supply the magic. Aravashnial did.

Korroc watched from his own bench, and what surprised him was how little there was to watch. He had expected something with weather in it. Magic, in his experience, thought very highly of itself and generally wanted an audience.

This looked like welding.

Pressure. Heat. Patience. The elf laid the working into the structure Thane had cut for it the way you lay a bead into a prepared joint — slowly, without force, watching the edges rather than the middle. Thane adjusted the steel as he went, reading it, reaching in with a fine pick to lift something a hair's breadth.

Once he said, "Hold."

Aravashnial held, without argument, mid-word.

Thane changed something Korroc could not see.

"Again."

The elf went on.

And Radiance began to glow — not the hard flat flare that had thrown Caleth back, but something down deeper in it, steady, the way a bank of coals goes when you have finally got the air right.

Caleth stepped closer without seeming to know he had. The light climbed his forearms and found his face and settled there, and Korroc looked away from it, because there are things a man is entitled to receive without being watched.

Aravashnial lifted his fingers clear.

Thane stepped back.

Nobody moved. Radiance lay on the bench exactly as it was.

Caleth reached out and took it up. For one instant the glow brightened —

— and then it accepted him.

Thane let out a breath. "There."

"It worked," Caleth said.

"Aye."

Aravashnial rubbed his fingertips together, examining nothing. "As expected."

Thane gave him a look that would have stripped enamel. "You said my binding was wrong."

"It was."

"So you didn't expect it to work until I fixed it."

"Which you did."

"Because you *told* me."

"Then my expectation," said Aravashnial serenely, "was justified."

Korroc laughed so hard he had to put his hammer down on the bench before he dropped it on his own foot.

---

Caleth stayed a few minutes more, turning Radiance over, testing the new working in small careful ways and talking it through with Aravashnial in a language of their own.

Thane did not stay. The instant nobody required his hands he had the cloth off the dagger and the dagger back in the fire.

Korroc smiled into his beard. Then remembered.

"Caleth."

The half-elf looked over. "What?"

Korroc nodded at the pack beside his bench. "Before you go."

Caleth followed the gesture, and understanding arrived on his face at once. "The wand."

"Aye."

Korroc dug it out. Plain wood, a wrap of leather at one end, nothing about it worth a second look from anyone who had not watched it pull four people back off the floor. He held it out.

"Getting low again."

Caleth took it.

And Aravashnial went absolutely still.

Korroc noticed it this time. That was the difference. On the road he had been busy dying of other problems; here he had a cup of water and nothing in his hands, and he saw it happen — and what was in the elf's face was not surprise.

It was recognition.

Aravashnial's eyes had gone to the wand in Caleth's hand, and something behind them slid quietly into a slot it had been waiting in for a long while.

"Oh," Aravashnial said.

Caleth glanced at him. There was a pause of about a heartbeat and a half.

Then, in a rather different voice: "*Oh.*"

Thane looked between the two of them.

Korroc frowned. "What?"

Aravashnial did not take his eyes off the wand. "I have seen him do this before."

"Aye. On the road."

"I remember."

He said it in a way that made plain that forgetting had never been among the available options.

Caleth turned the wand slowly between his fingers. "You said we should talk later."

"Yes."

Caleth looked around the pit — at the forge, at the anvils, at three dwarves and a guard and a stack of dented helms. "I suppose this is later."

"Considerably."

Nageru's mouth moved by perhaps the width of a hair.

Thane returned to his dagger. "Your problem now."

Caleth ignored him with dignity.

Aravashnial stepped up to the bench. "Do exactly what you did before."

"I was planning to."

"Do not alter the process because I am watching."

"I wasn't planning to do that either."

"Good."

Caleth looked at Korroc. Korroc shrugged and did not help. "You know what happened last time."

Caleth sighed and closed his hand around the wand.

Korroc found, watching, that he was putting the night back together.

A campfire on the road to Drezen. A near-dead wand, and a little light under the half-elf's fingers, and a thing that should not have been possible happening anyway while men stood around being tired. Aravashnial had gone quiet then too. Korroc remembered that much and had thought nothing of it, because there had been a great deal else going on. There always was. Within the hour there had been screaming at the prisoner cages, and shadowblood in a man's blood, and an invisible knife opening a tent, and a halfling on the ground with Thane's pommels above her.

*That was the same night?*

He nearly said it aloud. He got as far as opening his mouth.

*Later* had been a reasonable word to use, that night. It had simply never come round again — not through the towers, not through the roof, not through a false goddess or a citadel or seventy-five years of somebody else's war ending in an afternoon.

It had taken until a forge floor with nothing urgent in it.

Caleth closed his eyes.

The light came up beneath his fingers, faint and golden-white. Not in the wand at first.

In his hand.

Aravashnial leaned in perhaps an inch, and Korroc knew that look. The elf was not standing in a forge any more. He was somewhere considerably better lit, looking at a problem.

Then the light moved — passed out of Caleth and into the worn old wood, and the wand brightened as if something inside it had drawn a breath after a long time without one. Only a moment. Then it faded.

Caleth opened his hand. "Done."

Korroc held out his palm. Caleth gave the wand back. Korroc tucked it away against his ribs, where it lived. "Much obliged."

Aravashnial had not moved.

Caleth looked at him. "No."

"I have not asked anything."

"You are *about* to."

"Naturally."

"No."

Aravashnial frowned. Caleth stepped back from the bench with the careful footwork of a man withdrawing from a bad position.

"We already had this conversation."

"We had approximately thirty seconds of this conversation," said Aravashnial, "before an attempted poisoning, an act of sabotage, and an invisible intruder interrupted it."

Korroc blinked. "That *was* the same night?"

Thane, not looking up: "Aye."

"Huh."

Aravashnial had already moved on. "You told me you could push magic into objects that expend charges."

"Yes."

"You did not explain how."

"Because I don't know how."

"Has that changed?"

"No."

"Have you tested the limitations?"

"Some."

The elf's eyes sharpened. "*Some.*"

Korroc watched Caleth hear the trap close about half a second after stepping in it — a small stillness, the flicker of a man reviewing his own last word and finding it indefensible.

"Yes."

"Which objects?"

"Mostly wands."

"*Mostly.*"

"Aravashnial."

"Can you restore a wholly exhausted wand?"

"Yes."

"How much?"

"It varies."

"According to what?"

"I don't know."

"Your remaining capacity?"

"I don't think so."

"Spell level?"

"No."

"The type of magic within the item?"

"Not that I've noticed."

"Method of construction?"

"I have no idea how Korroc's wand was constructed."

Aravashnial nodded once and put that somewhere. "Does the process tire you?"

"No."

"Does it diminish your casting?"

"No."

"Can you do it more than once?"

"Not in the same day."

The elf paused. That one interested him; Korroc could see it land.

"Because you have attempted to."

"Yes."

"And?"

"Nothing happens."

"Nothing at all?"

"Nothing."

"Do you find yourself unable to *begin* the process — or does the magic fail after you have begun it?"

Caleth stared at him.

Nageru pushed off the wall. "I think we should leave."

"Agreed," said Caleth, already moving.

Aravashnial followed. "That does not answer the question."

"I know."

"Caleth."

"I can't do it again until tomorrow."

"Tomorrow at what hour?"

Caleth stopped. Turned round slowly. "What?"

"At what hour does it return to you?"

"I don't know."

"Sunrise?"

"I don't know."

"After rest?"

"I don't know."

"After preparing spells?"

"I don't know."

"Precisely twenty-four hours from use?"

"I *don't know.*"

Aravashnial stared at him with something close to grief. "Have you made any effort whatsoever to establish this?"

"I've been busy."

The elf looked around the pit. At the forge. At the ruined armor stacked three deep against the walls. At the glaive in Caleth's hand.

"Fair," he said.

Caleth looked genuinely thrown.

"But tomorrow," Aravashnial added, "we test it."

Caleth resumed walking. "No."

"Then the day after."

"No."

"There are several controlled trials that would require very little of your time."

"No."

"First we establish the recovery interval."

"No."

"Then we compare a partially depleted item against a wholly depleted one."

"*Aravashnial.*"

"A wand of arcane rather than divine make would also be instructive."

Nageru fell in beside Caleth without appearing to hurry, and Aravashnial followed the pair of them to the stair, and Korroc listened to the whole thing climb away from him.

"We should also establish whether it functions upon a staff."

"No."

"A charged wondrous item."

"No."

"A rod."

"Does a rod normally carry charges?"

"Some do."

"Then perhaps."

"*Excellent.*"

"That was not permission."

And then Nageru, further up, mild as milk: "You should stop answering him."

"Thank you, Nageru."

The footsteps climbed. The questions kept coming, thinner and thinner up the stone.

"Do you perceive the magic entering the item?"

"No."

"Can you perceive how much you have restored?"

"No."

"Can you stop partway?"

"I don't know."

"*Interesting.*"

"That was not meant to encourage you."

Then the corridor took them, and the forge was quiet again except for the fire.

Korroc looked across at his cousin, who was filing the inside of the punch dagger's grip as though none of the last hour had occurred in his presence.

"Think Aravashnial ever forgot about that wand?"

"No."

"Think Caleth did?"

"Aye."

Korroc picked up his hammer. "There's his mistake."

Thane's mouth twitched.

Both dwarves went back to work.

---

The punch dagger was finished shortly before supper.

Joran was closing out the last of the helms. Korroc had given up on his armor for the day and was leaning against the bench with a cup of water going warm in his hand, which was the correct way to end an afternoon and the only way he had ever known to tell that one had been a good one.

Thane sat at the grinding wheel. The dagger turned against the stone. Once. Twice. Again.

Then he stopped.

He wiped the blade down — and Korroc noted that he wiped it, on a blade that had never been near anything but its own making, and said nothing at all about it. He checked the edge with the pad of his thumb. Took one corner of the grip down with a file. Checked it again.

Then he slid his hand through the cross-grip and closed his fist.

The weapon nearly vanished. Only the blade stayed visible, standing out forward between his knuckles like something the hand had grown.

Korroc looked it over properly. "Ugly thing."

"Supposed to be."

"Good point."

Thane made two short strikes into the air. Nothing broad, nothing showy. Straight, compact, fast — the motion a man could make with his chest against something that had just learned that closing the distance had been a mistake.

Korroc nodded. "Fits you."

"Aye."

Thane turned it once in his hand.

Joran had stopped working again. He was looking at the dagger.

"Good balance?"

Thane glanced over — and then, without any pause Korroc could measure, reversed the weapon and held it out grip-first.

"See for yourself."

Korroc kept his face where it was.

He was thinking about a bunkroom four floors up and two weeks back, and a chained man straining toward a rapier laid just past his reach, and his cousin saying *I should stab you with it* and then handing it over hilt-first anyway because Korroc had told him to. And what that man had turned out to be. And what it had cost the half-elf who had just walked up those stairs.

Thane had been right that day and had been overruled and had never once said so out loud.

And here he was, holding a knife out grip-first to a Vhane.

Joran stared at him. Only for a second.

Then he set down his hammer and took it.

He checked the weight. Found the centre. Ran a thumb along the edge without looking at it. Turned the grip in his fist and opened and closed his hand twice, feeling where the metal sat against the bones.

Old habits. Smith's habits. The oldest ones there are, and they do not leave a man, whatever else does.

He handed it back. "Wants another fraction off the inside of the grip."

Thane looked. Tested it against his own palm. Looked again.

"Aye."

He picked up the file.

Korroc watched Joran go back to his own bench, and nobody said one word about what had just happened, because there was no need, and because saying it would have ruined it.

---

The weeks after that began to settle.

Not go quiet. Drezen did not know how. There were three hundred crusaders in it and every one of them had something that needed fixing, burning, burying, or blessing. But settle — yes.

There is a rhythm to repair, and Korroc had known it his whole life and had missed it for a year.

Damage is chaos. Damage is a thing that happens *to* you, all at once, in no order, while you are busy. Repair is repetition. Hammer, nail, stone, mortar, meal, sleep, again. Nothing about it is dramatic and every part of it is a kind of prayer, and the Father has never once required that a prayer be interesting.

He and Thane took most mornings at the Purity Forge, and the army supplied an endless line of work.

Split mail. Bent breastplates. Cracked shield bosses. Sword edges chewed to saw teeth by somebody who had been parrying with the wrong part of the blade and would be told so. Spearheads. Buckles. Helms. Hinges. Tools, more tools than anyone expected, because an army eats tools.

Not glorious things. Useful things. Korroc had never once been able to tell the difference and had stopped trying to explain that to people.

Joran joined them by degrees. Simple repairs first, then more. Always watched. Always accounted for, every hour of it, written down by somebody.

But trusted with a hammer.

Korroc had put it to Irabeth plainly.

"He's a smith."

"He's also a prisoner."

"Aye."

"He served the enemy."

"Aye."

"He worked the corruption forge for forty years."

"Aye."

Irabeth had stared at him. "You are not making your argument especially well."

"I'm not arguing any of that."

"Then what *are* you arguing?"

Korroc had shrugged. "He's a smith."

She rubbed her eyes for a while.

In the end she agreed. Perhaps because she agreed with him. Perhaps because three hundred crusaders destroyed equipment considerably faster than two dwarves could mend it.

Korroc did not much care which.

The titles Irabeth had handed out over a table of lists turned into work, which is the only thing a title has ever been good for.

Aron walked the curtain wall every morning with a slate under his arm, and there was not a mile of it he had not condemned some part of, and the man who had spent two years saying only what needed saying now had a very great deal that needed saying about drainage.

Horgus took the ledgers, and Korroc had been braced for the complaining. What he got instead was Horgus Gwerm at a trestle table in a cold hall before first light with three clerks and the whole supply of a reclaimed city laid out in columns, running it like a man who had been waiting his entire life to be handed something worth counting. He was also — and this was the part Korroc could not get past — *pleasant.* A full sentence of genuine courtesy to a quartermaster who had brought him bad numbers, and no remark whatever about anybody's boots.

"He's up to something," Korroc said.

Thane, not looking up: "He isn't."

"He said good morning to me."

"Aye."

"He *meant* it."

"That'll be the worst of it. Gets easier after."

Anevia was almost never Anevia. Korroc got used to not knowing which of the people in the market rows he was looking at, and to being wrong about it, and to Thane finding this considerably funnier than he did.

His cousin never said one word about what Drezen's new spymaster did with her days. But on the mornings she was out in the city, Korroc noticed that Thane's attention had a habit of going up the stairs and staying there.

And the eastern tower filled up.

Aravashnial had claimed it before anyone gave it to him and now had it in writing, and within a month there were books in it that had not been within three hundred miles of Drezen when the gates fell, and a warding on the doorframe that stood the hair up on Korroc's arms when he walked past it, and a quiet traffic of people going in and out who did not look much like crusaders.

He made no secret of any of it. He had said it at the council table in front of nine people and gone straight to work — building the order back up out of the wreck of Kenabres in the first fortress the crusade had taken in seventy-five years, and finding time on top of that to argue with Aron about the magical half of the walls.

Korroc thought it was a fine thing and told him so.

What Korroc noticed, and did not understand, and put away in the place where he kept things he did not understand, was Caleth.

The half-elf never once asked to be involved. He simply turned up. Carrying something, usually, or on his way somewhere that happened to be past that door, and Korroc — who had watched Caleth Aranor plan a battle by measuring a map with two fingers — did not entirely believe in that much coincidence.

But it was not his business. So he let it alone, and went back to his hammer, and thought no more about it than a man thinks about a thing he has decided not to think about.

Caleth and Nageru spent the rest of their time on the chapels. Sosiel had claimed them for it within a day of being handed a title he had not wanted, and having been given the spiritual care of a whole city, he had evidently decided to do it properly out of spite.

Iomedae's shrine took shape first, then Torag's. Then the old cathedral on Paradise Hill began to change — and not into a house of Shelyn, which surprised a good many people, because Sosiel Vaenic wore her badge openly and had earned the right.

He refused it.

Instead he opened the place up. Iomedae. Torag. Shelyn. Sarenrae. Irori. Cayden Cailean. Desna. Small shrines to start with, only spaces and symbols and a little room — enough that soldiers who had marched three hundred miles beside one another could stop arguing about which god had earned the best roof.

Korroc approved, and said so, and was pleased to be asked.

Mostly he approved of Sosiel putting the Caydenites at the back. Experience suggested wisdom in that.

---

The summons came on an ordinary afternoon.

Korroc was working a crease out of a crusader's breastplate — a bad one, a boot-heel crease, somebody had been stood on while down and had lived, which was the good half of the story. Thane sat at the next bench putting the last stitches into a sheath for the punch dagger. Joran was replacing buckles on a stack of old greaves.

A young crusader appeared at the top of the stairs.

"Korroc Stonevein?"

He looked up. "Aye."

"Thane Stonevein?"

Thane raised one hand without raising his head.

"Master Aravashnial asks that you come up to the council chamber."

Korroc's hammer stopped in the air. "What for?"

"He didn't say."

Of course he didn't.

"Caleth and Nageru?"

"Already sent for, sir."

Korroc looked at Thane. Thane looked back — and something in his cousin's face moved, very slightly, the way a floor tells you about a room before you have finished walking into it.

Korroc felt the same thing arrive low in his own stomach.

Aravashnial did not pull all four of them off their work without a reason. The elf had spent four days arguing with a book while the world burned around him and had not once asked anyone for anything he could do himself.

Thane slid the punch dagger into its new sheath and hung it on his belt beside the others.

Korroc looked at the collection. "Running out of belt."

"Not yet."

He set the hammer down on the anvil, where it belonged, and they went up.

---

Caleth was already outside the council room. Nageru stood beside him with his arms folded, apparently content to wait for all four before anybody went in — which was a small thing, and which Korroc noticed anyway.

Caleth looked from one dwarf to the other. "What took you?"

"Work," said Thane.

Korroc pointed at the new sheath. "He had to be sure number four sat comfortably."

Caleth looked down.

Mother's dagger on the right. Father's knife on the left. The wrist-sheath, which everyone pretended not to know about and which had been there since Thane was nineteen. And now this.

"Do you intend to stop?" Caleth asked.

"No."

"Thought I'd ask."

Thane pushed the door open.

The humour stopped there.

Irabeth was inside. So was Aron. So was Aravashnial.

Korroc had expected the council chamber and this was not it. The table had disappeared under paper. Maps lay open across one end with their corners pinned down by whatever had been nearest when the elf needed a weight — an ink bottle, two books, a stone off the floor, and somebody's abandoned cup. Ledgers stood in uneven stacks. Loose pages filled the gaps between.

Some of it looked recent. A great deal of it did not. One map was old enough that the edges had gone soft and brown as autumn leaf, and Korroc's eye went to it and stayed.

Aravashnial stood over the whole mess with both palms flat on the table.

Irabeth was beside him. Aron stood opposite, bent over one of the maps.

Nobody was arguing. Korroc disliked that at once.

Aron looked up first. "Good. You're all here."

"Warden," Irabeth said, without turning round. "The old survey — the western one."

Aron slid it toward her.

Korroc caught the word and turned it over. *Warden of Drezen.* Irabeth had put the title on him in front of the assembled companies four days ago, in the courtyard, in the flat voice she used for things she had already decided — and Aron Kir had taken it the way he took everything, with a nod and no expression, as though somebody had handed him a tool and told him what it was for.

It still sounded new. Korroc suspected it would go on sounding new for about a year and then sound as though it had always been his.

Caleth came in. "What's happened?"

"Nothing," said Irabeth.

Thane stopped beside Korroc. "That's usually when somebody tells us something's happened."

Irabeth almost smiled. Almost.

"Nothing has happened *yet.*"

Which was worse.

Korroc looked at Aravashnial. The elf had not said a word since they came in. Which was worse still.

Nageru closed the door behind them.

Korroc's eyes went back to the table, and to the maps, because they were maps of a place he had spent a year walking toward and three weeks living in, and because his hands knew stone the way other men's knew faces.

Several were Drezen. He had the shape of those walls in his bones by now. One was the citadel as it stood. Another was older. Much older.

And there were lines under the familiar rooms that Korroc did not recognise.

Passages. Foundations. Something running the length of the western side, beneath everything, drawn by a hand that had known what it was doing.

Beside the maps sat the ledgers, open, with slips of paper marking pages throughout. Korroc counted twelve markers before he gave it up.

"You've been busy," Caleth said.

Aravashnial looked up at last. "Yes."

That was all of it. That was the whole answer.

Irabeth glanced at Aron, and something went between them that was not a conversation but the end of one.

Aron straightened and rolled the map he had been reading halfway shut. "Right."

Korroc frowned. "Right what?"

Aron reached for his gloves. "That's my part done."

Thane's face went flat. "You summoned us up here and now you're leaving."

"I didn't summon you." Aron tipped his head at the elf. "He did."

Korroc looked between them. "What did you do?"

"Confirmed something."

"What?"

Aron hesitated — and Aron Kir did not hesitate; he was a man who had spent every word he owned years ago and issued the remainder like coin. Then he shook his head. "His information. His conversation."

Thane took one step toward the table. "That's a very irritating answer."

"I know."

"Then give a better one."

Aron met his eyes, and for a moment Korroc genuinely thought he might.

Instead the Warden of Drezen pulled on one glove. Then the other.

"No."

Thane stared at him. Aron returned it without any visible concern at all.

Irabeth came round the end of the table. "Aravashnial asked for the four of you privately, once Aron and I had seen what he'd found."

Korroc's stomach shut.

There it was.

*Found.*

Not suspected. Not wondered about. Not *looked into.*

Found.

Irabeth stopped beside him and dropped her voice. "Let him lay it out in his own order."

Korroc looked at her.

She held his eyes a moment longer than she needed to, and that told him more than he had any wish to know.

"Commander—"

"Korroc."

Just his name. Quietly.

He stopped.

She squeezed his arm once as she went past, which she had never done, and then she was at the door.

Aron followed her.

Thane turned. "Aron."

The warden stopped with one hand on the frame. "What?"

"Whatever this is." Thane's eyes went to the paper on the table and came back. "You already knew?"

"Only what he showed us."

"And?"

Aron looked at him. His answer took a moment to come.

"I think you should hear all of it before you decide what it means."

Then he stepped out into the corridor. Irabeth went after him.

The door closed. The latch settled, and the sound of it went through Korroc like a struck note.

Four crusaders on one side of a table. One elf on the other.

For several seconds nobody said anything at all. Korroc could hear the fortress going on outside — boots in the hallway, a voice somewhere calling a number, and further off, faint through a great deal of good dwarven stone, the ringing of a hammer.

Normal sounds. Useful sounds. The sounds of a place that had been won.

Aravashnial looked down at the documents in front of him. He gathered a loose page and set it square against another. Then he moved a map that did not need moving, half an inch, and looked at where it had been.

Korroc had watched this elf face demons without so much as adjusting his sleeves.

That frightened him more than anything else in the room.

At last Aravashnial looked up — at Korroc, and then at Thane.

"I have found something concerning your fathers."

And that was where the ordinary afternoon ended.
