# The Hallowed Tower

*Chapter Twenty-Six — Under My Protection*

---

"Where are we going?" Caleth said.

He said it to Nageru's back, because Nageru's back was what there was. The monk was already off the stone shelf below the fane and down into the dry grass of the valley, barefoot, unhurried, with his javelins across his shoulders and the sun not yet over the hills behind him.

Nageru stopped. He turned around. He looked at Caleth, and at the four horses, and at the four riders standing beside them in the cold morning, and then he lifted one arm and pointed.

"That way."

West. Not any particular piece of west. Simply *west*, the whole of it, a long brown country of broken ground going out under a sky the color of an old bruise.

Aravashnial, at the bay's head, looked at the pointing arm and then at the monk.

"How do you know?"

Nageru shrugged.

"I do," he said, and turned around, and started walking.

Caleth looked at Korroc. Korroc looked at Thane. Thane looked at the sky with the air of a dwarf who has been asked to follow a dream across the Worldwound by a man with no shoes, and has decided not to make anything of it before breakfast.

They followed.

---

It took a week.

Caleth had said it would, standing over the map in the command room with his fingers spread across the scale, and he took no pleasure in being right. A week was a long time to be in the Wound. A week was long enough for anything.

The first days were ordinary, in the way the Worldwound was ever ordinary. They rode through low hills and then through country with no hills at all, flat and cracked and gray-brown, where the grass grew in stiff tufts with dead ground between them and the wind came across it all day from somewhere a long way off. Twice in the first day small things came at them out of the broken ground — demons the size of dogs, all teeth and spite, the kind that were less a fight than a chore — and were dispatched. Korroc did most of the dispatching, with the axe, and was in a better temper afterward than Caleth had seen him in for weeks. Once, on the second afternoon, something larger watched them from the top of a far ridge for most of an hour and then was not there. Nobody chased it.

Nageru walked.

He walked at the front of them all day, every day, the way he had said he would, at a pace that should not have been possible for a man on foot and that the horses had to work to keep. He did not look at the ground. He did not look at the sky. He looked straight ahead, at a thing none of the rest of them could see, and when the ground forced them off his line he came back to it afterward as surely as a needle comes back to north.

Caleth watched him do it for two days and could not find the trick. There was no trick. That was the unsettling part.

On the evening of the third day they came to the Gray Road.

---

He had seen it once before, from a cave mouth by night, a long pale line in the dark. He had not understood, then, what it was.

It was a river. Or it had been. It lay across their path from one edge of the world to the other, a great trench in the land, gray as ash — gray banks, gray bed, gray dust rising off it in small slow ghosts where the wind went down into it — and it was empty. Fifty feet straight down, near enough. And across, the far bank was a dark line that the eye had to look for. Seven miles, Thane said, after a long time studying it with his hand over his eyes. Seven miles of nothing, where a river had been.

"Gray Road," Korroc said, and spat into the dust. "Some road."

Caleth stood at the edge and looked down.

There was no way down for a horse. The banks were sheer, or nearly, and crumbling, and the places where they were not sheer were worse. He walked the edge a quarter-mile north and a quarter-mile south and came back.

Then he took out a wand.

It was a plain thing, dark wood, a little worn at the grip, and it held a spell that would fold a man across a short distance — a step through nothing, a hundred yards or so, and out. The trouble with it, and the trouble with all such wands, was that it would fold the wielder and one other. One. He could not take the five of them and four horses down in one going. He would have to take them one by one.

He did the arithmetic. He did not like it.

"Eighteen," he said.

"Eighteen what?" said Korroc.

"Eighteen times." Caleth looked at the wand. "Down. And the same again, up the far side."

Korroc looked at the drop, and at the wand, and at Caleth, and Caleth saw him understand that the half-elf was going to step off the edge of that bank eighteen times, and back up it seventeen, carrying one of them each time.

"Start with the horses," Korroc said. "While you're fresh. They'll fight you."

Someone had to be with the horses when they were at the bottom, so Thane went first. 

But fight him they did. His own tall gray went down quietly, because it knew him. The bay went down quietly, because it was the kind of horse that went down quietly. Thane's dun mare went down with her ears flat and her eyes white and was, Caleth thought, perfectly reasonable about it. Korroc's horse went down screaming and came out at the bottom still screaming and had to be held, for some time, by Thane.

And then the people. Aravashnial, who closed his eyes for it. Nageru, who looked at the bed of the river as they came out onto it with an expression of great interest. Korroc, last, who said a long thing in Dwarven on the way down that Caleth understood as a prayer and was entirely sure was not a polite one.

Eighteen. He felt every one of them by the end. Not in his body. In the place behind his eyes where the wand drew on him, where every folding of the world took a small cold coin out of him and did not give it back until he slept.

They walked across the bed of the Gray Road in the last of the light, leading the horses, through dust that came up to the fetlocks and smelled of nothing at all. Seven miles. It took the rest of the evening. Nobody spoke much. The banks behind them went down into the gray and the banks ahead of them came up out of it, and in the middle of it, for a long time, there was nothing to see in any direction but the dry bed of a river and the four of them walking in it, and Nageru out in front.

Eighteen more at the far side, up.

By the end Caleth was not sure he could have told anyone his own name, and Korroc's horse had stopped screaming, which Korroc said was either a sign of progress or a sign that it had died of shock and simply not fallen over yet.

They camped a little way beyond the bank, in a fold of the ground out of the wind. Caleth sat on a stone with the wand across his knees and closed his eyes and put his hand flat along it, and pushed.

He had never understood how he did it. Aravashnial had asked him, once, at great length, on a forge floor, and he had said *I don't know* so many times that the words had stopped meaning anything. But it was there, the knack, whatever it was. He found the emptied places in the wand where the spell had been and he leaned on them, and something went out of him and into the wood, and the wood took it. Not all of it. Not all at once. But by the time he opened his eyes again it was warm in his hand, and full enough.

Across the small fire, Aravashnial was watching him do it. The elf did not say anything.

---

He cast the other thing at full dark.

It was a working he had used on the long road north to Drezen, and since, when they had a night ahead of them in country where nobody wanted to be asleep. It did not make them unafraid and it did not make them strong. It only made the night into a kind of rest that a man could take with his eyes open — so that they could sit up through the dark hours, and watch, and listen, and come to the morning as though they had slept the whole night through. It cost him something. It was worth it, out here.

Korroc took the first walk around the camp. Nageru sat a little apart, facing west, with his hands open on his knees. Thane laid his knives out on their cloth and began his slow tour along each edge with the whetstone, which he did every night now, and which Caleth had begun to suspect he did for the sound of it as much as for the edges.

Caleth had walked out a little way from the fire, to the edge of the light, to look at the far bank of the Gray Road going black against a sky with no moon in it. He heard Aravashnial come up behind him. He did not turn.

"Do you have any idea where Vang is?" the elf said.

Caleth looked at the dark.

"Our research," he said, "shows that he is at the Ivory Sanctum."

*Our research.* He had chosen the word. The dossier had been in a stack on the elf's own desk, in the elf's own hand — *Operates from the Ivory Sanctum. Location unknown.* — and Caleth had read it on a morning when he had not known whether the man who wrote it was alive. He did not know why he had said *our*. Perhaps to give it back to him.

"Do you know where that is?" said Aravashnial.

"No."

The elf was quiet for a moment.

"He still has my book," he said. "I want it back."

Caleth turned his head.

His book. The spellbook, he supposed — the one Jerribeth had been holding open in a cavern by the Molten Scar with her finger on its notation, which nobody had seen since. If Jerribeth had had it, then whoever Jerribeth answered to had it now. That was plain enough.

There was another book Vang kept. Caleth had heard him boast of it in water, a month ago, with a Riftwarden's blood still on the boast. He did not ask which one the elf meant. He did not trust his voice to ask.

"Very well," he said.

Aravashnial nodded. He did not go back to the fire. He stood beside Caleth at the edge of the light with his hands inside the sleeves of his traveling cloak, and Caleth could feel him choosing words, the way he could feel Korroc choosing which way to swing.

"One more thing," the elf said. "The others seem to have trouble trusting me."

He said it lightly. He did not quite manage it.

Caleth looked at him then, properly. The face in the dark was the face he had looked at over a desk and over a cot and across a dozen fires: long, fine, the bruising almost gone now, the eyes very steady. And under the steadiness — Caleth could not have said where; in the set of the shoulders, perhaps, in the hands kept out of sight — something that in another man he would have called nerves.

He had never seen Aravashnial nervous. He had seen him furious, and frightened, once, at a collar. Never this.

"We are all having trouble trusting you," Caleth said.

"And why is that?"

"Your escape." Caleth kept his voice level. "It was really too simple."

"I told you." The elf's voice was level too. "This is not the first time I have been in trouble. I have learned to take care of myself."

"I know you have."

"You know I would never willingly do anything —" Aravashnial stopped. Started again. "You know that. Don't you?"

"Yes," said Caleth. "It's the *unwilling* part that worries me."

He watched that land. He watched the elf take it, and turn it over, and set it down.

"How would I unwillingly be helping Vang?" Aravashnial said. "You have checked everything. You cast on me yourself. What is left?"

"Things I cannot check for." Caleth looked back out at the dark. "The unknown is the issue."

The whetstone went along the edge of a knife behind them, and stopped, and went along it again.

"So," said Aravashnial, after a while. "When you find this demon. What do you plan on doing then?"

"The succubus?"

"Yes."

"Talk to her."

"Don't you find it odd," the elf said, "that she contacted the monk?"

Caleth considered that. "No," he said. "Do you?"

"Yes. Why would she contact him and not all of you? Why only the one?" The elf's voice had dropped. "Don't you see that it could be a trick?"

"Certainly. Do you think it is?"

"It seems a little odd. Doesn't it?" A pause. "How much do you trust him?"

Caleth turned around.

"Nageru?"

"Yes."

He looked past the elf, at the fire, at the shape sitting apart from it facing west with his hands open on his knees. He thought of a cave under Kenabres. He thought of a ceiling in a cavern by the Molten Scar, and a man on the floor below him with three claws of a dragon in him, getting up. He thought of a parapet at night, and two letters, and a monk standing beside him in the dark until the stars came out without asking one thing.

"I trust him," he said.

He turned back to the elf.

"Are you trying to sow discord among us?"

"No." It came quickly. "No. But before all of this began — before Kenabres — you did not know him at all. None of you did. Can you truly trust him?"

"I feel," said Caleth, "that I could trust him with my life."

He heard himself say it. He heard what it was, said to this man, out here, in the dark — *I trust him with my life* — to the one person in the world he had every right to trust with it, and could not, and could not say why he could not.

*Brother, I am afraid.*

Aravashnial frowned.

It was a small frown, a scholar's frown, the frown of a man who had put a question to a text and had not got the answer he expected out of it. He looked at Caleth a moment longer.

"Very well," he said. "Well. I suppose we should go back to the others. Before they become suspicious."

"*More* suspicious," said Caleth.

Aravashnial looked at him. Then he nodded, slowly, and the frown stayed where it was, and he turned and went back to the fire.

Caleth stood at the edge of the light a long time after.

He did not go over it. He knew better than to go over it tonight, with nothing in him but a week's cold coins and eighteen folds and eighteen more. He only stood and looked at the far bank of a dead river and let the conversation sit in him, the whole of it, in order, the way it had been said.

*He still has my book.*

*How much do you trust him?*

He thought: *I could tell him.* Here. Now. Turn around and walk back to that fire and say, *I found the letters. I know who you are. I know who I am.* And see what the face did.

He did not.

He went back to the fire and sat down in his place with his back to a stone and his hand where it always was, on the left side of his coat, over the letters and the note and the small hard shape of the potion. Thane looked up at him once from the whetstone, and down again. Korroc came in from his walk and sat down heavily and said nothing about anything. Nageru, facing west, did not move.

And Aravashnial sat across the fire with a cup in his hands, and looked into it, and did not drink.

---

The country beyond the Gray Road was worse.

Not more dangerous, or not much. Only emptier. The tufted grass gave out on the fourth day and there was nothing but stone after it, and dust, and long low ridges running north and south like the ribs of something very large that had died a long time ago and been left out in the weather. There was no water that was not in their skins. There was no wood but what they carried. There were tracks, sometimes, in the dust between the ridges, and Thane read them, and did not say what they were.

Nageru walked.

Caleth rode behind him and watched the back of his head and thought about the question. *How much do you trust him?* It was the wrong question. He had known it was the wrong question when it was asked. But he had been given it, and he could not put it down, and so he carried it along with everything else and turned it over at odd hours, the way a man worries at a loose tooth.

The trouble was not Nageru. The trouble was that somebody had wanted him to be asking it.

On the evening of the fifth day Korroc said, apropos of nothing, staring into the fire, "Fiendsplitter's been quiet."

"Is that bad?" said Thane.

"Don't know. Never had it be quiet before." The dwarf scratched his beard. "It's like having a dog that's stopped barking. You start wondering what it's seen."

Nobody had an answer for that.

On the sixth day there was nothing, and nothing, and in the evening a long red sunset with the dust standing up in it like smoke.

---

On the seventh morning, while they were packing, Korroc went round them.

He did not say anything about it. He went to Nageru first, where the monk was knotting his wraps, and put one bare hand flat on his shoulder, and stood like that a moment with his eyes shut. Then to Thane, who looked at the hand on his arm and up at his cousin's face and did not ask. Then to Caleth.

His hand was warm, through the coat. Caleth felt it go in.

He had felt it before. Many times — the thread Korroc kept on all of them, that thin warm line in the chest that pulled a little of every wound toward the dwarf whether the dwarf wanted it or not. This was not that. This was the thickening of it, the deliberate working the dwarf laid on when he meant to take *half*. Caleth had seen him lay it on one man before a fight, and once on two.

Never on three. Never in the morning, before there was anything to fight.

Korroc opened his eyes and took his hand away.

"Half," Caleth said quietly.

"Aye." Korroc went back to his horse and began, with deep reluctance, to tighten its girth. "Felt like the day for it."

Caleth looked at Nageru. Nageru was looking west.

---

In the afternoon they came to the trees.

He saw them first as a gray haze along the horizon, and thought it was dust. Then they were nearer and he saw that it was not dust; it was a forest, a sparse one, standing on the broken ground in no order at all, with a great deal of open space between the trunks. Then they were among it, and he saw what it was, and reined in.

The trees were stone.

Not carved. Grown. They stood as they had stood when they were alive — the bark, the knots, the places where a branch had broken off and the trunk had healed around the wound — and every inch of it had turned to gray stone. The branches went up bare against the sky in the shapes branches make, and there were no leaves, and when the wind went through them it made no sound at all. A forest that did not rustle.

His painter's eye went over it and could not decide whether it was the most beautiful thing he had seen in the Wound or the most terrible, and in the end it settled on both, which was what it usually settled on, out here.

Korroc had dismounted. He was standing with one hand on the nearest trunk, and his face had gone still and inward, the way it went at a forge, or a grave.

"Not stone," he said. "Not proper stone. It was a tree. It remembers being a tree." He took his hand away and wiped it on his breeches, which Caleth had never seen him do after touching stone in his life. "I don't like it."

Nageru had stopped too.

He stood among the gray trunks a little way ahead of them, and he was not looking west any more. He was looking at nothing — at the ground, at the air in front of him — with his head a little on one side, the way a man stands who has heard his name called from a long way off and is not yet sure he heard it.

"I feel that we are very close," he said.

They waited.

"If we keep on," said Nageru, "I think we will be there tonight. Perhaps an hour after the sun goes down."

Caleth looked at the sky. It was already going long and yellow in the west.

"Should we stop?" he said. "Camp here. Go in at first light, rested, and see what we are walking into."

Korroc shrugged. "Good either way, I think."

"Are you sure?" Aravashnial said. He had not spoken much all day. He sat the quiet bay a little behind the rest of them, with his hood up against the dust. "Demons love the dark."

Korroc turned his head and looked at him.

"Maybe we do too," he said.

He did not say it unkindly. He said it the way he said most things — flatly, a dwarf stating the grain of a piece of wood. But it sat there, among the stone trees, for a moment longer than it might have.

They went on.

---

The sun went down behind the trees, and the gray trunks went to black, and then to nothing. There was no moon. They went slowly, walking the horses, in the dark among the petrified forest, with Nageru ahead of them a pale shape that was sometimes there and sometimes not.

And then there were sounds.

Ahead. Not near. A long way through the trees, but carrying the way sound carries in still country at night: something heavy, striking stone. A shriek that was not an animal's. And under it, rising and falling, a voice.

Nageru had stopped. Thane slid off the dun mare without a word and handed Caleth the reins, and went forward, and was a shape for three steps and a shadow for one more and then was not there at all. Nageru went after him.

Caleth stood among the stone trees with two horses' reins in his hand and Aravashnial at his shoulder and Korroc's breath loud in the dark behind him, and listened to the voice.

It was shouting. He could not make out words, not at this distance, but he knew the shape of it at once: short, hard, carrying, each phrase cut off sharp at the end. It was a voice giving orders. He had heard sergeants on the walls of Drezen with that voice. He had heard it in the cavern by the Scar, from a templar he never saw the face of.

This was not a templar.

"Someone's in charge," Korroc muttered.

"Yes," said Caleth.

---

They were not gone long.

Thane came back first, out of nothing, at Caleth's elbow, so that the bay threw its head. Nageru came after. They stood close in the dark, the five of them, and Thane told it in a low flat voice, the way he told anything he had looked at.

A building. Old. Very old — the roof mostly gone, the walls standing. More temple than fort, by the look of it, though there was a wall round the whole of it that a fort would have been glad of. And on the far side of it, a tower.

"It is what I saw," Nageru said. His voice was very quiet. "In the dream. It is the place."

"There's a drake," said Thane. "With a rider. Up, circling. And down on the ground —" he paused — "worms. Big ones. White. Like maggots, if a maggot was the size of a horse. They're going at a building inside the wall. Hitting it. Trying to get in."

"How many worms?" said Caleth.

"Hard to tell," said Nageru. "At least two."

"And the voice?"

"Couldn't see who," said Thane. "Somewhere back of it all. Giving orders. Language I don't have."

Caleth gave Thane his reins back and walked forward a few paces, to the last of the trees, and looked.

He could see it now. A dark mass against a darker sky, low, long, with a broken line along its top where a roof had been. A wall around it. And beyond, rising above the rest, narrow and black, a tower. Something was moving in the air above it, a shape with wings, going round.

"Is that the bell tower?" he said, and pointed.

"We think so," said Nageru.

The voice came again, across the dark, and this time Caleth caught a word of it, and then another, and his skin went cold.

Abyssal.

---

"So," said Caleth. "Do we go to them, or do we let them come to us?"

Korroc shrugged. "Them to us means we don't have to run." He sounded pleased with it.

Caleth laughed. It surprised him; it came out of him short and low in the dark, the first time in a week. "You forget," he said, "that I can get us there."

Korroc stopped.

"Hmph," he said. "Aye."

But Caleth was looking at the tower, and the laugh had gone. He could see the building, and the wall, and the tower behind it. He could not see the *ground*. Not well. Not from here, at night, with no moon and the whole of it a mass of black on black. And a fold into a place you could not see was a fold into a wall, or a pit, or the middle of the thing you were trying to stand beside.

"I can't see a place to go," he said. "Not well enough."

"We should use the tower for cover," said Thane. "Put it between us and whatever's out there."

"I agree. But I can't see a clear place to land near it."

Thane studied the dark a moment longer. "There'll be a room at the bottom of it," he said. "Below the bell. We could pop in there and climb up."

Korroc turned and gave his cousin a look of such profound and silent reproach that Caleth could see it even in the dark. It was the look of a dwarf who has just heard his own blood propose that he climb a tower, at night, in plate, into a fight.

Nageru made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

"We could tie a rope around you, brother," he said. "Pull you up."

Korroc snorted through his beard. It was a dismissal so complete that it did not need words, and it did not get any.

"So what do we do?" said Caleth.

Korroc looked at him.

"Let's just go," he said. "Be what may."

Caleth did not like it. He did not like any fold he could not see the end of. But there was a voice out there giving orders in Abyssal, and worms the size of horses going at a door, and somewhere in all of it — if any of this was what it seemed — a woman with a bow and a torn wing who had asked Nageru by name to come.

He frowned. He nodded.

"There is a building near the tower," he said. "Low. Close against it. I can see the line of its roof." He paused to look a moment longer. "I hope there's still a ceiling on it."

They came in around him. Thane. Korroc. Nageru.

He looked at Aravashnial.

The elf stood by the horses with the bay's reins in one hand and his hood back, and he did not come in. He had nothing in his hands but the reins. Nothing across his back. And Caleth understood, in the time it took to look at him, what it would be to fold him into that — a man who could not light a candle, in the middle of whatever was in there — and saw that the elf understood it too.

"The horses," Aravashnial said.

"The horses," Caleth agreed.

It was not a plan. Nobody had made it. It was only what happened. He would think about that later.

The world folded.

---

The roof groaned.

It was the first thing. They came out onto it in a cluster in the dark and it took their weight and *complained* about it — a long low grinding under his boots, the sound of old mortar deciding whether this was the night. Caleth froze. Everybody froze. The groan went on a moment longer, and settled, and stopped.

He looked down at the stones he was standing on. Old flags, laid on old timbers, and a great deal of moss in the cracks, and in one place, a yard from Korroc's boot, a hole into black nothing where three of them had already given up.

He let out his breath and went to the edge.

It was a temple. Thane had been right. From up here he could see the shape of it — a long nave with the roof gone off most of it, the walls standing up out of rubble like the ribs of a ship, a court beyond, and the outer wall going round the whole. The tower rose at the far end of the building they stood on, close enough that its stones were near to touching, and went up past them into the dark. There was a window in it, well above their heads. Above that, the open top, where something hung.

And down in the court, a long way off across the rubble, the voice.

It was not giving orders now.

He listened. It was Abyssal — he had been certain at the trees; he was more certain now — and it had changed. It had gone low, and warm, and wheedling. It was saying the kind of things one says through a door to a frightened animal, or a child: *there now, there now, come out, nobody is angry.* He could not catch all the words. He did not need to. He had heard that voice in other mouths, and he had never once heard it mean what it said.

It was trying to coax someone out.

"Let's drop down," he said, low.

Korroc groaned. Not loudly. But Caleth heard it, and understood it, and saw the dwarf's hand go to the axe on his back and then away from it, as though it had burned him. Fiendsplitter was awake. Fiendsplitter, after a week of silence, was very much awake. Caleth could see it on Korroc's face: the axe had found what it had been waiting all week to find, and it was screaming, and a drop off a roof into a dark court was not at all what the dwarf had had in mind.

Thane had his hand on his belt.

On Terendelev's scale. A small silver light went up off it, soft, and Thane went up with it — straight up, slow, the way a bubble goes up through water — past the edge of the roof and along the face of the tower toward the window above. He reached it. He looked in. He hung there a moment, looking.

Then he turned his head and gave them a thumbs up, and took hold of the stone at either side of the window, and pulled himself inside, and was gone.

Korroc stared at the empty window.

"Where's he *going*?" he whispered. "I thought we were dropping down."

"We should see if we can find the succubus," said Nageru.

He said it reasonably. And then he went to the face of the tower, and put his hands on it, and went up it — not climbing, Caleth would have said, so much as declining to fall — and was at the window, and through it, and gone after Thane.

Korroc shook his head.

"Not a sentence," he said, "I ever thought I'd hear. In that order."

And then everybody froze again.

Above them, wings. Close. The drake was coming round, low over the tower, and Caleth heard the great leather beat of it and the creak of tack, and then the rider's voice, very near, calling out in Abyssal to somebody on the ground.

He held still.

"He just asked," Caleth breathed, when the wings had gone on past, "what was in our direction."

Korroc looked at him.

Caleth went to the inner edge of the roof, where it fell away into the nave. He sat. He swung his legs over. He hung by his hands from the old stone a moment, feeling for the drop, and let go.

It was further than he had thought. He landed hard in rubble and rolled and came up with Radiance in his hand before he had finished rolling, in the roofless nave of an old temple with broken pillars all around him, and nobody there.

A silver light above him. Korroc, drifting down on his own scale — upright, armored, slow as a seed, with his face set in the expression of a dwarf who has accepted that he is going to be lowered off a roof by a dragon's gift for the rest of his life and is learning to live with it. He came down beside Caleth with a soft clink of plate on stone.

Then his head came up.

"Caleth," he said. "Something's near. It's coming."

"How do you kn—"

A worm came round the corner.

It was enormous. White — the dead white of something that has never seen the sun — and fat, and glistening, and it came round the broken end of the wall at the far side of the nave on a rolling wave of its own body, with a sound like wet cloth being dragged across stone. It had no eyes that he could see. It had a mouth.

"I can tell," said Korroc flatly. "I can feel life. That's life." He shifted his shield. "Of a sort."

---

High above them, the bell rang.

He would remember it. He would remember it longer than anything else that happened that night, longer than the fight, longer than what came after. One note. Clear. *Sweet* — that was the only word for it, a word he would not have thought to use of any bell — and so high and so pure that it seemed less to be heard than to be *arrived at*, the way one arrives at the end of a long proof and sees, all at once, that it is true.

It went out over the ruins. It went out over the stone trees. It went through Caleth where he stood, and he felt something go with it — a wave, a long cool washing, like the first breath of air in a room that has been shut up for years. It passed over him and through him and out, across the court, across the wall, into the dark.

For a moment the whole fortress was silent.

The coaxing voice had stopped.

Then, from the other end of the nave, a demon came round the wall and charged him.

---

He knew what it was by the shape. Long, low, many-jointed, armored in plates like a beetle's, with a long barbed tail coming up and over behind it like a scorpion's — and the mouth, opening, opening further than a mouth should, coming at him across the rubble at a speed something that size had no business having.

Korroc's shield was there.

He did not see Korroc move. There was simply a shield, between the mouth and him, and the mouth closing on it with a crack like a felled tree, and sliding off. The claws came after, left and right — and went wide, both of them, as Korroc turned the shield and Caleth turned his hip. And then the tail, over the top, the barb coming down at his face.

Radiance was in the way.

He had not decided to put it there. The blade had gone up as though it knew the shape of the strike before the strike began, and the barb rang off it, and he turned his wrist, and the blade came down and in, past the shield's edge, and found the place between two of the plates on the thing's chest.

It went in. The holy light went off it. The demon screamed.

He took it out and put it in again. And again. Three times, quick and deep, each one in at the seam where the armor met, and he felt the blade *know* the demon each time it bit — that small cold shock up the arm that he had felt for the first time in a cavern by the Scar, the sword saying *this one, this one is what you think it is.*

Above, steel. Shouting. The beat of wings, coming down hard on the tower.

He could not see the tower from here. He saw only the results — the drake coming round, low, its rider standing in the stirrups with a long lance, driving it at the bell-chamber window — and then, beside him, Korroc grunted and staggered, and two long gashes opened down the dwarf's arm out of nothing at all, through the mail under his plate, and began to bleed.

*Half.* Somebody up there had been run through twice, and Korroc had taken half of it, standing in a nave a hundred feet away, without a hand laid on him.

"Thane," the dwarf said through his teeth. And did not look up.

---

Something else came round the corner.

It was bigger than the beetle-thing and much fatter and it smelled — Saint's mercy, it *smelled*, it reached him before the thing did, a stink of marsh and rot and something worse — and it came at them on two great legs and swung two great arms. A toad, if a toad had been made to stand like a man by somebody who hated toads. Its hide was warty and running wet. He knew what it was. He had read of them, and he had not wanted to meet one.

It came into his reach and he met it. Radiance went in under the arm and the hide parted like rotten cloth and the light went in after it. And the thing struck him back, one claw across the chest, through the coat, and he felt the armor take most of it, the close hard skin of force the wardstone had left lying over him since Kenabres, and felt the rest go in, hot.

Korroc turned.

His boots were glowing. Caleth saw the light come up off them, gold, and then the dwarf was simply *across* — not running; the ground between him and the toad-thing had been folded up and put away — and Fiendsplitter came down.

The demon got out of the way of the first stroke. Caleth would not have believed it, of something that size, but it did — swayed back on its great legs, and the axe went past its face. Then the second came, and the third, and the fourth, and they did not go past anything. Caleth watched the axe go into it three times faster than Korroc had any right to swing a thing that was not his, and watched the dwarf's beard part in a grin that was not his either.

The demon screamed. It screamed like a thing that had been told it was going home, and did not want to, and could not stop it.

Then it was not there. Only the stink.

Something blotted out the stars.

The drake had come over the gap in the roof above them. It hung there a heartbeat, wings spread, and its throat worked, and it breathed down into the nave.

Acid. A great green-gray gout of it, falling, opening as it fell into a cloud. Caleth was moving before it reached them — sideways, low, a long step and a turn, the bravo's way of being somewhere else — and most of it went past him. Most. It caught his shoulder and his hair and he smelled them both start to burn.

Korroc took it all. He was standing in the middle of the nave with his axe still up, and the cloud came down over him like a bucket of slops, and Caleth heard him roar.

And then the worm.

It had been coming the whole time, rolling across the rubble, and now it reached them and reared up — up, and up, more of it than there had seemed to be, a white glistening column of it over his head — and came down at him.

He watched it come.

He let his weight settle onto his back foot. He let the hip turn. He let the whole of himself go loose and light, the way he had been taught a hundred times and had managed perhaps one time in ten — and the worm came down where he had been, a finger's width from his face, so close that he felt the wind of it and the wet, and he was not there.

Its mouth struck the flagstones and cracked them.

---

There were legs coming over the outer wall.

He saw them at the edge of his sight and did not at first understand what he was seeing. Long. Black. Jointed. *Many.* Coming up over the top of the old wall on the far side of the court one after another, feeling for purchase, the way a hand feels along a shelf in the dark. Very large. Larger than anything a wall that height should have had to keep out.

Spider legs.

He thought of the plain outside Drezen, and three made things the size of bull elephants, and put the thought away. *Not that. Not now.*

Another worm came up out of the rubble at the edge of the nave — not round a corner; *up*, out of the ground, out of a pile of fallen stone as though it had been lying in it all along — and it did not charge. It reared, and something gathered at its blind wet head, and a ray of black light went out from it and struck Korroc in the chest.

The dwarf rocked. Caleth saw him shrug it off — most of it; the stone in the dwarf's blood would not let most things in — but saw also the thing it left behind, a sag in the shoulders, a heaviness in the arm that held the axe. As though a day's labor had been laid on him in the space of a breath.

Above, at the tower, the drake was falling.

He saw it come down past the bell-chamber window, wings folded wrong, turning over once in the air. It hit the ground at the tower's foot with a sound he felt through his boots. Something had killed it up there, quickly. Something with knives, he thought.

Its rider had not gone down with it. The rider had *wings* — Caleth saw them open, dark against the dark — and hung in the air beyond the tower top, just out of reach.

*Incubus.*

The drake was not finished falling. It had come down against the roof of the building beside the tower, the one they had folded onto, and the roof, which had complained once tonight already, gave up. The whole of it. The drake went *through* it, into the nave, in a roaring avalanche of old stone and old timber and a dead drake, and the dust went up past Caleth in a gray wave.

He came out of the dust with his eyes streaming, and the beetle-thing was on him again.

He had been watching the tower. That was his fault. He saw it come at the last moment, the mouth and the claws and the tail, and he got the hip turned and the weight back and the step aside — and it was not enough. The bite missed. The first claw missed. The second claw hit him across the ribs and the third hit him high on the back, and the tail came over and the barb went into his shoulder, and he felt all of it, every inch, and something warm began to run down inside his shirt.

He staggered. He did not fall.

Far above, a voice he did not know said a word in Abyssal, and a wave of something gray and heavy and hopeless went out from the top of the tower — he felt the edge of it from down here, a pull at the bottom of the heart, *why bother, why any of it* — and stopped. Broke off. As though it had hit something that would not have it.

Then a sound of blows, very fast, from the tower top. Four of them.

The incubus did not come down. It was simply not in the sky any longer.

*Nageru,* Caleth thought, and almost smiled, with blood in his mouth.

---

He went down into himself.

There was a place there. The wardstone had put it in all of them, in a room above Kenabres, and he had spent a year learning how far down it went. It went a long way. He reached into it now, past the pain in his back and his ribs, and took hold, and with his other hand he took out a wand.

He did not have to think about what he was doing. That was the gift of it, the thing his teachers had never understood and the Spire had first mocked and then quietly approved. A spell was not a recipe. It was a structure, a set of joints and loads, and if you understood where the joints were you could take it apart and put it together another way.

He took the fire out of it.

He put the cold in.

The spell went off in his hand and what came out of it should have been flame. It was not. It was a small bright bead of white, flying, and it went out across the nave and burst.

Winter. A great white roaring flower of it, opening, the air itself crackling and freezing as it bloomed, frost running out across the rubble in a ring — and it went *around* Korroc. Caleth watched it do it. Watched the cold come rolling over the dwarf's place and part, part like water round a stone, and close again on the other side, so that Korroc stood in a perfect round of untouched air in the middle of a storm of ice and did not even have frost on his beard.

The beetle-thing was not so fortunate. It was in the heart of it. Caleth saw the ice take it, saw the plates of its armor go white and crack, saw it try to turn and find that its legs had frozen to the flags beneath it.

Then it was gone. Banished. Gone home in a puff of black smoke and a smell of burned frost.

Korroc did not look round. He had gone somewhere inside himself too; Caleth saw the light come up in him, gold, from the chest out, and saw half the gashes on the dwarf's arm close over as though somebody had drawn a thumb along them. Then he turned on the worm.

The worm was still coiled where it had struck at Caleth, its mouth in the broken flagstones, and it was just lifting its head when Fiendsplitter came down on it.

Four times.

Caleth had seen Korroc fight a great many things. He did not think he had ever seen him fight anything quite so thoroughly. The axe went in and came out and went in, and white wet stuff went everywhere, over the rubble, over the dwarf, over a fair amount of Caleth, and then there was nothing under the axe but a smoking stain on the stone. Gone, the way the demons went.

*So it was a demon too,* Caleth thought. *Of a kind.* He filed it.

---

Lights.

They came out of the walls.

That was what it looked like. Small pale lights, three or four of them, drifting out of the broken stonework of the nave as though the old stones had been holding them in all this time and had just let go. They bobbed. They wandered. They came drifting over the rubble toward the place where the dead drake lay half-buried under the fallen roof, and they hung over it, flickering, the way moths hang over a lamp.

Caleth knew what they were. He swore under his breath, in a way he did not usually permit himself.

"Will-o'-wisps," he said. "Drawn by the dying."

"Wonderful," said Korroc.

The spider legs had stopped coming over the wall.

Caleth looked for them. They were not there. The top of the wall was empty, and the court below it was empty, and for a moment he thought the thing had gone back the way it had come —

It was in front of Korroc.

It had not crossed the court. It had not climbed down. It had simply *been* there — one moment the wall, the next the dwarf, with nothing in between — and it was enormous. A spider. The size of an elephant, near enough, black and jointed and glossy, with a body like a siege-tower and a cluster of eyes catching the wisp-light, and two great forelegs that ended not in feet but in hooks. Long ones. Curved like scythes.

*Not those spiders,* he told himself, very clearly. *Not the made ones. A demon. A spider demon.* He knew the name. He had read the name in a book on the plates of the Abyss and had shut the book on it.

The bite missed Korroc badly. The dwarf turned his shield and the fangs went past.

The hooks did not miss.

They came in from both sides, fast, faster than the bite, and Caleth heard the sound they made going into Korroc's armor — not a ring, not a clang; a *tearing*, like canvas, like a sail splitting in a gale. The left one hooked under the rim of the shield and *pulled*, and the shield went out of Korroc's grip and away across the nave, end over end, into the rubble. The right one went in at the dwarf's shoulder, under the edge of the plate, and hooked, and pulled.

And the armor came off him.

Caleth stood and watched it happen and could not, for one long moment, make his mind accept it. The plate that had taken Torag's mark. The armor that decided what it belonged to. The hooks went in at the straps and the joints, unerringly, the way a butcher's knife goes in at the joints of a carcass, and they *peeled* it — the pauldron, the breastplate, the long back plate, coming away from the dwarf in pieces and flying off into the dark — and then Korroc was standing in the nave of a dead temple in his arming coat and his mail and his blood, with an axe in one hand and nothing in the other, looking up at a spider the size of a house.

The acid came down on both of them before Caleth had finished understanding it.

The second drake. It had come round over the nave, low, and spat — a ball of it this time, dense and green — and it fell into the bent air around Caleth and *changed*. The working he had laid before the fold was still on him; he had nearly forgotten it. What came in did not arrive as what it was. The ball of acid went to fire in the air above him, a soft rolling bloom of flame, and he was already turning out from under it, and what little of it touched him he barely felt. Korroc twisted aside — without his shield, without his plate, he moved like a younger dwarf than Caleth had ever seen him — and the fire scorched his back and did not take him.

---

He had perhaps the space of a breath to think.

He thought: the spider will take the dwarf apart before he can reach it. He thought: there are three lights coming, and they are not coming to look. He thought: I have two good spells left that will matter, and the hag is somewhere I cannot see, and Thane and Nageru are at the top of a tower with a bell.

The lights came at him.

Two of them at once, flicking in out of the dark, and he felt them strike — not a blow; a *bite* of lightning, a snapping crack under the skin, his teeth jarring together with it. He turned Radiance into the nearer and felt the blade go through it as though through smoke, and the light flared and dimmed and did not go out. The third went at Korroc.

Korroc did not look at it. Korroc had put his hand down.

Caleth saw him do it. Saw the dwarf, unarmored, with a spider over him and a light biting at his face, bend his knees and put one bare hand flat against the broken flagstones of the nave. Saw his eyes close. And saw the stone come up into him — that was the only way he could have described it; something going from the ground into the dwarf through his palm, as though the old temple had a heart in it somewhere and it had just begun, very slowly, to beat in Korroc's chest. The dwarf's whole stance changed. It *sank*. He was not standing on the floor any longer. He was part of it.

And then he burned.

Caleth had seen it before. Once, in a cave under Kenabres, in the worst hour of his life until then, and before that in Kenabres. He had never forgotten it and he had never seen it again. The dwarf went up in gold. Not fire, or not only fire — a living shape of it, tall and broad and roaring, a smith's furnace with the door thrown open, a dwarf made out of the heart of a forge. The heat came off him in a wave. And the wave was *warm* — it went out of him across the nave and over Caleth and into Caleth, and the gashes along his ribs and his back pulled closed, and the lightning-ache went out of his teeth, and he could breathe again.

The spider reared.

It came up on its back legs over the burning dwarf, the hooks going up and back for the stroke that would come down through the fire and finish it —

Caleth spoke.

One phrase. He had been saving it. He flung his hand out toward Korroc, and the air around the dwarf and around himself went *hard*. A sphere — clear as glass, invisible except where the wisp-light caught its curve — closed over both of them like a bell jar coming down over a candle.

The hooks came down on it.

They struck and slid. They struck again and slid again, scraping, a long screaming sound like a knife drawn down a slate, and the spider reared back and struck a third time and could not get in.

Caleth stood inside the sphere beside a dwarf made of fire, and breathed.

He looked round. The sphere had closed on more than he had meant it to. Three of the wisps were in it with them, bobbing near the curve, flickering. Shut in. He watched them realize it.

*Well,* he thought. *They are Korroc's problem now.*

---

He did not stay.

He could not. There was nothing more he could do for Korroc from in here, and a great deal he could do from out there, and the dwarf had all the fire he needed. Caleth found the place in himself that folded short distances and reached for it — and the sphere was not a wall to that, as he had half known it would not be — and stepped.

The world folded small. He came out of it on the far side of the nave, among the broken pillars, with the spider between him and the sphere and the drake coming round overhead with its rider on its back.

All three of them. In a line.

He went down into himself again, deeper this time. Past the place he had drawn on for the wand. He took hold of the working before he cast it and *pushed* — more into it than it was made to hold, the way you pack a charge too tight, and the spell shuddered in him with it.

He flung out his hand.

The cone went out of him white and screaming. It was not the cold of the wand's flower. It was the cold of the far north in the dead of the year, the cold that takes a man's breath and his fingers and does not give them back, and it went across the nave in a great widening wedge and took the spider full in the face and side, and went *up*, and took the drake.

The drake stopped flying. It simply stopped — wings locked white in the air, the whole long body frosted over in an instant — and it fell, and hit the rubble, and did not get up.

Its rider was not on it when it struck. Its rider had gone thin and black on the way down, and was gone.

The spider staggered.

It was not dead. Caleth saw that at once. But the white had gone into it, deep, frosting the great glossy body, cracking it, and two of its legs on the near side had gone stiff and wrong. It turned toward him, slowly, dragging them.

The wisps found him first.

Three of them now — the third must have come round the outside of the sphere — streaking at him across the nave, and the lightning came off them in snapping threads and caught him, and caught him, and he felt his heart stumble once in his chest and catch itself.

Inside the sphere, Korroc had picked up his shield.

Caleth did not know how. The shield had gone into the rubble on the far side of the nave; he had seen it go. And yet there it was on the dwarf's arm, inside the sphere — the sphere had closed over the place where it fell, he realized; the dwarf had only had to bend down — and Korroc was speaking, and something went over him like a skin of water, blue-white, and the lightning off the two shut-in wisps went into it and did not come out the other side. Then the gold came up in him again, brighter, and the last of the wounds on him closed.

The spider turned back to him. It struck at the sphere, at the place where the dwarf stood, three times, four, the hooks screaming down the curve, and could not get in.

*It cannot decide,* Caleth thought. *It wants him and it cannot have him, and it wants me and does not know how to reach me.*

He gave it no more time to decide. He lifted his hand and spoke, and three rays of fire went out of his fingers one after another, quick, red, true — fire, this time, real fire; he had taken the cold out of it and given it back what it was born with — and struck the spider in the frosted place on its side where the ice had cracked it.

Ice and then fire. A thing can take one or the other.

The spider shuddered from one end of itself to the other. Then it was not there.

---

He did not stop to look.

He was moving already, away from the wisps, across the nave toward the gap in its far wall where the court opened out beyond — because something was happening out there; he had been hearing it for some time under everything else, the way one hears a second conversation in a crowded room. Blows. A voice, a woman's, very large, raised in fury. And another sound, which he knew: a sound like a lamp bursting, white, again and again.

Nageru's fists.

The wisps came after him. They caught him in the back as he went, the lightning snapping through him from shoulder to heel, once, twice. He barely felt it now. He had taken worse tonight from things that meant it more.

Behind him, inside the sphere, he heard the axe.

He glanced back once. Korroc — still fire, still gold — brought Fiendsplitter down on one of the shut-in lights, and it came apart like a soap-bubble and was gone. He swung at the second. Three strokes. Gone.

Caleth reached the gap in the wall and looked through.

---

The court beyond was full of ruin and moonless dark, and in the middle of it was a hag.

He had heard her described. He had listened to Nageru tell her, in the study, in the elf's voice by the fire at the fane. It had not prepared him. She was enormous. Taller than the gate she stood in front of; she stood in it hunched, the way a tall man stands in a low doorway, with her long arms hanging nearly to her knees and her claws catching what little light there was the way iron catches it. Her hair was a gray rope. Her face was —

He did not look at her face for long. The smile went too far across it. Nageru had said so, and Nageru had been right, and once you had seen it you could not make yourself stop seeing it.

The door behind her was the tower door.

Nageru was on her.

He was on her the way Caleth had seen him on the glabrezu in the north court at Drezen, inside her reach, against her, in the place where a thing that size could not easily strike at a man. His fists were alight. The holy fire went off them every time they landed, white-gold, bursting, so that the whole court lit and went dark and lit again like a storm. Caleth saw two blows go into her. Three. Four.

And he saw them *not land* — not as they should have. The light went into her and something in her turned it. She was speaking, the hag, the whole time. Not a spell; Caleth would have known a spell. Words. A long low stream of them in a voice like a millstone, and he could not hear them all, but he heard enough. She was cursing Nageru's god. Naming him, and naming what she would do to him, and *meaning* it — and the holy fire on Nageru's fists dimmed by the smallest fraction each time she said the name, as though the words were a hand closing over a lamp.

Behind her, three knife-wounds opened in her back.

Out of nothing. One, two, three, low, deep, in at the kidneys, the way Thane always went in — at the seams of things. Caleth could see him. He could always see him. A small grim dwarf-shape, half-there, at the hag's back with his knives in, and his teeth bared.

She *screamed*. It was the loudest thing Caleth had heard all night, louder than the bell, and nothing like it. She turned, her whole great body coming round, her head swinging this way and that, looking for the thing that had hurt her and finding air.

Then she gave up looking and went for the one she could see.

She cast — he felt it go out of her, something cold and clever — and it went over Nageru and over Thane and found nothing to take hold of in either of them, and was gone. She snarled. She threw out her hands, and a wave went out from her, black, sick, the opposite of Korroc's gold — and then she struck.

Twice. The iron claws, across Nageru's chest and his side.

And then she had him.

*Do not let her get both arms around you.* He heard Aravashnial say it, across a fire, a week ago. She did not get both. She got one — one long arm round the monk's body, the claws going *in*, into his back, hooking there, holding him to her — and Caleth saw Nageru's face over her shoulder go white with it.

Nageru struck her with the other fist. Down, into the place where her neck met her shoulder. The holy light went off like a lamp bursting. He struck again.

Caleth was running.

He did not remember deciding to. He had dismissed the sphere behind him — he felt it go, a small release in the back of the head — and he was running across the court through the rubble with Radiance in his hand and the wisps snapping at his back, and the court was much too wide. He could see a worm, another of the white ones, coming out of the dark at the court's edge toward the hag and the monk, rolling, its blind head lifted. He could see Thane's knives go up again. He could see —

The hag fell.

She went down all at once, the way a tower goes down, straight and then over, and the ground shook with her. Thane's knives had gone in a fourth time, a fifth, somewhere in the dark at her back — Caleth did not see where — and her great body went to the flagstones and lay there.

Her claws were still in Nageru.

He saw the monk go down with her, dragged down by the hooked hand in his back, and saw him land on his knees beside her, and saw him turn — hard, all at once, in the grip — and *tear* himself free. The claws came out of him. Caleth saw his face as they did. Then Nageru put his own hand flat on his own chest, and closed his eyes, and the warmth his order had taught him to give away came up in him, and he gave a little of it to himself.

The court was very quiet.

Caleth slowed. He was perhaps fifty yards off still, and the wisps were coming after him, and he could hear Korroc somewhere back in the nave finishing the last of them — the axe; a pop like a bubble; silence —

And something went past him in a streak of gold.

He felt the wind of it. He had time to see that it was Korroc — still made of fire, roaring, his boots blazing under him — and then the dwarf was across the rest of the court in a single stride and skidding to a halt beside Nageru on the flagstones, with a wand out in his burning hand. He touched the monk with it. Nageru's back pulled closed.

And then Korroc turned around and looked at Caleth, and Caleth saw his face through the fire, and it was a face he had not seen on the dwarf before.

"*Caleth!*" Korroc roared. "You need to find Aravashnial! He's being attacked!"

---

Caleth's eyes went wide.

He did not stop running. He did not slow. He ran the last of the court at a sprint he had not known was in him, with Korroc's face in front of him and the dwarf's words going round inside his skull like a loose stone in a drum — *being attacked* — and he understood, as he ran, how the dwarf knew. The thread. The thin warm line Korroc had laid on the elf in the infirmary the day he walked home, and had never taken off. Some wound had just come down it. More than one.

He reached them. He put out his left hand and took Korroc by the burning arm, and felt the heat of it and did not care. He put out his right hand and took hold of the empty air where Thane was standing, and found the dwarf's shoulder exactly where he knew it would be.

He did not look at Nageru. He did not have time.

The world folded.

---

They came out among the stone trees.

The place where they had left the horses. He knew it by the shape of the trunks — that one, split; that one, leaning — and by the scuffed ground at their feet where five people and four horses had stood in a ring around him an hour ago.

There were no horses.

There was no Aravashnial.

There was nothing at all. The stone trees standing up into the dark. The scuffed dust. A long gouge in the ground, going away, where a horse had bolted with its hooves dug in. And the silence of a forest that did not rustle.

Caleth stood in the middle of it and could not, for the space of one breath, think.

*Gone.* The word came up in him from somewhere very deep and very old. *Gone, again. You left him with the horses because it was the sensible thing, because he could not cast, because it was what happened —*

"*There.*"

Korroc. Still burning. One great gold arm flung out, pointing, off through the trees to the north.

"He's over there," the dwarf said. "He's alive. That way."

They ran.

Caleth saw them before he saw anything else: two shapes under a tree, white, glistening, the size of horses, rearing up against its trunk. Worms. The same kind. They were going up at the tree the way he had seen them going at the building in the fortress — butting, striking, their blind heads lifting and coming down against the stone bark, as though they could knock it over if they kept at it long enough.

He could not see the elf.

He did not stop to look. He threw out his hand as he ran, and fire went out of it — not the rays; the thing that leaps — and it went to the first worm and *jumped*, from it to the second, a chain of red light hanging for a moment between them in the dark. Both of them screamed, if a worm can scream. Both turned.

And Thane was there.

Caleth saw him, as he always saw him: a dwarf-shape made of almost nothing, arriving. The knives went in once. The first worm folded down on itself into the dust and lay in a heap, collapsed, as though it had been a bag of something and the bag had been cut. The knives went in twice more. The second worm went the same way.

The forest was silent again.

Caleth stood under the tree with his hand still out and the fire going out of his fingers, breathing hard, and looked up.

Aravashnial was in the branches.

He was perhaps twenty feet up, wedged into the crook where three great stone limbs came out of the trunk together, and he had his arms wrapped round the nearest of them, and his cloak was torn, and there was blood — a great deal of it, dark in the dark — running down one leg and dripping off his boot into the dust.

He looked down at the three of them.

Then, slowly, carefully, the way he had done everything for a week, he began to climb down.

---

"I ran," he said.

He was sitting at the foot of the tree with his back to the trunk and his torn leg out in front of him, and his face was gray, and his voice was perfectly steady.

"They came out of the ground," he said. "Over there. While you were gone. The horses broke before I could hold them; I think they must have smelled them coming. I let the reins go." He looked at his own hand, as though it had done this without asking him. "And then they came at me. And I ran. And there was a tree." He looked up at it. "So I climbed it."

"You climbed a stone tree," said Thane. He had become visible again at some point; he stood a little apart, looking up at the crook where the elf had been, and at the trunk, and at the elf. "With that leg."

"The leg was not like this when I began," said Aravashnial. "One of them reached me as I was going up." He paused. "I climbed faster after that."

Caleth knelt.

He put his hand on the elf's leg, over the wound, through the torn cloth. It was bad — a long ragged bite, deep, the kind of wound that does not stop on its own — and he felt the blood warm under his palm. He closed his eyes. He reached for the other thing, the thing that was not the wardstone and not the spellbook, the thing Shelyn had given him and that he gave away so seldom that it always surprised him a little to find it still there.

It was there.

He felt it go out of his hand and into the wound. Felt the blood slow under his fingers, and slow, and stop.

He opened his eyes.

Aravashnial was looking at him. Very close. The long fine face, gray with pain, and the eyes very steady, and something in them that Caleth could not read.

"Thank you," the elf said.

Caleth did not trust himself to answer. He took his hand away and stood up.

Behind him, the gold went out of Korroc. Caleth heard it go — a long low sigh, like a forge-fire banked for the night — and when he turned round there was only a dwarf standing among the stone trees, in a torn arming coat with his mail hanging off one shoulder, very tired and very short and very much himself. He looked down at his own hands and flexed them.

"Right," he said. "Well. Horses are gone, then."

"Horses are gone," said Thane.

Korroc looked off into the dark where the long gouge in the dust ran away between the trees, and his face did something complicated.

"I'll not pretend," he said, "that I'll miss mine."

---

They walked back.

It was a long way, in the dark, and they did not hurry it. Aravashnial walked between Caleth and Thane with one arm over Caleth's shoulder and his weight coming down on it every second step. Korroc went ahead, and stopped from time to time, and bent, and picked things up. A pauldron, in the rubble at the edge of the court. A greave. The long back plate, lying face-down in a drift of fallen stone with its straps torn through. The breastplate last, in the nave, with the hammer and the anvil on it gleaming up at him out of the dust as though nothing at all had happened.

Korroc picked it up and turned it over in his hands, and looked at the straps — torn, every one, the leather and the buckles ripped clean out — and then at the plate, and ran his thumb along the edge of it.

"Metal's fine," he said. "Not a mark on it." He sounded faintly outraged. "Just the straps. It took the straps off me. Like a man skinning a rabbit."

"It was very efficient," said Caleth.

"It *was*," said Korroc, with feeling. He tucked the breastplate under his arm with the rest. "Straps'll mend. I'll do them in Drezen." He glanced at Caleth sidelong. "Don't tell anyone I fought the back half of that in my underthings."

"I would not dream of it."

"Thane will."

"Thane will," Thane agreed.

---

Nageru was waiting for them at the foot of the tower.

He was sitting on the step before the tower door, beside the long dark heap of the hag, with his hands on his knees. His wraps were torn open down one side and dark with blood, and there were long half-healed furrows across his back where the claws had been. He looked up as they came across the court, and saw Aravashnial, and something in his face that had been held very still let go.

He stood up. He did not say anything. He only nodded at the door behind him.

They went in.

---

It was a small round room at the bottom of the tower, with a stair going up the inside of the wall into the dark. There had been a barricade against the door — Caleth could see the remains of it pushed aside, old timbers and a broken bench and a stone that it must have taken somebody a great effort to move. A lamp had been lit on the floor.

And beside the lamp, with her back to the wall and a bow across her knees, sat a woman with wings.

She stood up as they came in.

Caleth stopped in the doorway.

He had seen her before. Not in waking life — none of them had — but in the long room of Aravashnial's study, in Nageru's voice, telling it: black hair, very pale skin, the black horns curving back from her brow. A red cloth knotted at her waist. Dried blood down one arm from shoulder to wrist. One wing, half-furled behind her, was torn through near its edge where an arrow had gone.

All of it. Exactly. As though the dream had been a painting and he had walked into the room where the painter had sat to make it.

She was beautiful. He saw it as he saw everything, with the eye that could not help itself — the line of the jaw, the set of the shoulders, the way she held the bow, low, not threatening, not quite letting go of it either. She was beautiful the way such creatures were always beautiful: perfectly, deliberately, as though somebody had studied what a man would find beautiful and then built it.

He had stood in front of a face like that before. A face like that, wearing *Iomedae's* — and he had known, *known*, what it was, and walked toward it anyway.

*Knowing wasn't enough.*

He did not move from the doorway.

"Thank you," the succubus said. Her voice was low and a little hoarse, as though she had not used it much. She looked from one of them to the next. "Thank you. All of you. For —" she made a small gesture at the door, at the court, at the heap outside it — "for helping me."

Nageru had come in behind them. He went past Caleth, and stopped in the middle of the room, and looked directly at her, and opened his mouth —

"This tower," Caleth said, "is hallowed."

Everyone looked at him.

He had not meant to interrupt. He had not, he found, meant to say anything. But he had been looking — he had been looking since the door — and he had felt it when he crossed the threshold, the way one feels the warmth of a hearth on walking into a cold house. It was in the stones. In the air. It went up the stair over their heads, and it was strongest here, at the foot, and he knew where it came from. He had felt it go out of the bell over the whole fortress, an hour ago, like the first breath of air in a shut room. It had settled here. In the tower. Round the bell, and down, and nowhere else.

"Holy ground," he said. "Consecrated. By the bell, I think. Anything evil that sat in this room would be suffering for it." He looked at the woman by the lamp. "She has been sitting in it. For some while, by the look of it."

He paused.

"She is obviously not being hurt."

Nageru looked at him. Then he turned back to the succubus and looked at her, for a long moment, closely, the way Caleth had seen him look at a stranger's breathing across a fire. Then he nodded, slowly.

Caleth came into the room.

He did not sheathe Radiance. He held it low, point down, at his side, where she could see it and where it was not pointed at anything. He stopped an arm's length from her and looked into her face.

"So," he said. "You are a demon."

She did not look away.

"Why," said Caleth, "should we not send you back to your home plane? With all the others?"

---

"Look at her, though."

Korroc. He had come in last, with his armor under his arms in a great awkward bundle, and he set it down by the door with a clank and straightened, and Caleth heard what was coming off the dwarf's back as he did it.

Fiendsplitter was screaming.

Caleth could not hear it. He had never been able to. But he could see it — in Korroc's jaw, in the way the dwarf's hand kept going to the haft over his shoulder and stopping, in the tightness round his eyes. The axe had found a demon. The axe was standing in a room with a demon and could not understand why it was not being *used*. And Korroc, while Caleth watched, reached back and drew it — not to swing; to put it away. He unslung his pack and jammed the axe-head down into it, haft and all, as far as it would go, and pulled the flap over the top and buckled it.

"*Quiet,*" he said to the pack. "I'm *thinking*."

Then he looked at the succubus.

"Look at her," he said again. "She's not evil. Is she." He frowned, as though the frown were a tool and he were fitting it to a joint. "Something's happened to her."

Caleth looked at her.

"How," he said, "is that possible?"

"Aye," said Korroc. He turned the frown on her directly. "How *is* that possible, lass?"

The succubus looked at the four of them. At the dwarf with his armor in a heap by the door and his axe in his pack. At the half-elf with the holy weapon held low. At the monk. At the other dwarf, against the wall, with his arms folded. And at Aravashnial, who had come in under Thane's arm and sat down on the bottom step of the stair with his torn leg out before him, and who was looking at her with his whole face very still.

She sat down by the lamp.

"I came to the Wound," she said, "a long time ago."

---

She told it simply. Caleth had expected — he did not know what he had expected. Something practiced. A creature of her kind would have a story ready the way a cutpurse has a smile ready, and he had braced himself to hear it and to listen through it for the place where the seams showed.

He did not hear any seams. He heard a woman telling a thing she did not like telling, in order, as plainly as she could, with her eyes on the lamp.

She had come to the Wound nearly eighty years ago — Caleth did the sum without meaning to; long before he was born — and she had been what she was. She did not soften it. She did not ask them to. She had done what her kind did, for a long time, and done it well.

And then there had been a priestess.

A priestess of Desna. She said the name and Caleth saw Nageru's head come up. Arueshalae had found her, and done to her what she did, and drained her nearly to death — and then, lying beside the dying woman in the dark, on a whim, she had done a thing she had never done before.

"We do not sleep," she said. "We do not need to. And so we do not dream." She turned her hands over in her lap. "I wondered what it was like. That was all. I was curious. I reached into her thoughts while she slept, to see." A long pause. "And it pulled me in."

Into the dream. Into the place that dreams are. And the priestess had died, there, in the middle of it, and Arueshalae had found herself — trapped, she said; caught, the way a fly is caught in amber — in a dream with no dreamer.

And something had been watching.

"She had seen what I did," Arueshalae said. "To her priestess. She had seen all of it." The lamp moved, and the shadows of her horns moved on the wall. "I thought she would destroy me. I would have, in her place. I would have done it slowly."

She did not.

She reached into the succubus — Arueshalae laid one pale hand flat against her own breastbone, to show where — into the deepest place there was, the place where a demon keeps the small hard seed of whatever mortal soul it was before the Abyss made it over. And she woke it.

"I remembered," Arueshalae said. Her voice had gone very quiet. "Everything. All at once. Who I had been, before. What I had wanted. Every dream I had ever had, when I was a thing that could dream, and how none of them had come to anything." She closed her eyes. "And she said in my ear: *Even demons can dream.*"

Nobody spoke.

Caleth looked at Nageru. The monk had not moved. He stood in the middle of the round room with his torn wraps and his bloody back, and his face had gone completely still, and Caleth — who had seen that face hold still over a dragon and a demon and a dying boy — saw something go across it that he had no name for.

She had woken, she said, with all of it in her. The memories. The shame. And a thing she had not had before, that she did not have a word for either, that sat in her like a coal — and she had understood what she had done, every bit of it, for all those years, and had wanted nothing in the world except to undo it.

She had hidden it. She was a spy; she had been a very good one. She went on wearing what she had been like a coat, among her own kind, and nobody knew. Until a child had wandered into the Wound, lost, and she had saved it, without thinking, because she could not do anything else — and that had been the end of hiding.

"Your crusaders would have killed me on sight," she said. "My own kind would have done worse. So I stayed in the Wound. I thought if I could learn something — something true, something they *needed* — I could bring it to the crusade, and they might listen long enough to hear it."

"And did you?" said Caleth.

She looked at him.

"I learned," she said, "that your templars were feeding crystals to demons. The purple ones. To make them more than they were." Her mouth twisted. "I had only begun to learn how, when they took me."

Aponavicius. She said the name as though it hurt her teeth. The marilith had taken her herself and put her in a cell under Drezen, and had tried — for a long time, and with great care, and in ways Arueshalae did not describe and did not need to — to make her into what she had been.

"She could not," Arueshalae said. "She tried, and she could not." Her hands had closed in her lap. "And when she went south to the war, I prayed. And the wards on my door fell."

The cell. The butterflies. Caleth thought of a thousand of them scratched into granite with fingernails, and the prayer in the middle cut deeper than all the rest.

*I am ready. Finally ready.*

She had fled. A few weeks, she said, before they came to Drezen. Into the Wound, with nowhere to go — except one place. The only place in all that country she could think of that might be safe for a thing like her, and only because it had been in a dream once.

"Her last dream," Arueshalae said. "The priestess. It was of this place." She lifted her eyes and looked up the stair, at the dark, toward the thing that hung at the top of it. "This was a fortress of Desna. A long time ago. She dreamed of it as it was."

She stopped.

That was all of it. She sat by the lamp, with her torn wing drawn in close round her, and did not look at any of them.

---

It was Thane who spoke first.

Caleth would have bet against that. He would have lost. The dwarf had stood against the wall through all of it with his arms folded and his face giving nothing, the way it gave nothing to a man in a market who was trying to sell him a horse. Now he unfolded his arms.

"Have you ever been," he said, "to the Ivory Sanctum?"

The succubus went white.

She was already pale; Caleth would not have thought she could go paler. She did. Her whole body pulled in on itself, wings and arms and shoulders, like a hand closing on a burn.

"I have," she said. "And I will not go back. Not ever."

"You don't have to," said Thane. "But you know where it is."

It was not a question. Caleth heard it not be a question. He saw the succubus hear it too, and look at the dwarf for a long moment, and understand that she was looking at somebody who had made his living finding the one person in a room who knew the thing he needed.

"Yes," she said. "I know where it is."

Thane did not move. But Caleth knew Thane. He had stood beside the dwarf on a great many nights, in a great many rooms, and he knew the particular stillness that came over him when a lock he had been working at for a long time finally gave under the pick. Not triumph. Thane did not do triumph. Only a kind of settling, all the way down, like a bolt going home.

*Ivory Sanctum location: unknown.*

Caleth did not look at Aravashnial. He found that he very much did not want to.

"Then you'll help us find Vang," Thane said.

The succubus stared at him.

"You," she said. She looked from Thane to Korroc to Nageru to Caleth. "You are going against *Vang*?"

"Aye, lass," said Korroc.

"Yes," said Caleth. "We are."

He heard his own voice come out of him, level and quiet and entirely certain, the way it came out of him when a calculation had finished and there was nothing left to do but say the answer.

"And I am going to kill him."

The room was very quiet. The lamp burned. On the bottom step of the stair, at the edge of his sight, Aravashnial did not move.

The succubus looked at each of them in turn. Caleth watched her do it. He watched her look at their faces one after another, slowly, the way one looks at a hand of cards one has been dealt and cannot quite believe — and he saw that she did not believe it. Not yet. She wanted to, and she did not dare, and she had lived a long time among things that said what you wanted to hear.

---

"What will we do with her?"

Nageru. He had not spoken since the door. He said it quietly, and reasonably, and Caleth heard under the reason something that sounded almost like fear — not of her; *for* her.

"We cannot take her back to the crusaders," said Nageru.

Caleth began to nod. It was sense. It was plain sense, and he had been about to say it himself — a demon, in Drezen, among three hundred paladins who had spent their lives and their friends' lives killing demons —

"Yes we can," said Korroc.

Flatly. Over the top of him.

"She's coming back with us," said Thane, at the same moment.

Caleth turned and looked at the two of them. The two dwarves. Korroc by the door, with his armor in a heap and his axe buckled into his pack and his beard full of worm. Thane against the wall, with his arms folded again. Neither of them looked as though they thought there was anything to discuss.

"Take a *demon*," Caleth said, "back to the crusade."

"They're not going to fight *us*," said Thane.

He said it the way he had said *you know where it is.* As a thing already settled. And Caleth, looking at him, understood that it was — that the dwarf had done the sum already, while the rest of them were still listening, and had arrived at it: that a citadel which had been taken by these four, and held by these four, and saved from a glabrezu through the wall by these four, was not going to raise a sword against whatever these four brought home.

He thought: *that is not how paladins work.*

He thought: *that is precisely how soldiers work.*

He frowned.

"That," he said, "will take a great deal of explaining to Irabeth."

Korroc grinned. It was his own grin, not the axe's. "Aye," he said. "It will."

And there it was. Caleth stood in the middle of a hallowed room in a ruined fortress at the far end of the Worldwound and felt it happen — felt the thing settle, the way he had felt a hundred arguments settle among these four, without a vote and without anyone quite agreeing to it, because two dwarves had said *yes* and meant it.

He looked at the woman by the lamp.

*Be the kind one, Spireborn. Even when the others choose the hammer.*

He had thought, for a long time, that he knew which of them would be holding the hammer. He had been wrong about that once already this year.

He was wrong about it again. He felt it, standing there. It did not feel the way he had expected it to.

---

They talked about the bell.

Caleth had gone up the stair to look at it, while Thane went out to the hag with a knife and a sack and came back with the sack heavier, and did not say with what. He climbed until the stair came out into the open top of the tower, under the stars, and there it hung: a great bell of silver, pure silver — he had not believed that when Nageru said it; he believed it now — with a ring of runes cut round its lip that he knew for Desna's, and a hammer lying on the stones beneath it where Thane had dropped it.

He put his hand on it. Not to ring it. Only to feel.

It was cold. And under the cold, very deep, so deep it was more like a tide than a thing, the working that held it here. Not to the tower; to the *place*. To the world. A lock. He could feel the shape of it, the way he could feel the shape of a ward on a door, and the shape was very old and very good and wanted nothing to do with being moved.

"Can we take it?" Korroc said, from the top of the stair, breathing hard. He had climbed it, which Caleth would have paid to see.

"No," said Caleth. "Not today." He took his hand away. "It does not want to go anywhere. Something has seen to that. And it weighs as much as a house."

Korroc looked at the bell with the deep professional sorrow of a dwarf looking at a great deal of silver he is not allowed to have.

"Drezen could use a thing like that," he said.

"Drezen could," Caleth agreed. He looked at it a moment longer. "Perhaps one day."

---

"One of us stays with her," Caleth said.

They were back at the foot of the stair. He had been working it out on the way down.

"I fold the rest of us to Drezen," he said. "Now. Tonight. I have seen the command room a thousand times; I could find it in my sleep. We tell Irabeth what is coming, before it comes. And then I come back and bring the last of us, and her." He looked at the succubus. "I will not fold a demon into the middle of Drezen without warning. Not even this one. Somebody will put a sword through her before anyone has said a word, and they will be right to."

Nobody argued.

"Who stays?"

"I will stay with her," said Nageru.

He said it at once, before anyone else could. As though there had never been any question. Caleth looked at him, and at the succubus, who had gone very still by the lamp, and nodded.

The rest of them came in around him. Thane. Korroc, with his armor in his arms and his axe in his pack, muttering at it. Aravashnial, standing now, on his good leg, with one hand on Thane's shoulder.

Caleth looked at Nageru one last time, in the lamplight, with his torn wraps and his bloody back and his hands open at his sides, and the demon by the wall behind him.

*How much do you trust him?*

"I'll be back," he said.

"I know," said Nageru.

The world folded.

---

It came back as lamplight and voices and the smell of old maps.

The command room. The long table. Every face in it turning toward them at once — clerks, sergeants, a runner by the door, a paladin with a ledger — and the sound of somebody dropping something.

Irabeth was at the far end of the table. She looked up. She saw them, and saw what was missing, and her hand went flat on the table.

Thane stepped forward.

"We need the leadership," he said. "Nobody else."

Irabeth did not ask. She looked at his face for the length of a breath, and whatever she saw there settled it.

"Clear the room," she said.

It cleared. Caleth watched it go — the clerks gathering their papers, the sergeants not quite looking at Korroc's armor in a heap on the floor, the runner already out of the door before anyone had told him where to run to — and as the last of them went, and the great doors began to swing shut on the corridor, he reached into himself and found the thing that folded long distances, and took hold of it. It was getting harder to find. He found it.

The doors closed. Caleth did not see them close.

---

He came out of the fold at the foot of the tower, in the dark, with the dead fortress silent all around him. The lamp was still burning inside the door.

He heard their voices before he reached it. Low. Nageru's, and hers. He did not mean to listen. He was in the doorway before he could have helped it, and Nageru was sitting on the bottom step of the stair where Aravashnial had sat, and the succubus was by the lamp, and they both looked up at him.

He had caught only the last of it. Nageru's voice, quiet, in the tone he used for things he was not sure he was allowed to say:

"— Desna may have had something to do with that."

The succubus looked at the monk for a long moment. Then she nodded, slowly.

Caleth did not ask. He held out his hands.

---

It came back as lamplight again.

The room was empty of clerks now. There was Irabeth, at the head of the table, standing. There was Aron, beside her, with his side still bound from the dawn attack, looking as though he had been told something he was going to need to hear twice. There was Sosiel, by the fire, very upright, his hands folded in front of him in a way that meant he was praying or about to argue. There was Anevia, perched on the edge of the table with her arms crossed, who looked up as they appeared and said, before anything else, "Oh, you're *joking*."

And Thane by the window, and Korroc beside his armor, and Aravashnial in a chair by the wall with his bloody leg up on a stool, where somebody had put him.

Irabeth was in the middle of a sentence. Caleth gathered that she had been in the middle of it for some time.

"— and I am going to have to convince a citadel of three hundred paladins," she was saying, "that this is a *good thing* —"

She stopped.

She looked at Nageru. She looked at Caleth. And then she looked at the woman between them, with the black horns and the torn wing and the bow held low and empty at her side, and the whole of Irabeth's face went still.

She did not reach for her sword. Caleth saw her not reach for it. He saw what it cost.

"*What,*" Irabeth said, very quietly, "are you doing to me."

It was not, he thought, a question for anybody in particular. It might have been for her god.

The succubus did not move. She stood between Caleth and Nageru in the middle of the command room of Citadel Drezen — the citadel she had escaped from, the citadel she had been chained under — and looked at the commander of Drezen, and did not lower her eyes.

Caleth stepped up beside her.

He had not planned it. He had planned the fold, and the warning, and the return. He had not planned this — but he found, as he did it, that some part of him had known since the tower room that it would come to this, and had been getting the words ready.

"Irabeth," he said. "Let everyone know."

She looked at him.

"If anyone has a problem with her," Caleth said, "she is under my protection." He kept his voice level. He did not need to raise it; the room was small and very quiet. "She has promised to help us against Vang, and against the demons. Anyone who has a problem with that may bring it to me. If she is touched —" he paused, and let them hear the pause — "they will deal with me."

The fire cracked in the grate.

Irabeth looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at Thane, by the window, who had not moved. At Korroc, beside his heap of armor, with his arms folded across his torn coat and his chin up. At Nageru.

Something in her face eased. Not much. A very little.

"I don't think," she said, "that you are the only one they'll have to deal with. If they try something."

Korroc grunted. It was agreement. It was a great deal of agreement.

Anevia let out a long breath through her nose and uncrossed her arms. "Well," she said. "That'll make for a lively week."

Sosiel said nothing at all. He was looking at the succubus, steadily, the way he looked at a wound he had not yet decided how to dress.

"She knows where the Ivory Sanctum is," said Nageru.

Every head in the room turned.

"She will help us find it," Nageru said. He said it simply, the way he said everything — a thing that was, set down where it went. "And we will go."

---

Nobody spoke for a time.

Caleth stood beside the succubus with his hand on his coat, over the letters and the note and the potion, and felt the room take it. Irabeth's face, doing arithmetic. Aron's, being told a thing twice. Thane at the window with his stillness all the way down. Korroc's chin.

And Aravashnial.

He had not meant to look. He had been not looking at him since the tower room, the way one does not look at a sore tooth with the tongue, and it had worked, and then it stopped working. He looked.

The elf sat in his chair by the wall with his torn leg up on the stool and his hands folded on his knee. He was looking at the succubus. He had been looking at her, Caleth thought, since she came out of the fold — since the tower, perhaps; since she first stood up beside the lamp. His face was very calm.

Caleth could not read it.

He had been able to read that face, once. Not well, not always — but enough. Enough to know when the elf was amused and when he was tired and when he was about to deliver a lecture on aurochs whether anyone wanted one or not. He had learned it over a year, across a great many fires, without ever setting out to learn it, the way one learns the face of a man one is going to have to trust.

He looked at it now, and it told him nothing at all.

*Don't you see that it could be a trick?*

Four nights ago, out by the Gray Road, in the dark, with a dead river behind them. *Why would she contact him and not all of you? How much do you trust him?*

Caleth looked at the demon he had just put under his own protection, in front of the commander of Drezen. He looked at the monk beside her, whom he trusted with his life, and had said so. He looked at the man in the chair by the wall, his father's brother, who had stood just outside the edge of his life for twenty-five years and sent medicine to a sick child and never once come in.

He had spent his whole life watching everything. He was doing it now. He stood in the command room of Citadel Drezen with his hand over his heart, and watched, with the whole of himself.

He found that he did not know which of the two of them he was watching for.
