# What Came Back Clean

*Chapter Thirty — The Thread He Did Not Lay*

---

The entrance chamber was empty, and Korroc did not like it.

It was a big room and a cold one, the first thing a guest would see coming in at the front door, and whoever had built it had wanted that guest small. The ceiling went up into dark. The walls were ivory-paneled to the height of two men and bare stone above, which meant the man who paid for it had run out of money or out of patience, and Korroc had opinions about both. There was a bench along one wall. There was a stand for a lamp with no lamp in it. There was nothing else at all, and the great doors at the front were shut, and behind them, somewhere out in the cold of the gorge, there was a thing that turned men into stone.

"Nothing," said Thane, straightening up from the floor. "Dust. Two sets of boots, old. Nobody's come in this way in a while."

"Aye," said Korroc.

He stood a moment longer anyway. That was the trouble with him and he knew it: a job was not finished when the hammering stopped. A job was finished when you had let the work go cold and come back and looked at it in daylight with your hands behind your back, and found nothing you wanted to change. They had killed the master of this house an hour ago. The house did not feel finished.

He turned around and looked back the length of it.

The long hall ran away from him, black-tiled, still smelling of burned worm. The short stair where Vang had stood. The scorched place in the middle of the floor where a heap of robes had been. And past that, under the arch, the red glow of the temple, where a goat-headed thing twenty-five feet tall stood on its dais holding a moon on a stick.

"The big room," he said. "We never finished the big room."

---

Caleth was already walking. He had been quiet since the hall — quiet in the way that meant he was carrying something and had decided not to set it down where anyone could see it — and he went down the length of it with Radiance across his shoulder and the bag swinging at his hip, and Korroc went after him.

He stopped at the scorched place.

Korroc had walked past it twice already and not looked at it. There was not much to look at: a spread of black cloth lying the way cloth lies when the man inside it has gone, a sleeve flung out, the hem burned to a crust. The worms were ash. The smell was still in the air.

Caleth crouched and took hold of a fold of it and lifted.

"Leave it," said Korroc, before he had decided to say anything.

"No." Caleth was turning the cloth close to his face, pinching it between finger and thumb. "No. Look at this."

Korroc looked. It was burned. There were holes in it he could have put his fist through, and the edges of the holes were brittle and curled, and under the char the weave was —

He stopped.

He put his hand out and Caleth gave him a corner of it, and Korroc rubbed it and felt what he had felt once in the dark of a mine with his father's hand on his shoulder, waiting for him to say what he had found before anybody told him.

"That's not wool," he said.

"It is not."

"That's not anything." He turned the corner toward the braziers. The char came off black on his thumb; the weave under it did not. Burned through in a dozen places, and not one thread of it frayed at the edge of a single hole. "I've put a hammer into steel that gave up sooner."

"Black robes of the archmagi," said Caleth, and there was a note in his voice that had not been in it for some hours. "I thought so when he was standing in them and I was too busy to be sure. Korroc, they are not a thing a man *finds.* There are perhaps a dozen written about and half the writing is argument over whether the other half were ever real."

"They're burned."

"Yes." Caleth gathered the whole weight of it up into his arms, carefully, the way you pick up something that is asleep. "They are also fixable."

Korroc looked at him.

"That's cloth," he said.

"It is."

"I'm a smith."

"You are a smith with a forge that remakes what is laid on it, and I have watched that forge put a working into a blade that is not supposed to take workings." Caleth had the robes over one arm now and was folding them as he talked, briskly, as though the thing were already decided. "I am not telling you it will take. I am telling you that when we are home I want the two of us to carry this down to Joran and stand over it for an hour before either of us says it cannot be done."

Korroc thought about the Purity Forge. He thought about a man with no healing left in his hands, holding an awl steady for him while he set a rivet.

"An hour," he said.

"An hour."

"Then bag it, and don't leave half of it on the floor."

Caleth was already down on one knee, going over the basalt for threads.

When he had the last of it he stood, and shouldered the bag, and went on under the arch without another word about it.

---

The temple was worse with the fighting over. In a fight a room was only ground: where the footing was bad, where a man could get behind you. Now it was a room again, and Korroc could see what it was for. The walkway down the middle between the speckled columns. The altar at the statue's feet, a slab of black stone with a channel cut round the top edge of it and a hole bored in the near corner, and Korroc, who had spent his life making things that had to carry something away from somewhere, knew exactly what that channel and that hole were for. The ceiling painted like a night sky, dark blue, pricked with stars. Somebody had taken trouble over those stars. Somebody had lain on a scaffold with a brush and got them right.

He hated that more than he hated the statue.

"Remember," he said, "this is where three of them came out at us."

Thane nodded. He had drifted ahead to the foot of the dais, light-footed, hands empty, the way he went anywhere he thought might still have something in it. He lifted his chin at the statue.

"But they came from over there."

Red curtains, floor to ceiling, hanging either side of the dais. They had been pulled across so neatly that even now, after a fight that had put four men on the floor of the next room, the folds hung straight. Korroc looked at them and thought about his mother shaking out a sheet.

Thane went to the left-hand one. He did not pull it. He put two fingers on the edge of the cloth and eased it back a handspan and stood there a long moment with his head turned, and then he opened it the rest of the way and stood aside and motioned them in.

The room behind it was small and clean and there was a circle on the floor.

Korroc's first thought was that it was good work. He could not help it; it was always his first thought. The circle was iron, laid into the stone itself, the metal sunk flush with the floor so that a man could walk across it barefoot and feel nothing but a line of cold. Inside it, worked in the same iron, a rune with seven arms, each arm ending in a hook that curved back toward the center. Black candles stood at intervals round the outside, burned down to stubs and relit and burned down again.

The second thought arrived about a breath later and was not about craft at all.

He had seen that rune before. Upstairs, in the study, in the floor by the writing desk, and nobody had been able to say what it was, and the four of them had walked round it with the care of men stepping over a sleeping dog.

Caleth went down on one knee at the edge of it.

He did not touch it. He put his hands on his thighs and leaned over it and looked, and his face did the thing it did when the scholar in him got out ahead of the rest — the small frown, the lips moving. Then the frown changed.

"Circle of the Abyss," he said.

"That's a name," said Thane. "What's it do?"

"It holds." Caleth tilted his head. "You draw one of these and you call something out of the Abyss into the middle of it, and it comes, and it cannot get out, and you can talk to it for as long as you have the nerve." He paused. "He summons things from outside to talk to them. Don't step on that."

Korroc looked at the burned candles. There were a lot of them. A man did not burn that many candles having one conversation.

"The one upstairs," he said.

Caleth's head came up.

"Yes," he said slowly. "Yes. The same." He looked back down at it, and Korroc watched him put it together and did not enjoy watching it. "I stood over that one for a quarter of an hour and told you I did not know what it was. I thought it was set against us. A ward." A short breath through the nose, not quite a laugh. "It was a chair. He had a chair in his study for whatever he wanted to talk to."

"And he'd talk to it alone," said Thane. "In the room where he keeps his books. With the door shut."

Nobody said anything to that.

Caleth stayed on his knee a while longer. Then: "Actually — we can turn this off."

"Can you?"

"*We* can. Not me." He looked up. "Thane. Can you look at it? The circle has to be interrupted. It isn't the rune that does the work, it's the ring; the ring is the wall. Break the ring anywhere and the wall is gone."

Thane came and crouched beside him and looked at the iron for a while without touching it either.

"Break it how," he said. "Scratch it? Chalk? Last time it was charcoal."

"Last time it was ink on parchment and charcoal on lead, and both of those were writing." Caleth sat back on his heels. "This isn't writing. This is a thing somebody *built*. You'd have to —"

"Take a piece out of it," said Korroc.

They both looked at him.

He shrugged. He had not meant to say it out loud; it had simply come up the way a measurement comes up when you look at a beam. "It's iron in stone," he said. "Sunk flush, and sunk deep by the look of the seat, and keyed in — see the little swellings there and there, every foot or so? Those are dovetails. They poured it hot, or they drove it in cold and peened it. Either way." He came over and crouched too, and his knees complained, and he ignored them. "You don't scratch iron. You take it out. Get a chisel under it where it meets the stone, at a joint, and lift a finger's length of it clean away, and then it's not a ring any more. It's a ring with a gap in it. And a wall with a gap in it isn't a wall."

Thane looked at him for a moment with an expression Korroc could not read and then said, "Huh."

"What?"

"Nothing." The dwarf was already reaching for his pack. "It's just that this lot have been standing round a wizard's problem for a day and the answer was a cold chisel."

"The answer is usually a cold chisel."

"That's not true and you know it's not true."

"It's true often enough to try it first."

Korroc gave him the chisel out of the master's roll — the good one, the one he had taken off a table in the forge upstairs two hours ago and had not been able to put down since — and Thane took it without comment and turned it over once in his fingers and did not say anything about that either, which was how Korroc knew he had noticed what it was.

It took him eight minutes. He worked where one of the seven arms came down and met the ring, because that was where two pours met and a seam was a seam even in good work, and he went slow and quiet and kept the chisel low, and tapped it with the butt of his knife rather than a hammer so as not to ring the iron. Korroc watched him and felt something in the back of his chest ease a fraction. There was not much in this house he had enjoyed. This he enjoyed. His cousin on his knees on a demon's floor doing metalwork badly and carefully, the way a man does a thing he was not trained for and means to get right anyway.

The piece came out in three parts. Thane picked them off the floor, looked at them, and put them in his pocket.

"Done," he said, and stood, and stepped back.

Caleth passed a hand over the gap and nodded slowly. "It's nothing now," he said. "It's a picture of a circle."

Korroc looked at it. A ring with a bite out of it. It did not look like anything at all. That was the point of it, and he thought — not for the first time in this house — about how much of the work here had been done by people who were very good at what they did.

Nothing else in the room. A shelf with nothing on it. They came out.

The other curtain was on the far side of the dais, and behind it was another room the same size, and in the middle of that one, on a table of dark wood with nothing else on it, stood a statue about a foot and a half high.

The same goat-headed thing as the one outside. Smaller. Better.

That was the part that got him. The big one was ivory and it was twenty-five feet tall and it was meant to make you feel like a beetle. The little one was meant for a man to look at in a quiet room with the curtain pulled, and whoever had carved it had put love into it. The curl of the horns. The weight in the shoulders. The hand round the glaive haft, the fingers all slightly different, the way fingers are.

Thane walked over, picked it up, looked at it for about one second, and threw it at the floor as hard as he could.

It went off like a dropped plate. Pieces of it skidded across the stone and under the table and out past Korroc's boot into the temple beyond.

"Aye," said Korroc, and grinned, and felt better than he had in an hour.

---

Out of the temple. Back into the middle hall, where the short stair came down from the west with its basalt landing and its labyrinth tiles, and the black floor was still scattered with the small roasted things that had been the master of the house.

Directly opposite the foot of that stair, on the other side of the hall, there was a pair of double doors.

"Those," said Thane.

He checked them the way he checked everything, which was to say for longer than anyone else would have thought necessary and about half as long as he would have taken a year ago, and then he put his shoulder to one and pushed it open and the smell came out at them.

Not rot. Korroc knew rot. This was green and thick and wrong, like a cellar where something had been growing against the wall for a long time without anyone looking at it.

"Thick sheets of — " Thane had gone quiet and flat. "Right."

Korroc came up to the doorway and looked past him.

It was a cavern of a room, or it would have been if you could have seen the walls. The whole northern half of it was furred over — walls, ceiling, the angle where the two met — in great thick mats of pale fibrous stuff, hanging in sheets and tangles, not web and not fungus and something unpleasantly between. The light did not go far into it. And up in the tangle, twenty feet above the bare floor, three shapes hung.

Pods. Or cocoons. Each one the size of a pony.

"Fireball," said Thane, in a voice with nothing in it at all.

Caleth had come up on Korroc's other side and was looking at the fibers with his head tilted and his eyes narrowed in the way that usually ended with the rest of them standing around for ten minutes.

"Definitely fireball," said Thane. "Now."

"I don't know what makes this," said Caleth.

"Nageru." Thane did not look away from the ceiling. "Use that flask —"

"Don't use the flask." Caleth put out a hand. "I have fire. Be patient."

"Then fire."

Korroc, who had been on the receiving end of Caleth's fire twice in the last hour and had the eyebrows to show for it, took one large step backward out of the doorway.

Caleth cast.

The fireball went out of his hand down the length of the room and burst in the middle of the tangle, and the light of it came back out of the door and lit the five of them orange for a second and a half.

And the fibers did not burn.

That was the thing. A web burns. A web goes up like a dry hedge, all at once, with a sound like tearing cloth, and Korroc had seen it a dozen times and had been waiting for it without knowing he was waiting for it. These sagged. They blackened where the fire touched and they curled and they *sagged*, the way a green branch sags in a fire, and then they hung there, steaming.

And one of the pods began to move.

It swung, and bulged, and something inside it chittered — a dry clicking run of sound like a handful of knucklebones shaken in a cup.

"Great," said Thane. "Not what we wanted."

The pod split.

What came out of it came out headfirst and sideways at once, and Korroc's first clear thought about it was that it had been put together by somebody who had never seen an animal.

It was a crab. That was the nearest thing. A great low crab the size of a cart, pale as a thing under a rock, its shell wet-looking and its legs jointed wrong, too many joints, each leg ending in a point that found the fiber and held. It had no face. Where a face should have been it had a mouth, round, lipless, ringed inside with rasping teeth like the mouth of a lamprey, and set along the lengths of its legs, in rows, were eyes. Small black eyes. They twitched independently of one another, and some of them were looking at the ceiling while others were looking at him.

And along its back, under the shell, were blisters. Four or five of them, swollen and translucent, and in each one, floating, gray, unmistakable —

"*Torag's hand,*" said Korroc.

Brains. Human brains. Kept.

The thing came out of its pod and down the wall of fibers and hung there twenty feet up, and all the eyes along its legs came round, slowly, one row and then another, until every one of them was pointed at the door.

It had come out to see what had woken it. That was all. It was not angry. There was nothing in the way it moved that was angry. It had been asleep in its bag on the ceiling and somebody had set a fire under it, and it had come out to look.

Caleth threw another fireball at it.

It burst directly on top of the thing, and the whole tangle lit, and the creature flinched — a real flinch, a whole-body jerk back up into the sheets — and then came on again.

Nageru stepped past Korroc into the doorway.

He did not raise his hands. He put one foot forward, and squared himself, and opened his mouth, and *breathed*.

What came out of him was a cone of acid.

Korroc had known this man for the better part of a year. He had fought at his shoulder in a tomb, a chapel, a burning citadel and a petrified forest. He had seen him do a great many things that a monk of a quiet order ought not to be able to do and had got used to most of them. He had never once seen him do that.

The acid went out in a spreading fan, green-white, hissing, and struck the thing full on and ran down its shell and ate holes in the fiber mat behind it, and the creature staggered back again with a noise like a kettle.

"That's new," said Caleth, in a tone of mild academic interest.

Nageru breathed out, closed his mouth, and said, "It is old."

Which was all anybody got.

---

After that it went the way these things go, which is to say badly and fast.

Thane was already moving. He went right, along the wall, to where a flight of stairs went down ten feet or so to a pair of double doors at the south end — the only other way out of the room — and he set himself at the top of them with his back to the stone so that he could watch the stairs and the ceiling both at once. It was a good place to stand. Korroc noted it the way he noted a good weld.

The thing on the ceiling turned. Nageru's acid had got its attention; apparently it could tell the difference between fire thrown from a distance and a man standing in its doorway breathing on it. One of the eye-rows fixed, and a ray came out of the middle of the mass of them — a thin washed-out gray line, no light to it at all — and struck Nageru in the chest.

Korroc felt the *shape* of it go past, the way you feel a cold draft in a warm shop. It was a weakening. It was the kind of working that gets into a man's arms and takes the strength out of them and leaves him standing there wondering why the hammer is suddenly heavy.

Nageru's shoulders came down half an inch, and came back up, and that was all of it.

The doors at the bottom of the stairs opened.

Korroc saw them go, and saw Thane see them go, and saw his cousin's whole body change shape in the quarter-second before the second creature came up the steps at him — low, fast, far faster than a thing that size had any business being, the legs going like a shuttle in a loom.

Two claws came at him. Thane was not where either of them went. He gave ground along the wall with his feet crossing neat as a dancer's and the claws took stone out of the wall where his head had been, and then the mouth came in under them and the mouth got him, high on the thigh, and Korroc heard his cousin grunt.

And felt it.

Half of it came down the thread and into him, a hot bright line of pain in his own leg, and under the pain something else: a cold crawling wrongness that wanted in. Poison. He felt Thane take hold of it and refuse it, out there across the room, the way the man refused everything — flatly, without ceremony, as though it had made a request and he had simply said no.

Korroc came into the room.

He went to Nageru's right shoulder, which was where he belonged, and set his boots on the bare floor, and reached down.

The stone of the Sanctum came up to meet him.

It always did. It did not care whose house it was. It was stone, and he was Stonevein, and there was a thing his grandfather's grandfather had known how to do that had nothing to do with Torag at all and everything to do with what ran in a dwarf's blood when the blood was old enough; and he sank into it and let it come up through his boots and into his legs and his hips and his spine, until he was not a man standing on a floor but a part of the floor that happened to have a hammer.

Then he lifted his hand and let the fire out of it.

That was the other thing in him, and it was not a gift and never had been. It was a flaw. His oracle's blood ran hot where it should have run even, and the price of it was that cold went through him like a nail through a plank — but the flaw had a face on the other side, and the face was this: two red rays of fire leaping off his fingers across thirty feet of fouled air.

The first went wide. The thing *moved*, flowed sideways along the fiber like water finding a crack, and the ray went past it and burned a hole in the mat.

The second hit.

"*Hah,*" said Korroc.

And the third pod opened above him and something dropped out of the ceiling onto the floor at his elbow.

It landed with a slap of wet shell on stone and came at him before it had finished landing. Mouth and two claws, all three at once, and Korroc did not even have to think about it; the shield came round on its own, the way it had come round ten thousand times, and the Father's hammer and anvil took the mouth and took the first claw and took the second, and the creature found nothing to bite but a dwarf's considered opinion.

The shield did not ring. He noticed that. It had not rung since the forge floor in Drezen, when the three of them had sat all day and something in his shield and something in him had gone together like a weld and stayed gone together. It took blows now the way a cliff takes rain. He could not have told anybody what had changed. He only knew that the thing on his arm and the thing in his chest ran the same way, grain with grain, and that a fiend's claw skidding off it felt less like being hit and more like being *disagreed with*.

Caleth came in past him and cast.

What came out of the half-elf was a stream of acid, and it was the fire-chain — Korroc knew the shape of the working by now even if he could not have named a part of it, the way you know a man's walk at a distance. It was the spell that leapt. He had turned it inside out the way he turned things inside out, taken the fire out of it and put something else in, and the acid struck the creature at Korroc's elbow and jumped from it to the one hanging on the ceiling and from that one all the way down the room to the one on Thane, and all three of them screamed at once in three different places.

Nageru crossed to the one on the floor and hit it.

The first blow landed like a maul on a post. The second and the third went in on top of it, bang, bang, and the shell cracked and something clear ran out of it and the thing's legs scrabbled and did not quite give way.

Thane backed up two steps and prayed.

Korroc felt that too. It came over all of them at once, light and dry and faintly cool, like standing in a doorway: Alseta's blessing, the goddess of doors and thresholds and the spaces between one thing and the next, which was exactly the right god for a man who had spent his life on the wrong side of doors. Korroc had stopped being surprised by it a long time ago. He had stopped being surprised by most things about his cousin. It still did him good, every time, to feel another god's hand on his shoulder and know whose it was.

The creature on Thane came after him and got him again. Claw, claw, and the mouth going for the same place on the thigh. The claws landed. Korroc bled where Thane bled. The mouth came in and Thane twisted on his hip at the last instant and the teeth tore cloth instead of flesh, and then the thing tried to get its legs round him, to fold him up against itself the way a spider folds a fly, and he went out of it low and came up four feet away with both blades out.

And then they started talking.

That was the only word for it. All three of them, across the length of the room, chittering — that dry knucklebone sound, but in runs and patterns now, back and forth, one and then another and then the first again. Korroc felt the hair come up on his arms. It was not animal noise. He did not have to be a scholar to hear the difference between a dog barking and a man speaking.

Caleth went very still beside him.

Korroc glanced at him and saw his face: the eyes unfocused, the head a little on one side, every scrap of him listening. Not frightened. *Working.*

"Caleth. Are you with us."

"Mm," said Caleth, and did not move.

The one on the ceiling dropped.

It came down to the floor with a crash and a lightning bolt came out of it.

Korroc saw the flash and the room went white and he had no time at all. It came down the length of the room in a line that took Nageru and himself together — and Nageru rolled, somehow, the way he always somehow rolled, under it or through it or around it, and came up with his robe smoking at the hem and nothing else wrong with him.

It took Korroc in the chest.

Plate armor is a wonderful thing. Korroc had said so all his life, to anyone who would listen and several who would not. Plate armor is a roof over a man. There is exactly one circumstance in which plate armor is a man's enemy, and he was standing in it: a sheet of good steel, strapped to him with new straps that a one-handed dwarf in Drezen had cut and set for him a few days ago, and every bit of it thirsty for the lightning.

It went through him and out through his boots into the stone, and for a moment he was not a man at all. He was a bell that had been struck.

When he came back, he was still standing, because he had been in the stone, and the stone had held him up when his legs had stopped voting. His beard smelled wrong. His back teeth hurt.

He said something in Dwarven that his mother would have cuffed him for.

Then he turned and threw a handful of the Smith's mercy down the room into his cousin, who was bleeding from a leg and a shoulder and who, Korroc happened to know, had been bleeding on his behalf for about ninety seconds. Gold went out of his hand and found Thane and the worst of the leg closed. Then he put his own palm flat on his breastplate, over the hammer and anvil, and said a short word, and some of the ache went out of his chest and down into the floor.

The one Nageru had been hitting turned on Nageru.

It got him. That was the long and short of it — the mouth first and then both claws, three strikes out of three, and Korroc felt a hot sheet of it come down the thread and open up across his own ribs, and the monk's blood went across the floor in a spray. And then the thing tried to gather him in, the way the other had tried with Thane, and Nageru was simply not inside its reach any more. He had gone to the ground and come up again outside it without appearing to travel between the two.

Caleth's hand went to his belt.

Korroc caught the glint of it out of the corner of his eye: the short black rod, the stout ugly one that did nothing on its own. He had asked about it once. *It is a lever,* Caleth had said. *It casts nothing. It only makes whatever goes through it heavier.* Korroc had understood that perfectly and had said so, and Caleth had looked pleased for the rest of the evening.

The half-elf took the rod in his fist and reached for the other thing he did — the inside-out — and the lightning he had meant to throw was not lightning when it left him. Acid arced out of his hand and leapt from the creature on Thane to the one that had opened Nageru up, and it went into them like something poured.

Nageru went back in.

And missed. Four times.

Korroc watched it happen and it was almost worse than being hit. The first fist went over the thing's back. The second went wide. The third and fourth found nothing but air, and the creature was *moving* for him now, properly, reading him, giving ground and coming back, all those twitching eyes on their stalks taking in where his weight was before he had finished putting it there.

Nageru stopped.

It was about half a breath. He simply stopped, in the middle of a fight, with a thing the size of a cart reaching for him — and then he moved his back foot four inches, and hit it.

One blow. Straight into the crack Caleth's acid had opened. The whole creature went down on its belly with a sound like a dropped barrel and did not move again.

At the far end, Thane's blades went in.

He hit once out of four, which Korroc knew because he could count his cousin's misses by the curses, and the once was enough. The mother's blade went in under the shell at the joint and came out again and the thing folded up on the stairs.

And the last one went up into the ceiling and vanished.

It climbed. Fast, hand over hand up the hanging sheets of fiber, and then it was in among them and the mats closed over it and there was nothing up there but gray tangle and a smell.

Korroc put his head back.

"I don't see where it went," he said. "Anyone?"

He laid his palm on his own chest again and took another piece of the ache out of himself while he said it, because a man who waits until the fighting stops to see to his own wounds is a man who does not finish fights.

"Can't see it," said Caleth. "But —"

A fireball went up into the ceiling and burst among the fibers, and the mats steamed and sagged and blackened and did not catch, and nothing fell out of them.

Nageru had gone very still at the center of the floor with his head tipped back. Korroc watched him listen. Then the monk lifted his arm, pointed — and in the same movement pulled a javelin off his back and threw it.

It went up hard and fast and buried itself in a strand of fiber and stuck there, quivering, with nothing on the end of it.

Nageru said a short word in Dwarven. He had got it from Korroc and he used it about twice a year, and his accent was terrible.

Something came out of the ceiling then — a scatter of small hard lights, five or six of them, that curved down and apart in the air and went into each of them in turn, Korroc and Thane and Nageru and Caleth, little stinging thumps like being flicked by somebody's thumbnail. Korroc looked down at his breastplate, and up at the ceiling, and was more insulted than hurt.

But he had seen where the javelin went.

That was all he needed. He could not see the thing. He did not need to see the thing. He knew where Nageru had thrown, and Nageru did not throw at nothing, and the distance from the javelin to anything else up there was a length a man could cover with the right tool.

He set his feet and he prayed.

*Father. There is a thing in the roof of this house with men's minds in bags on its back. I would like it out of the roof.*

The fire came down.

It came out of the ceiling itself, a great column of it, white-gold and roaring, dropping straight down through the hanging mats over a circle wider than a wagon — not his own flame but the other kind, the kind that came down from somewhere above the stone and only passed through fire on the way. It struck, and the fibers burned at last, and a wash of heat rolled out over the floor and made every man in the room put an arm up.

Then it was gone and the room was quiet.

Korroc walked out into the middle of the floor under the burned place and stopped, and let his breath out, and sent his senses out of himself the way he had learned to do without ever being taught — the way you listen in a dark workshop for whether there is anyone else in it. Life. There was always so much of it, everywhere, if you knew how to feel for it. Four men in this room and a woman in the doorway and a thousand small things in the walls.

Nothing in the ceiling at all.

"Dead," he said.

Then he got out his wands, because people were bleeding, and he did Nageru first because Nageru was opened up worst, and Thane second because Thane would not have asked, and himself last.

---

Nageru climbed the fibers.

He went up the burned edge of the mat where it was still whole and did it one-handed, which Korroc found personally offensive, and his nose wrinkled all the way.

"These fibers are warm to the touch," he called down. "They feel like vegetation. They feel *alive*."

"Don't eat any," said Thane.

"I had not intended to."

He found the pods the creatures had slept in, and nests in among the tangle where they had kept their things, and that was when it stopped being a nest and started being a house, which Korroc liked even less. Things kept. A pouch of gems, uncut, a good handful. Lumps of raw metal — ore and part-worked both, and Korroc took one in his hand when it came down and turned it over and said "that's silver, that is, and badly smelted," and put it in the bag with something like disapproval. And two wands.

Caleth looked at the wands for a while, and then looked up at Thane and then away, and said, "Confusion."

Nobody said anything.

Thane, from the stairs, said, "Burn them."

"They are worth —"

"I know what they're worth."

Caleth put them in the bag and said nothing else about it, and Korroc, who had spent a fight a few hours ago bleeding out of a hole his own cousin had put in his side and had refused to let go of him while he did it, thought that was probably the right way to handle it.

"That third one," Nageru called down, "is definitely dead."

"Aye," said Korroc. "I know."

Nageru came to the edge of the burned mat, and stepped off it, and was standing on the floor.

---

The double doors at the bottom of the short stairs were still standing open from the creature that had come out of them, and the smell that came up through them was different and worse.

Sweet. That was the thing that took the top of Korroc's head off. Sweet, like a jar of preserves left out through a hot week, laid over the flat iron smell of old blood.

It was an eight-sided room, well cut, the angles true. Two slabs of stone against the east and west walls, raised to about the height of a kitchen table. On each slab, a body.

"Ah," said Korroc, very quietly. "Ah, no."

He did not go fast. He had learned that in the first year of the crusade and relearned it in Kenabres with the whole sky on fire: you do not run at the dead. There is nothing there that requires running, and going fast at them is a way of not looking at them, and they deserve to be looked at.

The group spread out through the room the way they did — Caleth went straight to the table against the south wall, where a great leather-bound book lay open among rusted instruments and clouded glass vials; Nageru went to the heap of clothing and belongings piled at the foot of the western slab; Thane went past them all to a second set of double doors at the back of the room and put his ear near them without quite touching them.

Korroc went to the bodies.

They were human. He established that first, because he had been in places where it was not obvious, and this was one of them. Human, both of them, one larger and one smaller, and both of them opened.

Not butchered. That was the horror of it, and he made himself take the whole measure of it standing there with his gauntlets off and his hands at his sides. Butchery he could have understood; he had seen what a demon does to a man in a hurry, and it is loud and stupid and over quickly. This was *work*. The chest of the nearer one had been laid open along the breastbone with a single clean cut and the ribs spread and held, and held with pins that somebody had made on purpose for the job, small steel pins turned on a lathe with a milled head so a thumb could seat them. Somebody had gone to a bench and made tools for this. The incisions were even. There were old cuts beside new ones, partly healed, sewn and opened again.

And at the wrists and ankles, shackles.

You do not shackle a corpse.

Korroc stood there with that for a while and found that he was not angry. He had expected to be angry. He had been angry in the forge upstairs and angry at the statue and angry about the painted stars. What he had instead was a kind of enormous flat quiet, the quiet of the shop after the fire's gone out, and he recognized it, and knew it would turn into something later and that he would not enjoy it when it did.

Behind him Caleth said, "This is a record."

"Of what?"

"Of this." A pause and the sound of a page. "Sketches. Measurements. Notes on every — " another pause " — I can't read it. I don't know the language. I don't know the *shape* of the language."

Korroc put his hand on the dead man's forehead.

It was cold and it did not matter that it was cold. He had done this in a tomb at Sesker's Gully for a woman dead a hundred years and he had done it in a courtyard in Kenabres for people whose names he never learned, and he did it now, in Old Dwarven first because that was what the words were made in, and then in Common, because whoever this was had probably never heard a word of Dwarven in his life and ought to be sent off in a tongue he knew.

*The Father is a smith. He does not waste good iron. What was made well is not thrown away because it was broken by a bad hand. Go on, then. Go on.*

He did it twice, once at each slab, and took his time, and nobody in that room said a word while he did it, and when he had finished at the second one Thane was still standing at the back doors with his ear turned and had not moved at all.

---

"There are glyphs here."

Caleth had left the book. He was at the northwest wall, the one that was covered floor to ceiling in black marks, and he had his face about a hand's breadth from it.

Korroc came over, pulling his gauntlets back on. The marks were not like the runes in the lead room upstairs. Those had been scratched in close crowded bands like a miser's ledger. These were large, and spaced, and in places rubbed out and done again, and in one place there was a long row of them with a line struck through the lot.

"What is it?"

"It's not finished," said Caleth. He sounded almost offended. "That's the first thing. Somebody has been *working* on this. Look — here, and here. That is the same figure three times with a change in it each time." He traced the air above the wall without touching it. "It looks like a binding. The working you use to call something from another plane and hold it to a bargain. But it's been turned round. They have been trying to make a binding that works the other way — that holds a thing *of this plane* in this plane. That keeps people here."

"That's the one on the whole house," said Thane from the doors. "The one that wouldn't let you step sideways."

"No." Caleth shook his head slowly. "No, that's something else, and that one works. This is — " He stopped, and when he went on his voice had changed a little. "This is someone trying to invent a new spell. And failing. For a long time."

Korroc looked along the wall at the crossed-out line.

"On people," he said.

"Yes."

"On these people."

"I would think so."

"Aye," said Korroc.

Nageru said, from the heap of clothing, "Korroc."

He had straightened up with something in his hand, and he was looking at it with his head bent, and his face — Nageru's face, which did not do a great deal — had gone wrong.

It was a boot.

"Is this not Aravashnial's boot?"

Korroc crossed the room. It was a boot, a good one, soft dark leather with a low heel and a silver buckle at the ankle, and the sole was worn down on the outside edge the way a man wears a sole when he stands too long at a desk with his weight on one leg; and the whole of it was stiff and black most of the way up with something that had dried a long time ago.

He had seen that boot. He had seen the pair of them under a table in Drezen with the ankles crossed, a hundred times.

He looked up and found Caleth looking at it too, from across the room, and the half-elf nodded once.

"Ruined?" said Caleth.

Nageru turned it over. "Yes."

He dropped it back on the pile.

Nobody picked it up again. There was a great deal in that heap — torn cloth, belts, a boy's jerkin, a woman's shawl, cut rather than unfastened, every piece of it belonging to somebody who had been walked into this room on their own feet. Korroc looked at the size of the pile and did not count and made himself stop looking at it.

"Thane," said Caleth. "The door."

---

Thane did not find a trap on it, which annoyed him, because he said so. He got out his picks and worked, and the lock turned over with a click, and he stood back and let Nageru push the door open.

Behind it was a wall of black.

Not a dark room. Korroc had been in plenty of dark rooms; he could see in them better than any of the humans and most of the half-elves he had ever met. This was a surface. It stood in the doorway like a sheet of slate, flush with the jamb, and the light from the laboratory went up to it and stopped dead.

"Caleth?" said Nageru.

Nothing.

"Caleth."

Korroc turned round.

Caleth was standing about eight feet behind them. He was on his feet. He was balanced. His hands were at his sides, and Radiance was in the crook of his arm, and he was looking straight at the open door, and there was nobody behind his eyes at all.

Korroc had seen a man do that once, after a beam came down on him in a yard in Kenabres. He crossed the floor fast and got a hand on the half-elf's arm, above the elbow, where the coat was thin.

"Caleth."

Nothing. Not a flicker. The eyes did not move; the breath went in and out; and the arm under Korroc's hand was rigid in a way that a sleeping man's arm is not.

And then Korroc felt it.

The thread. The warm line that ran between him and all of them, the thing he had been strengthening and thinning and carrying since a ruin outside Kenabres a year ago — the thread to Caleth went *hot*.

It was not a wound. He knew wounds; they came down the thread sharp and simple and he could tell you where they were. This was a heat with no place to it, rising, running up the line toward him, and under the heat something worse: a pulling, a rhythm, like a hand opening and closing.

He had felt it once before. A fortnight ago, on the road, in the dark, with the horses gone and everyone asleep — a long rising heat and a fear under it and then a stop, and he had put one foot on the floor of his tent and had not gone, and in the morning he had said *sleep?* and got *some*, and let it be, on purpose, because a man is entitled to his own nights.

He was not going to let it be this time.

"*Caleth.*"

Nothing.

Korroc turned his head to the doorway and the sheet of black standing in it, and the thing that was coming up the thread into his chest, and he said:

"Close the door."

Nageru swung it shut.

Caleth blinked.

The heat went out of the thread like a lamp going out. The half-elf's eyes came back — Korroc watched them come back, watched the man arrive in his own face, which is a thing you should never have to watch happen to a friend — and he looked round at the room as though he had not seen it before. At the slabs. At the wall of glyphs. At Korroc's hand on his arm.

"How long," he said.

"Four breaths. Five."

Caleth stood very still. Then he said, "That isn't possible," almost to himself, and went past them all to the shut door, and stood in front of it with his hand not quite on it.

"They did experiments on him here."

Thane's head came round. "Him?"

"Aravashnial." Caleth did not turn. "In this room. On that slab, I think — the near one. They were trying to turn him into some sort of vessel."

"Sounds about right," said Thane.

And that, Korroc thought, was a thing he would come back to later: that his cousin had heard *they have been doing experiments on a man we have been eating breakfast with for two months* and had answered it in the voice he used for weather.

---

Caleth told them standing up, with his back to the door.

He did not want to. Korroc could see he did not want to, in the way he kept his hands behind him and flat against the wood; but he did it, and he did it in order, the way he did everything, and Korroc stood in the middle of a dissection room with his gauntlets in his hand and listened.

"The mark," said Caleth. "It took hold of the mark. Not — " He stopped and started over. "You have seen it ache. It ached all the way down the stair before the fight, and then it went quiet when he died and it has been quiet since, and I was glad, and I was stupid to be glad. This was not an ache. This was something reaching under my skin and taking hold of it. And then I was here, and the room was clean."

"Clean," said Korroc.

"The dust was gone. The floor was not stained. The instruments were laid out in rows." Caleth's eyes were somewhere past all of them. "And the door opened behind me, and two guards came in dragging a prisoner between them. And it was him, and he was fighting them, and he was shouting at them to get their hands off him, and one of them hit him across the face."

He stopped. Korroc waited.

"They put him on the table. I never saw them do it; the thing jumped, the way a dream jumps. He was on the table with his wrists and his ankles strapped and there was something standing over him." Caleth's hands came out from behind his back. "A creature. Floating. Swollen — a great bloated head with a brain in it the size of a washtub, and under it a mass of limbs hanging down, thin ones, a dozen of them, and they moved — they moved *together*, like a man's two hands, not like an animal's legs. It had something in one of them and it put it down against his chest and he cried out."

"The crabs?" said Thane.

"No." Caleth shook his head. "No. Different. Not the same thing at all."

"Then where is it."

"I don't know."

Thane looked at the shut door, and at the ceiling, and said nothing, and his hand went to the hilt of the mother's blade and stayed there.

"And then the room was empty," said Caleth. "And the door opened again. The same two guards. The same man, fighting them. And I thought it had begun again from the start." He breathed in. "It had not. His sleeve was gone. There was blood dried along his collar that had not been there before. It was a different day."

Korroc understood then where this was going and found that he did not want it to.

"The door kept opening," said Caleth. "I do not know how many times. It went faster and faster, and every time it opened, it was another day, and every time they brought him back he was less. He fought them. Then he limped. Then he stopped trying to pull away and only walked. Then they were carrying most of his weight. Then all of it. His robes went. His own robes, that he has worn since Kenabres — one day they were torn and the next day they were not there, and there were bruises across his shoulders and his arms, and he was crying out before they had got him as far as the table. And the thing above him kept working. There was a purple light in its hands, over and over. A small crystal, flashing."

Nageru said, very quietly, "How long."

"Weeks." Caleth's voice did not change. That was the thing Korroc would remember about it afterward: it did not change at all. "Weeks and weeks. I watched a season go past in a room."

"He was taken in the spring," said Korroc.

"Yes."

"And we have been — " He stopped, because his voice had done something he had not told it to do, and he took a moment and set it straight. "We have been in Drezen. Mending straps."

Nobody answered that.

"There is more," said Caleth. "There is the end of it. I am not going to tell you the end of it standing here." He turned around and put his hand on the door. "Because I think he is through this door, and I would rather find out."

---

The daylight spell went into the room ahead of them and broke the black wall into nothing, and what it showed was cells.

A row of them down each wall. Iron bars, floor to ceiling, set into good stone — Korroc noticed, because he always noticed, that the bars were set properly, pinned at top and bottom into drilled sockets, and that somebody had cared about this room's workmanship more than they had cared about the room with the stars on the ceiling. Every cell was empty. No bed, no bucket, no straw. Just floor.

Caleth stepped over the threshold, stood a moment, and stepped back out.

"There is silence cast in there," he said. "The whole room. If you go in, be careful — you will not be able to tell anybody anything."

Thane folded his arms. "If you're not going in, I'm not going in."

Korroc laughed. He could not help it; it got out of him before he had decided anything about it. "Come on, then, lad," he said, and went in.

The silence took him at the door.

It was absolute. His boot came down on stone and made no sound at all; his armor did not speak, and a dwarf in plate always speaks. He could hear his own blood and that was all, and after two steps his ears began to hunt for something and find nothing, and he understood for the first time why Thane did not want to be in here.

But he had another way of listening.

He stood still in the middle of the floor and let his senses out. Four lives behind him in the doorway. Nageru coming in at his shoulder, bronze and quiet. And —

There. Down the left-hand side. Third cell.

A life. One. Small and low and very slow.

He went to it.

There was a shape on the floor of the cell, lying on its side with its back to the bars, and the daylight came through the bars and made a ladder of bright and dark across it. Thin. A long thin body in a tunic that had been something else once, and bare feet, and hair that had been pale and was now the color of the floor.

Korroc turned round and waved Thane in.

He saw his cousin's whole face say no. Saw him stand there in the doorway with his jaw set and his arms still folded for the space of three breaths, looking at a silent room full of cages and the one dwarf standing in it — and then shake his head, hard, the way a man shakes off water, and come in.

Thane knelt at the lock. Korroc stood over him and watched his hands and could not hear a single thing they did. At one point his cousin's mouth moved around a word that was certainly not a prayer, and the silence ate it, and Korroc almost laughed again and did not.

Then Thane stood up and pulled the door open.

Korroc went in and stopped dead.

It was Aravashnial.

He had been ready for that. He had said the words to himself coming down the row — *this is going to be him, get ready, this is going to be him* — and it had not helped in the slightest. Because he had not been ready for the rest of it. The man on the floor weighed nothing. He could see that without touching him. The arms were wrong, the way a thing is wrong when the shape you know has been taken out of it, and the hands were curled up against the chest, and all along both forearms, and along the bare shins, there were cuts — not slashes. Straight cuts. Even ones. Some old and shining, some new and crusted, in rows.

And round the neck, black against the gray skin, sat a collar.

Korroc stood there.

He was aware, distantly, of being unable to move, and aware that this was ridiculous, and that there were four people behind him and a man on the floor in front of him and that standing still was helping nobody. He could not do anything about it. Because he had seen that collar before. He had seen it come off a neck on the open ground west of Drezen two months ago and go into a half-elf's coat, and he had stood in an infirmary for the rest of that day with his bare hands on an elf's chest, and —

*and this one is still on.*

He shook himself. It was a physical thing; he actually shook, once, like a wet dog, and the silence took even that.

Then he got down on one knee and put his arms under the elf and picked him up.

He weighed nothing. Korroc had carried wounded men in plate across broken ground for half a mile and he had never in his life carried anyone who weighed so little. He went back down the row with him and out through the door, and the sound came back all at once like a door opening on a storm — boots and breath and his own armor and Caleth saying his name.

---

"Bring him to a table." Caleth was already moving. "Get that body off it and lay him down. *Thane*, get that collar off him. Korroc, heal him."

Korroc was casting before he was halfway across the floor.

He had been doing this since he was a young dwarf. He had done it in his sleep, in a flooded tunnel, on his knees in a burning courtyard with a demon forty feet away. He gathered it and shaped it and put it out into the man in his arms, the plain golden warmth of it, the Smith's mercy, the oldest and simplest thing he knew how to do.

The collar flared green.

And the spell did not land.

It went out of him and reached the elf and *stopped*, the way water stops at a sheet of glass, and ran off, and was gone. Korroc felt it go to nothing. He had not felt that happen in twenty years and he had never once felt it happen when it mattered.

He got the elf onto the table and he was not gentle enough about it and he knew it.

"Take it off," he said.

Thane did not move.

"We don't know if that's the real Aravashnial," he said.

The room went quiet.

Korroc looked up at his cousin across the body on the table. Thane was standing three feet back from it with his hands at his sides and his chin down, and his face was the face he had worn in a cell block full of silence: shut, stone, *no.*

"Thane —"

"We don't." Thane did not raise his voice. "We've had one of him for two months. He walked out of the west on his own two feet with a story and we checked him. Caleth checked him for evil and found none. Caleth checked him for magic and found none. I sat in a room and cast a working that shows a lie and he didn't tell one. Four of us looked him over for two months and got nothing, and all four of us knew something was wrong, and we talked ourselves out of it every single time. Every time." He jerked his chin at the table. "So that's a wizard in a cage in the house of the thing that took him. It's exactly what I'd put in a cage if I were them."

"Thane, he is —"

"I'm not saying leave him. I'm saying I don't know what that is, and neither do you, and the last four times I didn't know what something was, it was wearing a face."

Korroc opened his mouth and found nothing in it.

Because the plain truth was that his cousin was right and that it was worse than his cousin knew. Korroc had looked at the other one. Not once — a hundred times, over two months, across a table and a fire and an infirmary cot. He had the sight; it was the first thing the Father had ever given him and he had never had cause to doubt it, not once, not in fifty-one years —

— except once.

A chair in a cellar in Kenabres. A man in chains who had not been a man. And his sight had gone out to it and come back clean.

He felt it move under him, that old crack, the way a flaw moves in a billet when you put the hammer to it in the wrong place, and he put his boot on it and stood on it and looked across the table at his cousin.

"Then we take it off and find out," he said.

Caleth said, flatly, "And we won't find out if we don't take that collar off."

"I don't like this," said Thane. "And I don't like being pushed around."

There was a pause.

Korroc watched Caleth take a breath. He watched him put the whole of it down — the orders, the urgency, whatever enormous thing he was carrying that he had been carrying since the laboratory door — and set it aside and start again.

"Thane," said Caleth. "Please take this collar off of him."

Thane's face did not change. But he came to the table.

---

"This is different than the other one," he said, after a while.

He had the elf's head tipped gently to one side with two fingers and was going round the collar an inch at a time. It was black metal, close-fitting, and where the other had carried a single faceted green stone this one carried four, set at the quarters, and all four of them were lit from inside with a slow green pulse like something breathing.

"I see a seam," said Nageru, leaning in and pointing.

"Aye. But there's no mechanism to pick." Thane stood back, frowning. "There's nothing to get at. It's not a lock, it's a — " He made a vague irritated gesture. "It's a thing that shuts."

He reached into his sack and brought out the chime.

Korroc had seen it used exactly once, in the Gray Garrison, on a door that had no business opening. Thane held it up by its cord and struck it with a fingernail, and it rang, small and clear and strangely sweet in that room, and the note hung in the foul air and died.

Nothing happened.

Thane put the chime away and leaned back over the collar and looked at it for a long moment with his head turned, the way he looked at a floor.

Then he swore in Dwarven.

"What," said Korroc.

"It's trapped."

Caleth went very still. "Trapped how?"

"If I knew how I'd have said how." Thane's voice had gone thin. "There's a thing under the near stone that doesn't want to be under there. That's all I've got. I open this the wrong way and something happens, and the something happens about two inches from his throat."

Caleth stood over the table with his hand half-raised and nowhere to put it. Korroc watched him think and watched him not have an answer, and then watched his face change.

"Well," he said. "*Well.*"

He pulled the bag off his hip and went into it to the shoulder and came out with a book.

Korroc recognized it. It had come out of the first room in this house, out of the cabinets in Vang's study — a narrow thing bound in dark board, and Caleth had opened it in the study and gone through four pages and shut it again and said *I can't read a word of this,* and put it in the bag with the others.

Caleth opened it now on the clean end of the table and went through it fast, and Korroc, looking over his arm, saw what he had not bothered to look at two hours ago.

Drawings.

Collars. Dozens of them. A collar in plan and in section and in parts; a collar with the stones at three points and the same collar with the stones at four; a hinge drawn eleven different ways on one page with ten of them crossed through. Pages and pages of a man working out how to build a thing, with the thing getting better as the pages went on.

Thane put his hand out, palm up, and did not say anything.

Caleth gave him the book.

Thane took it to the end of the table and turned the pages toward the back, and stopped, and bent over it. He was there a long time. Korroc stood on the other side of the table with his hand resting on the elf's wrist — he could feel a pulse in it, fast and light, like a bird in a box — and watched his cousin read.

"That's it," said Thane at last, and pointed. "That one. That's what's round his neck."

Caleth came and looked. "Can you read that?"

"Enough." Thane did not look up. "I've been studying Abyssal when I could."

Korroc's eyebrows went up and he said nothing. He thought about a ledger in a cellar under Drezen with Staunton Vhane's small handwriting running down the margin in a tongue nobody at the table could read, and his cousin sitting over it for four nights with a lamp. It had been a year. His cousin had been quietly learning the language of hell for a year and had not mentioned it, because of course he had, and of course he had not.

"Good," said Caleth. "Then go slowly."

"I was going to."

He went slowly.

It took almost ten minutes, and they were ten of the longest minutes Korroc had spent in that house. Thane worked under the largest of the four stones, where the seam ran, with two picks and a thing like a dentist's probe, and every so often he stopped and went back to the book and put his finger on a line of it and moved his lips, and then came back. Nobody spoke. Nageru had put himself at the door and was watching the laboratory. Arueshalae stood at the foot of the table with her bow down and her face carefully turned away from the elf, which Korroc noted and filed and did not have time to think about.

Korroc kept his hand on the wrist.

The bird in the box went on hopping, and did not stop, and did not slow, and once or twice there was a stutter in it and Korroc's own heart stopped for about a second and then it went on again.

*Hold on, you difficult, condescending, insufferable man. Hold on. There is a dwarf here who is going to be very unhappy if you do not.*

*Click.*

It was a very small sound. All four stones went dark at once, like four candles pinched out, and the green light that had been under everything for the last quarter of an hour was simply not there.

Thane let his breath out and worked the seam with his thumbs and the collar came apart in two halves, and as he lifted the inner one away Korroc saw what came away with it: needles. A ring of them, a dozen or more, short and fine, bedded in the flesh of the elf's neck all the way round. They came out as the metal came out, one after another, each one with its own small bead of dark blood behind it.

Thane set the collar down on the table and looked at it, and then went and stood by the wall.

Korroc put both hands on the elf's chest and let the Smith's mercy go.

And this time it went in.

It went in like water into dry ground. He felt it go and felt how much of it the man took and kept taking, and all down both arms the long even cuts drew together and shut, and the raw places on the wrists where the shackles had been closed over, and the color of the face changed — not to a good color, but to a color that at least belonged to something living.

"There," said Korroc. "*There.*"

And then he started to look properly, because the mercy had closed what was open and had shown him what was not.

Under the skin of the left arm, halfway between wrist and elbow, there was a lump.

He found another in the right. Then one in each leg, below the knee. Then, when he opened the ruin of the tunic, one in the chest, right of the breastbone, deeper than the rest.

Five.

Caleth came and looked.

"They were putting something inside him," he said.

"Aye."

"Those need to come out."

"Aye," said Korroc. "I'll need to cut them out."

---

He had done surgery before. Not often, and never well; a man who can close a wound with a prayer does not get much practice at opening one. But he had hands that had spent fifty years doing small exact work with sharp tools, and that counts for more than people think.

He had no surgeon's tools in his pack and never had. What he had was the master's roll.

He unrolled it on the end of the table and found them where he had known they would be, because every forge he had ever stood in kept them in the same order and a man who lays his tools out badly does not get to be master of one. Five pairs of tongs, graduated: the big flat-jawed brutes at the far end for moving stock, and down and down, and at the near end a fine-nosed little pair no longer than his hand, made for holding a rivet steady in a flame while somebody else swung. The chisel had come out of this roll too. He had a strange moment of gratitude toward a man he had never met and whose house he was standing in.

He took the small tongs and the smallest of his own knives and he laid them both on a clean cloth, and he washed his hands with a flask of water and did not care that it was the last one, and he said a word over the blade.

He looked at the tongs a moment before he set them down. They had been made for holding a thing that was going to hurt you.

Then he cut into the left forearm.

The skin parted. And what he saw under it, immediately, before the blood even came, was purple.

"Of course it's purple," he said.

Nageru's voice came from down the wall, low and quite certain. "That is a strong evil."

Korroc reached in with his fingers to lift it out.

He got one fingertip on it.

What came up his arm was not pain. He would have preferred pain; he was on good terms with pain, they had an arrangement. This came in through his hand and up through the bone of his arm and into the inside of his head, and it was *other people*. A great crowding rush of them, all at once, hundreds of them, every one of them in the worst moment of their life — the terror of it, and the understanding, and the going-out — and all of it arriving in his skull in less than a second with nowhere to put it.

He snatched his hand back and the room came back around him.

"That hurt," he said.

He stood there a moment with his jaw working. Then he picked up the tongs.

With the steel between him and it, it was bearable, which was not the same as easy. He got the jaws round it and drew it out and it came, slick and dark, a crystal about the size of a thumb joint with a light moving slowly inside it like something at the bottom of a pond. Korroc felt it the whole way. Not the crowd this time, but the other thing — a drawing, a steady pull on something in him that was not blood, as though the stone had put a small quiet mouth against his life and begun to drink.

He turned and let it fall and it skittered away across the floor.

The pulling stopped.

"Right," he said. "One."

He moved to the other arm.

The second was worse, because he knew. Knowing made it worse, which was a thing about the world he had always resented. He made the cut and went in with the tongs straight away and did not touch it with his skin at all, and it still took everything he had to hold the reins closed and lift, and he was sweating by the time he dropped it.

Behind him, Caleth had gone down on one knee by the first crystal, not touching it, looking.

"Nahyndrian crystals," he said. "The real thing."

The name went round the room and landed. Korroc had heard it for a year. He had seen fakes in a demon's trophy room in Drezen and he had stood in front of a great one in a cellar with a tiefling's ritual round it; he had heard Arueshalae say the word in Irabeth's command room with a map under her hands. He had never seen one come out of a friend's arm.

Arueshalae came and crouched beside Caleth.

"That is what they are using to make the demons stronger," she said.

"What do you know about them?"

She shrugged, and the shrug had something uncomfortable in it. "Just that."

"Can they be destroyed?"

"Crush them."

Thane said, from a little way off, not moving, "Arueshalae. Can you take them out of him?"

Nageru's head came round at once.

"She is no longer an evil demon," he said. "Do not assume."

"I'm not assuming anything." Thane's voice had the flat careful quality of a man who is holding onto his temper with both hands and knows he is doing it. "She's not evil. Grand. She's also not a holy paladin, and that's a lump of concentrated Abyss, and it's putting my cousin on his knees, and she might be able to pick it up and put it in her pocket. That's not an insult. That's a question."

"It is the kind of question —"

"*Lad,*" said Korroc, without turning round. "Leave it."

He had his hand in a leg.

He did not want to be having this conversation and he did not have the air for it. And underneath the not wanting, he found he could not actually be angry at his cousin, which was inconvenient, because being angry would have been restful. Thane was not being cruel. Thane was being a man who had walked into a house where nothing had been what it looked like, four times running, and had now watched them pull a copy of a friend out of a cage, and did not know — *nobody* knew; that was the whole trouble; Korroc did not know either and he was the one with the hands in the man — what any of this really was. His cousin had always done his thinking out loud with a knife in it. That was not a flaw in him. It was how he had kept them all alive at the Gray Garrison.

He got the third crystal in the tongs and lifted.

And the world went away.

It was not like the others. The others had been a rush. This one got in under the rush and took hold of something at the back of his head, and he was in it — not seeing it; *in* it — and the room was gone and there was nothing but a chorus of ending, and he had a grip on the reins and could not remember what hands were.

He came back on his knees at the side of the table with his forehead against the wood and somebody's arm across his shoulders.

"— Korroc. *Korroc.*"

"Aye," he said.

His own voice sounded a long way off and about an inch high. He got his eyes open. The room swam and steadied. His face was wet and his hands were shaking in a way they had not shaken since he was nineteen, and there was a crystal on the floor three feet away that he did not remember dropping.

He looked up.

Aravashnial was on the table above him with two crystals still inside him.

Korroc got his boots under himself. He got up. He put his hand on the table and he was steady enough, more or less, and he picked the knife back up and found the place on the elf's other leg where the fourth one sat, and set the edge against the skin, and made the cut.

And then he reached for the tongs and there was a hand already on them.

"I'm not a paladin either," said Thane.

Korroc looked at him.

His cousin's face had not changed. It was still shut; it was the same face it had been three feet back from the table, the one that said *I don't know what that is.* But the hand on the tongs was absolutely steady and it was already moving, and Thane bent over the leg and put the jaws in and had the thing out in about two seconds flat, the way a man pulls a nail.

He went white.

He did not stop. He straightened with it held out at arm's length and swore in Dwarven, long and quietly and with real craftsmanship, and finished with:

"*Damned.* No wonder."

He turned and dropped it with the others.

"All right?" said Korroc.

"No." Thane wiped the tongs on his sleeve and handed them back. "Do the chest."

---

The one in the chest was deeper.

Korroc cut down to it, slowly, with the elf's breath going in and out under his hands and the lamp held over his shoulder by a half-elf who did not say one word the entire time. The flesh here was thicker, and the thing had been put in a long way, and whoever had put it there had not been careful about what they went through on the way.

"It's deep," he said.

"Can you reach it?"

"Aye."

He got his fingers down and opened the way, and Thane came in over his hands with the tongs and found it and took hold and drew it up.

It came out slick with blood and it was bigger than the others.

And Thane stopped.

He had it up at eye height, held out in the steel, and he had gone completely still. Not the still he went when he was listening at a lock. Korroc, looking up from the open chest with both hands full, saw his cousin's face start to do something he had no name for: the mouth pulling, the eyes fixed, and something behind the eyes going somewhere else.

"Thane."

Nothing.

"Thane. Put it —"

Caleth's hand came across and knocked it out of the tongs.

It hit the floor and rolled and Thane came back into himself all at once with a sharp indrawn breath, and Caleth, standing there with his hand still out, swore at himself in a voice Korroc had never heard him use.

"That thing was watching us."

"It was talking to me," said Thane.

Nobody said anything for a second.

Then Arueshalae, from the end of the table: "Some crystals still contain the souls of demon lords."

Korroc looked at her. Then he looked down at the thing on the floor.

Then he put the elf's chest closed with a wash of gold, took two steps back, and pulled his hammer off his belt.

"Then we crush it."

Thane threw up both hands. "And if you let something out?"

"Then get ready to fight."

He said it plainly, because it was plain. He had been in this house for most of a day. There had been four locked doors and a secret wall and a room lined in lead, and behind every one of them there had been a thing somebody had made and kept and taken care of, and he had been walking round them all afternoon being careful. He was finished being careful. There was a stone on the floor of this room with somebody's soul in it, and it had just spoken to his cousin, and he had a hammer.

He set his feet and swung.

The first blow chipped it. A piece came off the shoulder of it and spun away, and the light inside did not change.

The second blow put a crack in it, from the top down, and he saw the light inside shift.

He swung again, and again. Each one found the crack and drove it deeper, and the crack forked, and forked again, and the thing on the floor began to look less like a stone and more like a window somebody had thrown a brick at. The hammer rang off the flagstones each time. He could feel the pull of it on him every time he came close, that small quiet mouth drinking, and he did not care.

Behind him he was aware of Caleth moving — of the half-elf putting himself bodily between the floor and the table, with his back to Korroc and his arms a little out, shielding the unconscious elf from whatever was about to come out of it.

Sixth blow.

It burst.

It went off with a flat crack and the pieces went everywhere, and out of the middle of the shards came a thin coil of dark smoke, rising, turning —

and thinning —

and gone.

Silence.

"Well," said Caleth, after a moment. "That was anticlimactic."

"Aye," said Korroc. He lowered the hammer. He discovered that his arms were aching. "It was."

Nageru had gone to one knee by the shards and was looking at them with his head tipped. He stayed there a while. He did not say what he was looking for and nobody asked.

Thane had not stopped frowning.

"Get the rest," he said. "All four of them. Now, while we've got the hammer out."

They did. Korroc broke the other four on the stone floor of a dissection room and each one went the same way: chip, crack, crack, burst, and a little nothing rising off the shards. He felt lighter with every one. By the last of them his hands had stopped shaking.

He swept the shards into a corner with his boot and went back to the table.

---

"He's not waking," said Caleth.

"No." Korroc had his hands on the elf again and was going over him the way you go over a piece you have just taken off the anvil: everywhere, slowly, with the flats of the fingers. "He's dehydrated. He's starving — there's nothing on him, look at this, there's nothing on the man at all. He's exhausted past the far end of exhausted." He turned the slack hand over and pressed the nail bed and watched the color come back too slowly. "And he's got a fever. Slight. That'll be the leavings of it. Whatever they were doing with those stones, it was still being done when we walked in."

He healed the incisions he had made, with the wand, because he had a wand for ordinary work and a man should not spend the Father's own grace on five neat cuts he put there himself. Then he spent one anyway on top of it, because he wanted to.

Then he put both hands flat on the elf's chest and prayed the other prayer — the one that is not for wounds. The one for what has been taken out of a man that is not blood.

It went in. He felt it go in and felt it find things and begin to put them back; and the eyelids under the tangled hair fluttered, and the breath went deeper, and the fever eased a half a degree.

And it was not enough.

He could feel it. That was the maddening part. There was something in there that his hands could not get hold of, the way you can feel a flaw in a bar and not find it with a file. It was not the hunger and it was not the fever and it was not the cuts.

"Something's still not right with him," he said.

Caleth did not answer that directly. He was standing at the head of the table looking down, and when he spoke it was to no one in particular.

"They gave him something," he said. "A purple liquid. In a glass vial. Thick, with a light in it that moved slowly."

Arueshalae's head came up.

"They gave him a *potion?*" she said. "And he is still alive?"

Korroc looked round at her.

"Wait," he said. "Say that again."

"The elixir." She had come a step closer and her face had changed; it was the first time Korroc had seen anything like fear on her. "The Nahyndrian elixir. It is what they make the crystals into. It is what they gave the demons in the cells, when I was in the cells. It is — " she searched for it " — it is not survivable. Not usually. Most of the ones they gave it to in the beginning died, and the ones who did not were not the same afterward. For a mortal it is worse. For a mortal it is a way of dying."

"But Caleth said they were making him a vessel," said Korroc slowly. "So they were going to give him the potion. And then — "

He stopped.

He looked down at the table. At the long thin body of a man he had sat up with for an afternoon with his bare hands on his chest, and the raw ring of needle marks round his throat, and the closed cut over the breastbone where something had been living under the skin.

"— and then put another being inside him," he said.

Arueshalae nodded, slowly.

"You took the thing they were putting in him," she said, "out of his chest."

Korroc turned his head and looked at the corner where the shards were.

"Oh, my Gods," he said. "They were going to make him a puppet for —"

He did not finish it. There was not any need to finish it, and he found that he could not, and he stood there with his hand still resting on the elf's shoulder and his mouth shut.

---

"Korroc," said Caleth.

He had not moved from the head of the table. His hands were behind him again.

"I said I would tell you the end of it."

"Aye."

"Then I am going to tell you now, because you are standing there deciding that we were too late, and we were not." Caleth's voice was perfectly even, which Korroc had learned to distrust entirely. "Listen to me.

"At the end, the room was different. The table was pushed aside and there was a chair in its place, a heavy one, and he was strapped into it at the wrists and the ankles and across the chest. The collar was on him. He was marked all over from everything that came before. He was barely breathing."

He paused.

"And Vang was there. Standing in front of him. Moving about the room the way things move in a dream — in front of the chair, and then beside it, and then across the room, without ever crossing the distance between. And he had a vial in his hand with a thick dark liquid in it and a purple light moving in it slowly." Caleth's jaw tightened. "And he took hold of Aravashnial by the hair and pulled his head back, and Aravashnial saw what it was. And for the first time in the whole of it I heard him speak clearly, and what he said was *no.* And then *no, please.* And then *don't* —"

"Lad," said Korroc.

"And Vang forced his mouth open and poured it in," said Caleth, "and held him until he swallowed it."

Nobody moved. Nageru had come in off the door.

"He convulsed. He arched against the straps and screamed — not a word, not a plea, nothing in it but pain. And then he went rigid, and his head went back, and his eyes opened, and they were *black.* All of it. Edge to edge." Caleth took a short breath. "And Vang leaned over him and said, *yes. There you are.*"

"The crystal," said Korroc.

"The crystal. Or whatever was in it." Caleth did not look at the corner where the shards were. "And then he spoke, except it was not his voice — it was something low and guttural underneath, and for a moment there were two of them coming out of the same throat at once. One deep, and full of a kind of malice I have no word for. And the other one faint. Strained. Almost lost under it."

Korroc found that he had put his hand back on the elf's shoulder.

"And Vang was *delighted*," said Caleth. "He said, *remarkable. The transfer has begun.* And he said *now, show me.*"

"And?"

"And it did not finish."

Caleth said it flatly, and then he said it again, differently.

"It did not finish. The eyes went black and then they were green again. And then black. And then green. Over and over, and his whole body jerked every time it changed, like a man being pulled in two directions by the arms. And Vang stopped walking about and bent over him and said, *curious. Why are you still there?*"

Thane had gone very still at the end of the table.

"Then he started to struggle," said Caleth. "Not against the straps. Against something in the room with him that I could not see, and neither could Vang, and he was shaking with the effort of it. And he opened his mouth and a scream came out, and halfway through the scream the sound changed into something monstrous — and then it broke. And he gasped, in his own voice, and what he said was: *get out.*"

Korroc heard somebody let out a breath and realized it had been him.

"Vang said, *that is not possible.* Vang said, *your resistance should have been extinguished.* He said it twice. He was walking round the chair again, and his voice was going up, and he was saying *you cannot possibly maintain control* — " Caleth stopped and began again. "And then it stopped. All of it. He slumped forward in the chair and went still, and Vang waited, and then there was a breath. Just a breath. A weak, broken, ordinary breath, in his own voice. And Vang stepped *back* from him."

"Back," said Nageru.

"Back." Caleth's mouth moved. It was not a smile. "He said, *no.* And then he took hold of the back of the chair with both hands and said, *no, no, no. The vessel was prepared. The elixir was correct. The transfer should have been complete.*"

He let that stand in the room for a moment.

"And then he came round in front of him and got a fistful of his hair and pulled his head up and shouted in his face: *what did you do?*"

Korroc looked down at the man on the table.

A starved elf with a ring of needle marks round his throat and five closed cuts in him and a face like a dropped candle, who weighed nothing at all, who had been dragged through a door onto a slab every day for the better part of a season and had gone from fighting the guards to being carried.

"And what did he say," said Korroc.

"*Nothing.*" Caleth's voice finally did something. "That is what he said. He got his head up far enough to speak and he said *nothing*, and Vang called him a liar and struck him across the face, and said *you have interfered with the process. Somehow you have prevented the transfer.* And he said, *you will tell me how you did this.* And then he was gone, and the room was empty, and the elf was still in the chair with his head down and I could not tell whether he was breathing. And then it let me go."

The infirmary smell of the room, the sweet rot of it, seemed to have got stronger.

Korroc put both hands on the edge of the table.

"He beat him," he said.

"Yes."

"Strapped to a chair. With a demon lord's soul being poured into him and nothing of his own left at all." Korroc heard his own voice go rough and let it. "He beat Xanthir Vang."

"Yes," said Caleth. "And Vang never found out how. He was still asking."

Thane said, from the end of the table, quietly, "Neither will we. Not till he can talk."

"No."

Korroc looked at the elf for a long moment.

"Insufferable man," he said. "*Insufferable* man."

---

Caleth had gone into his pack.

He came out with a scroll, and unrolled it, and read it aloud, and whatever the working was it settled over him and sat there like a coat. Then he reached into the bag and brought out the great leather book off the laboratory table — the one he had stood over twenty minutes ago and said *I don't know the shape of the language* — and opened it on the clean end of the table and bent over it.

And began, immediately, to read.

Korroc watched him do it and thought, not for the first time, that of all the strange things he had got used to in the last year, the strangest was probably this: that a man could be handed a page of marks that meant nothing at all, and say six words, and then the marks meant something. He had no feeling for it. He had tried once to explain the difference between a good weld and a bad one to Caleth, and had watched him nod intelligently and not understand a word, and it had been oddly comforting.

Caleth read fast, muttering. His finger went down a page and turned it, and down the next, and his eyebrows went in.

Then he said: "I feel like this is the real Aravashnial. But I don't know how."

"Say the rest," said Thane.

"The book says they were trying to make him a spy." Caleth turned another page. "That is what all of this was. Not a soldier, not a weapon — a *spy.* They wanted a man who could walk into Drezen. And he resisted. That word is in here four times in three pages, in a hand that gets worse each time." He looked up. "It was taking too long. So they stopped, and they went with a different plan."

Nobody said anything at all.

Korroc put it together with his hands still on the table. It did not take long. It was not a complicated piece of work; it was a very simple piece of work, and that was the horror in it. *They wanted a man who could walk into Drezen. They could not make this one do it. So they made another one.*

And it had walked out of the west four mornings after they buried his father, and fallen down in the dirt, and said *been walking, for days.*

And he had laid his life on it.

He had knelt on an infirmary floor with his bare hands on a thing's chest until the sun went down, and bound his own blood to it, and carried its wounds for two months. He had felt it hurt in a petrified forest and had shouted for Caleth to go to it. He had felt the thread go slack when it was carried off to the bell fortress and had read that as *far*, not dead, and had been pleased, in his small private way, when it came back.

And his sight had looked straight at it.

A hundred times. Across a table in the command room. Over a fire below the fane, where something about the man had been wrong enough that Korroc's eyes had come up and found Nageru's eyes already waiting — and they had looked at each other, and looked at Caleth, and all three of them had said nothing, because none of them could find one single solid thing to put a name to.

And every time, the sight had come back clean.

Twice now, he thought. Twice in fifty-one years the Father's own gift had looked at a thing wearing a face and told him there was nothing in it.

He took his hand off the table.

"Korroc," said Caleth. "Are you —"

"His eyes," said Korroc.

Because they had opened.

"They were purple," he said, and he heard how strange his own voice sounded and could not help it. "Just now, when they opened. I'd swear to it. And they're — look. *Look.*"

They were going green.

It happened over about three seconds, like a stain running out of cloth: the flat violet going out of the iris from the edge inward, and under it, coming up, the pale cold green that Korroc had seen looking at him over the top of a book for a year.

Caleth was moving before Korroc had finished speaking.

---

Panic crossed the elf's face.

It did not build. It arrived, whole, the way it does in a man who has been woken suddenly too many times — the eyes going wide and the whole body jerking under Korroc's hands and trying to get away from all of them at once.

"Don't worry." Caleth was at the head of the table with both hands up, palms out. "It's us. We're here to rescue you."

Aravashnial got one elbow under himself. He had no strength at all; the arm shook with the weight of him and nearly went. He got himself up a few inches and went backward along the table, dragging, away from Caleth, away from Korroc, until his shoulder hit the far edge and there was nowhere left.

He looked at Caleth. Then at Nageru, standing a little behind. Then at Korroc.

"Is that really you this time?"

Korroc felt that land in him somewhere low and stay there.

*This time.*

"Last time I checked," said Caleth, "it's us."

The elf took a breath. It shook. He shook his head — once, twice, faster, the way a man does when he is arguing with himself and losing.

"No," he said. "No. It's a trick. It's a trick. It's Vang."

"Vang is dead."

Caleth put his hand into the bag and came out with a double armful of scorched black cloth and held it up in front of him, high, the way a man holds up a banner he has taken off a field.

"Vang is *dead,*" he said. "Look at it. I burned him out of these in his own hall, and there was not enough of him left afterward to put back into them. These are in my bag. He is not coming through that door."

The elf looked at the robes. Korroc watched him look — watched the eyes go over the hanging sleeves and the holes burned through them, and watched something behind the eyes take hold of what it meant and then lose its grip on it.

"It's a *trick* — "

And then he saw Arueshalae.

Korroc had forgotten she was standing there. She had not moved and she had not spoken and she was at the foot of the table with her bow pointed at the floor, and the elf's eyes went past Caleth and found her, and whatever had been happening in his face stopped and something far worse took its place. He made a sound. He got his heels under himself on the tabletop and shoved backward and would have gone straight off the end of it if Korroc had not got a forearm behind his shoulders.

"That's Arueshalae." Caleth kept his hands up and his voice level and did not step closer. "She is with us. She's been blessed by Desna."

The elf stopped.

Korroc watched the name go into him and find something to hold on to. He did not relax — there was nothing in him loose enough to relax — but the thrashing stopped, and he lay back against Korroc's arm, breathing hard, staring at her over Caleth's shoulder with an expression Korroc could not read at all.

"Desna," he said.

"Yes."

"Yes," said Aravashnial. "Yes. All right."

He did not say anything else. He shut his eyes, and his breathing began to even out, and in about a minute he was somewhere between sleep and not.

Nobody told him about the other one.

Korroc thought about it. He stood there with his arm behind a starved man's shoulders and he thought about saying *there is something in Drezen wearing your face* and he looked at what was in front of him — the needle marks, the shaking, *is that really you this time* — and he did not say it. He looked up and found Caleth's eye and found that Caleth had got to the same place by a different road, and the two of them put it away without a word.

---

"We have to get him out," said Caleth.

He had his hands in his hair, which he did about once a month. "And I cannot fold us out from inside this house. The whole place is locked — I tried it in the fight upstairs and it is in the stone, not in any one room. So we walk him out. Through the cells, the laboratory, the webs, the hall, the labyrinth stair, the back way, two illusory walls and a gorge."

"So we walk back out with him," said Nageru.

"Yes."

"Actually." Nageru tilted his head. "We pulled a carpet out of that first bedroom. Did it not look magic?"

Caleth stopped with his hands still in his hair.

"Yes," he said slowly. "Yes, it did. I have not had time to study it."

"You have time."

Caleth looked at him. Then he pulled the bag round and opened it and put his arm into it up past the elbow, and then past the shoulder, which Korroc had watched him do perhaps thirty times and had never once got used to; and he came out with a roll of heavy cloth.

He unfurled it across the floor. It was a rug — a good one, deep red, worked all over with a pattern of knotwork that doubled back on itself, with a long fringe at each end. He crouched over it with his hand a half-inch above the weave and said a few words and went quiet, and his eyebrows went up.

"Yes," he said. He sat back on his heels and looked up at all of them. "It's a flying carpet."

Nageru chuckled. It was a rare sound and it did everyone in the room good.

"Then I think," he said, "it has just become a flying stretcher."

---

They laid him on it with Korroc's cloak under his head, and it took his weight and sat a foot off the floor and stayed there, steady as a table, and Korroc put a hand on the edge of it and pushed and it went where he pushed it.

"Right," he said. "That's better than I expected, and I'll not pretend otherwise."

They went out through the laboratory, Caleth steering the carpet with two fingers on the fringe and Thane ahead at the doors; and in the laboratory Nageru stopped a moment at the heap of clothing by the western slab, and did not go through it.

Nobody went through it. Korroc looked at the pile and at the thing floating along at waist height beside him and understood perfectly well that there was a time to do an inventory and this was not it, and that everyone had come to the same conclusion at the same moment and that nobody was going to say so.

They left it where it was.

---

Out through the web room, which smelled now of burned vegetation and dead crab, and up the ten feet of stairs, and into the middle hall.

And at the hall Thane stopped and pointed across it.

"Those," he said.

A pair of double doors in the hall wall that nobody had opened. Not the stair. Not the web room. Just a door, in a house where they had now opened everything else.

"Should we check them? Just in case."

Caleth looked at the doors. Then he looked down at the carpet and the man on it, and Korroc watched him weigh a thing with a sick man on one side of it and the words *just in case* on the other, and lose, because he always lost that one.

He sighed. "Probably."

Thane checked them, opened them, and went through the gap sideways without seeming to open it very far.

They waited.

He came back in about two minutes and spoke very low.

"Wide hallway. Snakes off south a bit. Doors north, doors south. Weapon rack in the corner — big stuff, oversized, two glaives in it." A pause. "And there's snoring."

"Snoring," said Korroc.

"North door. Two of them."

He was already going. Korroc opened his mouth to say something about waiting and shut it again, because there was no point and never had been.

Thane was gone a little under a minute.

What Korroc saw, standing in the mouth of the hallway with the carpet at his hip, was: his cousin going through a door with no more sound than a cat; a pause; and his cousin coming out again, wiping both blades on a rag, with his face set exactly as it had been when he went in.

"Two fiendish minotaurs," he said. "Asleep. They're not now."

"Did they wake?"

"One made a noise." Thane put the rag away. "Not much of one."

Korroc thought about it for a second — two of them, horned, half again the height of a man, the same breed that had nearly taken his head off in Staunton's hall — and about the fact that his cousin had done both of them in the dark in under a minute, and that the only report he was going to get was *not much of one*.

"Good lad," he said.

Thane snorted and went to the south door.

---

It was locked. Thane looked at the lock for a moment, and then put the picks away and reached into his coat and came out with a ring of keys.

"Where in the Father's name did you get those?"

"Off him." Thane did not look up. "In the hall, after. They were in the robes."

He tried three and the fourth turned.

The room behind it was large and, for the first time in that house, comfortable.

That was what Korroc could not get past. A writing desk in one corner, good work, walnut. A full-length mirror in the second, in a carved frame. A semicircular bath sunk into the floor in the third, with a step down into it and a shelf for oils. A bed in the fourth, canopied, hung with dark red stuff, with a coverlet turned down.

And in the middle of the room, in the open space between all four, a thing that was half a gurney and half a table, with leather straps at its corners, and the straps were black and stiff with old blood.

And a set of manacles on a short chain, bolted to the near post of the bed.

Korroc stood in the doorway and looked at it and did not go in.

He was aware of Nageru coming up behind him, and stopping, and of neither of them saying anything. A man gets a certain number of things in a day that he can stand to have explained to him and Korroc had used his up in the room with the slabs. He did not want this one explained. He understood it entirely, standing in the door, without a word from anybody, and understood also that there had been a woman in this house with four arms and a dog's head who had worn the shape of a beautiful elf-woman for years and had used this room, and that she was not dead — she was *gone*, for a hundred years, home, barred out, which was the best anybody got — and that she had used this room on people.

"We'll not be taking anything out of here," he said, "that we don't have to."

"No," said Nageru.

Caleth went to the desk.

The wardrobe held clothes, and the clothes were the ones she had worn — Korroc recognized a gown from the cavern at the Molten Scar, the color of it, and had to look away. The desk held paper. Caleth went through it standing up, fast, and about four sheets in his hands stopped.

"These are Templar," he said. "These are their signs. Their recognition signs, their marks, their — this is how you tell one of theirs from a man off the street. This is Anevia's whole year of work in six pages." He squared them and put them in the bag without reading further. "She can have them tonight."

Then he went still again, and came up with a single sheet.

"And this," he said, "was going to Hepzamirah."

He read it out.

Korroc leaned against the doorframe and listened, and what came across to him, more than anything in it, was the *tone*. It was a letter from one person of quality to another about a mutual acquaintance who was becoming tiresome. It called Vang *our esteemed representative of the Lord of the Locust Host*, and complained about his *dalliance with his guests*, and about his *pastimes*, in the voice of a woman who has found her colleague keeping ferrets in the pantry.

The guests were the crab-things. That came clear about four lines in. *The Dark Tapestry* — Caleth's voice changed slightly when he read that — and *these aliens in your Father's Ivory Sanctum*, and, a line later, in a phrase Korroc did not expect to be able to forget, *some clattering tangle of legs and brains.*

"The brains," said Nageru.

"Yes." Caleth did not look up. "They were not his guests only. He was *supplying* them."

He read it.

*Vang has been supplying them with victims intended for sacrifice. He knows better than to short my needs for the rituals, yet still. These mortals belong to your Father, not some clattering tangle of legs and brains.*

Korroc shut his eyes.

Two slabs in an octagonal room, with the shackles on, and a pile of clothing at the foot of one of them that had been cut off rather than taken off. *These mortals belong to your Father.* A quarrel over whose they were.

"Go on," he said.

Caleth went on. The letter said that she had not managed to crack the guests' minds and bend them to her wishes, *nor Vang either, for that matter* — Thane made a short noise at that — but that she would, and that when she did, the crusade would have an interesting new tool used against it, and that Vang would simply have to accept that the guests had never been his at all.

And then it changed subject, pleasantly.

*The effects of Nahyndrian elixir continues to impress me. My own experiences, combined with the fact that the last four demons who imbibed survived with ease, tell me that Minagho's serpentine scum and her insane scientist have finally perfected the recipe.*

Korroc came off the doorframe.

"Minagho," he said.

"Yes."

"That's the one that holds the broken city. That's the one in the letter from Kenabres — the first one, out of the cellar under the Tower of Estrod, the one that set Vorlesh on the wardstone." He was aware that his voice had got loud and did not moderate it. "That's the one at the top of the ladder Nurah came up. That name has been over our heads since the first week of all this."

"Yes," said Caleth.

"And she's got a *recipe*."

"She has somebody who has a recipe." Caleth's finger was on the page. "'Her serpentine scum and her insane scientist.' Two people, neither of them named." He looked up. "And they have *perfected* it. The last four demons who drank it survived with ease. It did not use to work. Now it does."

Arueshalae had come to the door. Her face was very still.

"The ones in the cells did not survive with ease," she said.

"No. And he gave it to a man." Caleth's eyes went to the doorway, to the carpet floating in the hall beyond with the elf asleep on it. "He gave it to a mortal, because he wanted a vessel, and he did it with a recipe that has only lately stopped killing *demons.*"

Nobody answered.

"There's a little more," said Caleth, and read it.

*I look forward to the next shipment from the Fane, and hope to be able to join you soon to aid you in recruiting more of the lords and ladies of Midnight to our cause.*

"The Fane," said Korroc.

"Capital F." Caleth was looking at the word. "Not ours. Not Nageru's. Somewhere else entirely, and somewhere that *ships*." He shook his head slowly. "I don't know it. We will write it down and give it to Irabeth and she will put it on the list."

"And the last line?"

Caleth turned the sheet over, and turned it back.

"'And in regard to the Drezen situation, you need not fear. I am in the process of'," he read.

He stopped.

"That's it," he said. "That's where it stops. Mid-sentence, at the bottom of the page, and there is no second page."

Thane was already at the desk, going through it properly — every drawer out, the backs felt, the underside of the top, the carpet lifted at the corner. He did it fast and thoroughly and gave it about ninety seconds.

"Nothing," he said. "She never wrote it. It's a letter she was in the middle of."

"Then it was today," said Nageru.

Korroc looked at the half-written page in Caleth's hand, and thought about a beautiful furious woman standing at the foot of a stair in a long black hall waiting for them to come down, who had got up from this desk to do it with her pen still wet.

"Aye," he said. "Well. She'll have a hundred years to finish it."

---

They went out the way they had come in: back through the hall and the rooms and the stair and the long passages, and the carpet followed Caleth's two fingers the whole way with the elf asleep on it. Through the forge, and the kitchen with its well-stocked pantry, and the ivory room with its three doors standing open and the goat-head on the east wall holding its moon; down the carved stairs and through the great cavern and out through two walls of rock that were not rock, which Korroc walked straight into one after the other, exactly as he had on the way in, and said nothing about on the grounds that nobody else had either.

They came out into the gorge in the late afternoon.

It was cold. The light came down the hundred feet of broken wall and did not reach the floor, and there was a wind in it from the west. Korroc stood in it for a moment with his eyes shut and let it go through his beard, and did not care in the slightest that it smelled of ash and alkali. It was outside air. It had not been breathed by anything in that house.

They went east along the gorge floor, well away from the front gate and the thing that Arueshalae said was sitting in front of it, until Caleth stopped and looked around and up and said this was far enough.

Then he counted them, and his face fell.

"I can't take all six of us," he said. "Not in one. Five, at a stretch, and I would not stretch it with him on the carpet."

Korroc nodded. It was not even a decision.

"Then take yourself, him, Thane and the lass," he said. "Nageru and I will stay and wait for you to come back."

Caleth looked at him. "I will be ten minutes."

"Aye. Go on."

They went. The air folded over on itself and they were not there, and the carpet and the sick man and the half-elf and his cousin and the woman with the bow were all simply gone, and the gorge was very large and very quiet.

Korroc sat down on a rock.

Nageru did not. He stood a little way off with his hands loose at his sides, facing west along the floor of the gorge toward the Sanctum's hidden front, and did not move at all. After a while Korroc stopped expecting him to.

The wind went along the gorge. Somewhere high up, a bird.

Korroc put his hands on his knees and looked at them. There was blood under the nails of both. It was not his.

And because there was nothing else to do, and because a man alone in a quiet place will always go and press on the sore tooth, he reached for the thread.

Not the live ones. Those were there: three of them, thin with distance now, stretched out east and north toward a camp at a ruined fortress a week's ride away, and he could feel all three and they were fine. He reached past them, to where the fourth one had been.

Nothing.

There had been a thread to Aravashnial since the fourth morning after the graves. He had laid it on the floor of an infirmary with the man asleep in front of him and he had never once taken it off. It had gone when Caleth folded the elf to the bell fortress and come back when they arrived, like a dog coming out from under a table — *there you are* — and it had frayed away again when they rode off west, and he had read that, correctly, as distance.

And now there was nothing, and there had been nothing for days, and he could not tell.

That was the whole of it. He sat on a rock in a demon's dooryard with his hands on his knees and he could not tell whether the thread was gone because the thing it had been tied to was four days' ride away, or because the thing it had been tied to had never had a life in it to tie to in the first place, or because it had, and it did, and it was somewhere in Drezen right now wearing a face.

He had felt it hurt in a petrified forest. He was almost sure of that. He had turned round in the dark with worms coming out of the ground and shouted *Caleth, you need to find Aravashnial, he's being attacked* — and he had been right, and something had been up a tree with a bite in its leg, and Caleth had laid hands on it.

So there had been something there.

What, though.

"Korroc," said Nageru.

"Mm."

"You are thinking very loudly."

Korroc looked up. The monk had not turned round.

"Aye," he said. "I am."

Nageru waited the way he waited, which was the most restful thing about him and occasionally the most annoying.

"At the fane," said Korroc at last. "At the fire, when he was telling us about the hag. You looked up and I looked up."

"Yes."

"We were right."

"Yes," said Nageru.

"And we said nothing."

"We had nothing to say." Nageru turned round at last. His face in the gorge-light was as still as it always was. "You looked at me and I looked at you, and then we both looked at Caleth, and Caleth had seen it too, and the three of us knew the same thing: that something was wrong, and that not one of us could name a single thing that was wrong. I have thought about it since. If I had spoken, I would have said, *there is something in his manner.* And Caleth would have said, *he has been a prisoner for two months.* And he would have been right."

"We'd have put a sight on him."

"Caleth did put a sight on him." Nageru said it gently. "Three of them. There was no evil in him, there was no magic on him, and there was no tracking. Thane cast a working over him that shows a lie and he told none. We did everything there is to do, brother. There was nothing to find."

Korroc looked back down at his hands.

*There was nothing to find.* He turned it over and did not much like the shape of it. It was meant as a comfort and it was the opposite of one, because the comfort would have been *we were careless.* Careless is fixable. A man can be less careless. What Nageru had just handed him was: *you did everything, and it was not enough, and there is no version of this where it would have been.*

"I looked at it," he said.

"I know."

"No, lad. I *looked* at it." He heard his own voice drop and let it. "That's not a wizard's trick, what I do. That's not a spell I learned out of a book and might have done wrong. That's the first thing the Father ever put in me. I was nineteen years old in a shop in Kenabres and I looked at a man and I *knew*, and I have never once had to wonder since." He opened his hands and closed them. "And I have looked at that thing across a table a hundred times and it came back clean. Every time. Clean as a new bar."

Nageru said nothing.

"And it's the second time," said Korroc.

Then he shut his mouth, because he had not meant to say that, and because the first time had a chair in it and a man in chains in a cellar below Drezen and he had never told anyone, in the whole of the year since, exactly what it had done to him.

Nageru, to his great credit, did not ask.

He came over instead and sat down on the rock next to him, which took a while because Nageru sitting down was a production, and the two of them watched the wind come up the gorge.

"When I was a boy," said Nageru eventually, "I was taught that the hand is not the eye. That a man who has spent his life training one of them will trust it past its reach, because it has never failed him, and the failing will come as a betrayal rather than a limit." He turned his head. "It is a limit, Korroc. It was always a limit. You simply had not found the edge of it before."

"That's meant to help, is it."

"No," said Nageru. "It is meant to be true. Helping is different and I am not good at it."

Korroc laughed, which surprised him.

And then the air folded over itself and Caleth was standing in the gorge ten feet away looking harried, and said, "Right. Both of you. Quickly."

---

What happened next happened in pieces and Korroc did not have hold of all of them.

They went from the gorge to the camp at the bell fortress, which was a shock in itself — one moment the cold shadow of a hundred-foot scar and the next a hillside in the last of the sun with tents on it and a cookfire going and forty people turning round. Caleth put them down in the middle of it and said six words to a wizard Korroc had never seen before, a nervous young man in Drezen's colors with ink on his fingers, and then folded away again and was gone.

The camp was wrong. That was the first thing Korroc registered. Not frightened — *wrong*, the particular churn of a place where news has just gone through. People standing up from things they had been doing. Anevia halfway across the slope with her hand on a tent rope, looking at the place where Caleth had been.

"What's happened?" said Korroc.

Anevia came over. Her face had the closed, fast look it got when she was working.

"Your half-elf came in out of the air with Aravashnial on a flying rug," she said, "and then told me the Aravashnial who rode back to Drezen with Aron this morning is an impostor, and then went away again. That's everything I know and I'd be grateful if you'd fill in literally any of it."

Korroc opened his mouth.

"Rode back this morning," he said.

"There was an attack on the citadel. Aron went. Aravashnial went with him." Her eyes were on his face and reading it, the way she read everything. "Korroc. What's the matter with you?"

"Nothing," said Korroc. "Everything. Later."

The young wizard with the ink on his fingers cleared his throat and said he was ready when they were, and Korroc, who had been folded through the world more times in the last two months than he had ever expected to be in his life, took hold of Nageru's elbow out of some obscure instinct and said, "Go on, then, lad. Mind the armor."

---

They came out in a corridor in Citadel Drezen, in torchlight, with the smell of wet stone and boiled oats, and Korroc had never in his life been so glad to be anywhere.

It lasted about fifteen seconds.

They were outside the command room. They stood in the corridor — the young wizard excused himself with great speed — and before Korroc had finished getting his bearings the air at the far end of the hall folded and Caleth and Thane came out of it.

"He's in my quarters," said Caleth, without preamble. "On the bed. Arueshalae is sitting with him. Come on."

The command room had Irabeth in it, and Aron, and Sosiel, all three standing over the central table with papers and a map spread under their hands.

"You're back," said Aron, looking up. "Good. While you've been gone we've had more of the drakes and riders, three times, nothing we couldn't handle —"

"Where is Aravashnial?" said Caleth.

Aron blinked at the interruption. "In his study. He wanted to read up on the bell, he said he'd —"

"Come with me."

He was already turning.

"What is going on?" said Irabeth.

"We'll explain soon." Caleth was across the room. "I have someone to check on first."

Korroc saw Irabeth's face do the thing it did — the brief flat displeasure of a commander being walked out on in her own command room, and then the decision, arriving about a second later, to let it happen because the man walking out was one of the four and did not do this.

"Aron," she said. "Go with them. I'll be here."

---

The study was two floors up and along, and they went fast, and Korroc went last because of the plate and because of his knees.

He arrived about four seconds behind everyone else, and he knew before he got there, because he could hear nothing, and because when he came round the door Caleth was standing in the middle of the room with his hands open at his sides.

It was empty.

Not disturbed. That was the worst part of it. The lamp was lit. There were three books open on the desk, one of them face-down to hold a place. There was a cup of something, half drunk, and when Korroc put the back of his fingers against it, it was cool but not cold.

A chair pushed back from the desk at an angle, the way a man pushes it back when he gets up intending to come straight back.

Caleth said something under his breath that Korroc was fairly sure he had learned from Thane.

"Right," said Aron. "Right, I've been very patient. *What is going on?*"

Caleth turned round.

"We rescued Aravashnial from the Ivory Sanctum," he said. "Today. He has been in a cell there since the spring. He is in my quarters, on my bed, asleep, and he weighs about as much as a boy." He let that sit for exactly one breath. "The one who has been here is an impostor."

Korroc watched Aron Kir take it.

Aron was not a fast man. He was a careful man, which was better, and Korroc had watched him be careful about a great many things in a year — a wall, a ration schedule, a boy with a crushed hand. He stood there in the doorway of an empty study and Korroc saw him go through it: the man at the table. The man who had come walking out of the west. The man who had gone with him to the bell fortress and sat at the foot of a tower telling soldiers not to touch a bell. The man who had ridden back from that fortress with him this morning.

"He was —" said Aron. And then: "I rode next to him for four hours."

"I know."

"He talked about the *bell.*"

"I know," said Caleth.

Aron Kir said a word that Korroc had not previously known he had in him.

---

They went back down to Caleth's quarters, all of them, fast.

He was still there.

Korroc did not realize how hard he had been holding his own breath until he came through the door and saw him: a long thin shape under Caleth's own blankets with the pillow doubled over under his head and his hair spread on it, and Arueshalae sitting on a stool at the foot of the bed with her bow across her knees, who looked up and said, "Nothing. He has not stirred."

Korroc went to the bed and put two fingers on the elf's throat, above the ring of punctures, and felt the pulse, and stood there a second longer than he needed to.

Behind him Aron said, "I'm going to go and explain this to Irabeth. I'll put the citadel on alert and have everyone start looking."

"Aron," said Caleth.

"Mm."

"It wore his face well enough to fool me. It has been in the command room. It has heard every word we have said since the spring." Caleth did not turn round. "Tell them to be careful what they're looking for."

Aron went out.

And a little while after that, the citadel started to move.

Korroc heard it before he saw any of it, which was right, because that was how a place like this always told you. Boots in the corridor outside, more than usual and going faster than usual. A door somewhere below banging and not being apologized for. Then a horn, two notes, that was not the attack signal and not the muster and was the one they used for *stand to where you are*. Then more boots. Then Irabeth's voice, two floors down and through a stone wall, pitched at a volume that carried without being a shout, which was a thing she could do that Korroc had never been able to work out.

Sosiel came up within the half hour.

He came in quietly with his bag and nodded to Korroc and did not waste a word, and the two of them did what they had done in an infirmary two months ago, dividing the work between them without discussing it: Sosiel at the head and Korroc at the hands, Sosiel's long fingers going over the throat and the jaw and the eyelids while Korroc went down the arms and found the five closed incisions and showed him each one.

"What came out of these?"

"Nahyndrian crystals. One in each limb. One in the chest, deeper."

Sosiel's hands stopped for a moment. Then they went on.

"And you crushed them."

"All five."

"Good," said Sosiel. And then, because he was Sosiel: "Thank you."

He had him moved to the infirmary within the hour, on a board, with two of his own people carrying and Korroc walking beside it the whole way with one hand on the rail.

---

The infirmary was three-quarters empty, which in Drezen was a kind of miracle and did not feel like one. They put him at the far end, in the corner bed, where the lamp was.

Korroc stayed.

He took the gauntlets off and set them on the floor and sat down on the stool beside the bed, and after a while he took the breastplate off too because there was no sense in it, and set it against the wall with Torag's hammer and anvil facing out, and sat in his arming coat with his elbows on his knees.

Sosiel worked for two hours and then went away and came back and worked again. Somewhere around full dark he said that the fever was down, and that there was nothing more to be done tonight that would not be better done tomorrow, and that if Korroc would not go to bed then he at least ought to eat something, and Korroc said *aye*, and did not.

The citadel searched itself.

He heard all of it from the corner of the infirmary: the boots going up and down, the doors, a voice somewhere calling a room clear and another voice telling it to look again. Twice a pair of soldiers came through the infirmary itself and went along the beds and looked under them, which would have been funny on any other day. Once Irabeth went past the door at a pace and did not look in.

By the time the bell went for the middle watch it was clear that they were not going to find it.

Korroc had known that for a while. He had known it, he thought, since the study, since the cool cup and the pushed-back chair. A thing that could live in this fortress for two months without one of four knights and a paladin of Torag catching the edge of it was not a thing that was going to be found by men with lanterns looking under beds. It had put its cup down and got up and gone, and it had gone before they ever came up the stairs, and not one of them had the first idea what it was, or where it had gone, or what it had carried out with it in its head.

The whole citadel was awake and on edge and finding nothing at all.

He sat with that, and with the lamp, and with the man in the bed.

---

Some time after the middle watch, Aravashnial's breathing changed, and his head turned on the pillow, and he said something in Elven that Korroc did not catch and did not need to.

Korroc leaned forward.

"You're all right," he said. "You're in Drezen. You're in a bed. There's a dwarf sat here and he's not going anywhere."

The breathing eased. The elf did not wake.

Korroc sat back.

And then he did the thing he had been not doing for four hours.

It would have taken him about three seconds. That was the truth of it, and the truth of it was what made it so hard to sit still. The man was in front of him, breathing, starved and cut and full of holes where stones had been. He was exactly the sort of case Korroc laid the thread on without thinking twice — he had laid it on Irabeth in the middle of a fight on a tower top with a drake standing over her, he had laid three of them in one morning on the road west because it felt like the day for it, he had laid it on *this man*, in this room, in this corner, two months ago, and had not taken it off until the world took it off for him.

He put his hand out over the blanket.

And did not do it.

He sat there with his hand six inches above a sleeping man's chest and discovered that he could not.

He turned it over while he sat, honestly, the way he would have turned over a piece of work, because he had never in his life been any good at lying to himself and had never seen the use of starting.

*Is it the stones?* Some of it. There had been five of them in this man and he had held three of them in his bare hands, more or less, and whatever it was they had been doing was still being done when he cut the last one out. He did not know what was left behind in a man who had carried those for weeks. He did not know what would come down a thread out of that.

*Is it that you're tired?* He was tired. That was not it either.

It was this.

He had laid the thread on a thing in this room once. He had knelt on this floor with his bare hands on its chest and bound his own blood to it, and carried half of everything that happened to it for two months, and the whole time, the thing he had been pouring himself into had been something that got up out of a chair today and walked out of this fortress without a trace. And his sight had said there was nothing wrong. And he had believed his sight, because it was the first thing the Father ever gave him, and because in fifty-one years it had only failed him once, in a cellar, with a man in chains who was not a man.

Twice now.

And so here he sat, with his hand over a chest, looking down at a face he had known for a year, with every single reason in the world to believe it was the right one — Caleth's reading of a book he had needed a spell to open; a collar that had been built around this neck; five stones cut out with his own hands; *is that really you this time* — and he could not make himself put the thread down.

Not because he thought it was false. He did not think it was false. He would have stood up in front of Irabeth and said so.

Because he no longer trusted the part of him that did the thinking.

That was the flaw, and he could feel it under the hammer, and he knew exactly how a flaw like that runs if you keep working the piece without dealing with it. It does not stay where it is. It travels.

He took his hand back.

He put it on his own knee and sat with it there, and looked at the elf, and said, very quietly, into the lamplight, to nobody:

"I'm sorry. Give me a day."

Then he settled on the stool, and set his back against the cold wall, and stayed.

The fortress went on looking all night and found nothing.
