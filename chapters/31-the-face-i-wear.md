# The Face I Wear

*Chapter Thirty-One — The Midnight Fane*

---

The elf had found a bench in the west court, and the demon had found the other end of it.

Nageru saw them from the wall walk. He had gone up for the wind, which in Drezen came off the Worldwound with nothing to slow it and was honest in a way the courtyards were not. He stood at the parapet with his hands loose at his sides and looked down, and there they were: Aravashnial with his staff propped against his shoulder, the carved moons above his head, and his face turned toward her, and Arueshalae sitting very straight with her wings folded close, the way she sat whenever she was among people who might change their minds about her.

He could not hear them. He did not need to. He could see the elf's hands.

For two weeks those hands had lain still on blankets, or trembled on cups, or closed around a broken piece of carved wood and would not let it go. Now they moved. They drew a shape in the air and stopped and drew it again, better. One finger came up — *and another thing* — and Arueshalae laughed. Nageru saw her shoulders go, and saw her catch it, and look around the court to see who had noticed.

Nobody had. Nobody but him.

He let his breath go out, long and slow, and let the wind take it.

It was a thing worth watching. A man who had spent a season in the dark being taken apart by demons, sitting in the sun beside a demon, asking her questions. Nageru knew the questions without hearing them. He had asked some of them himself, in a hallowed tower a week west of the fane, with his back still bleeding. *What did she say. What did it feel like. Do you remember who you were.* Aravashnial would want it all. A demon who remembered her mortal life, whom Desna had reached into and woken, who had turned from everything she was. To a man who had spent his life learning how demons worked, she was the most interesting thing in the world.

And she let him ask. That was the part Nageru watched. She sat at the end of the bench, very straight, and she let him ask.

*There are no strangers. Only kin not yet recognized.*

Below him the elf drew another shape in the air. Arueshalae leaned in to see it.

Nageru stayed on the wall until the wind had cooled his face, and then he went down to find out what Caleth wanted.

---

Caleth wanted the four of them in Aravashnial's study, and Aravashnial out of it. That was its own kind of message.

"He's been through it," Caleth said. "Twice. He doesn't need it a third time." He had the coded journal open on the desk in front of him, the one from the locked chest in the Sanctum, cheap leather and a tie-cord. Beside it lay a stack of loose pages covered in his own neat hand, and beside that a second stack in a hand Nageru did not know, blunt and square, the letters cut rather than written.

Thane's. Thane sat on the corner of the desk with his arms folded and looked as though he had not slept.

"It took the three of us," Caleth said. "I tried alone for four days. I got nowhere. Thane had the Abyssal and I had the cipher, or most of it, and neither of us had the other half." He glanced at the empty chair behind the desk. "Aravashnial had the other half. He has seen codes like this before. Templar field codes. He saw the shape of it in about an hour, and was very rude about how long it had taken us."

"Sounds like him," said Korroc.

"It does." Something passed over Caleth's face and was gone. "Sit down. This will take a while, and you will not like any of it."

Nageru did not sit. He stood by the window where he could see all three of them, and listened.

It was Vang's journal. Not the man's thoughts; the man did not seem to have written his thoughts down anywhere. His work. Page after page of it, in Abyssal, in a cipher, about the refinement of Nahyndrian crystals. How they were cut. How they were dissolved. How the elixir was made from them, the elixir that gave a demon mythic power, the elixir Arueshalae had called *a way of dying* for a mortal man.

"And the chisel," Caleth said. "The one that cracked the wardstone at Kenabres. He had a hand in that too. There's a page on the tempering."

Korroc said something short in Dwarven. Nageru did not know the word. He knew the tone.

"There are plans in the back," Caleth went on. "Early ones. Not finished. For a golem." He turned a page and did not look at it. "Made of the crystals. Or made around them, it isn't clear which. The figures he gives for what it could do are—" He stopped. "They are very large figures."

"He's dead," said Thane. "Vang. He's not building anything."

"He was not working alone." Caleth turned another page. "This is the part. Listen."

They listened.

"The crystals are not stone," Caleth said. "They were never stone. They are blood. The lifeblood of demon lords. Demon lords who were killed — assassinated, the word he uses is the word for a murder done in secret — and whose blood has gone hard over however many ages, the way sap goes to amber." He looked up. "We have been carrying them. Korroc has been crushing them. They are the blood of murdered gods, and someone has found where it pooled."

Nobody spoke.

Nageru thought of the chest crystal, in the laboratory, the bigger one. Arueshalae's voice: *Some crystals still contain the souls of demon lords.* He thought of the thin coil of smoke that had risen from each one when Korroc's hammer came down, and thinned, and gone.

"Where," said Korroc.

"The Abyss. A realm there called the Midnight Isles. They are mining it." Caleth set his finger on a line. "And this is the strange part. The Isles have a ruler. A demon lord. Nocticula. And she has not given them permission."

Thane unfolded his arms. "Permission."

"Permission. Her realm, her blood in the ground, or at least her ground. The cult of Baphomet has been trying to bring her into the war on their side, again and again, and so far she has not agreed." His finger moved down. "The last entries are about a new attempt. Agents sent from the Templars in the Abyss to try again. Two names." He looked up. "Minagho. Who seems to be trying to get back into her master's favor after the wardstone was lost on her watch. And Hepzamirah. Baphomet's daughter. Who runs the Templars, and who runs the mine."

"Arueshalae said Hepzamirah was in the Abyss," said Nageru. "Harvesting."

"She was right." Caleth closed the journal. "And there's one more name. How the crystals come through. A rift, at a place called the Midnight Fane. They mine on the Isles and they ship through the rift and they pack them for the road on this side."

*The Fane.*

Nageru heard Thane breathe in. He heard Korroc's chair creak. Jerribeth's letter, unfinished on her desk in the Sanctum: *the next shipment from the Fane.* Capital letter. Nobody had known what it meant.

They knew now.

He found that his hands had closed, and he opened them.

It was only a word. A fane was a temple; any temple; it meant nothing more than that. There was a fane thirty miles west of here that had stood empty for longer than anyone remembered and had opened its doors to him, and old men had called him by a name he had not yet said aloud to anyone. That was a fane. This was another. A word did not belong to anyone.

But he had heard it in Caleth's mouth with *Midnight* in front of it and something in him had gone still in the wrong way, the way water goes still over a hole in the riverbed.

"Well," said Korroc, into the silence. "That's a lot of reading."

"Yes," said Caleth.

"You look terrible."

"Thank you, Korroc."

"I mean it kindly." Korroc stood, and put one broad hand flat on the closed journal, and left it there a moment as though he could feel something through the leather. "Murdered gods. Saints and stones." He took the hand away. "I'm going to go and get my axe back."

---

Irabeth was in her office when they found her, which was the only place she ever was, and she looked up from a stack of requisitions with the expression of a woman who has been waiting for a knock for weeks.

"Korroc."

"Commander."

"You're here for it."

"Aye."

"Thank Iomedae." She was already standing. She went to the rack behind her desk, where Fiendsplitter hung across two pegs above her own sword, and lifted it down with both hands and held it out to him across the desk, haft first, the way one hands back a borrowed thing that has been more trouble than it was worth.

Korroc took it.

Nageru watched his face. He could not hear the axe; nobody could hear the axe but whoever held it. But he had seen Korroc take it up before, and he knew what the voices in it did to the dwarf's face, the small hard set at the corner of the mouth as though somebody very close by had started talking and would not stop.

It came now. And then something else came after it, something Nageru had not expected. Korroc's shoulders went down half an inch.

Relief.

"It wants to know where she is," Irabeth said. "The succubus. Arueshalae. It has wanted to know every hour of every day for a month. It wants to know when I sleep. It wants to know when I eat." She sat back down. "It told me last week that it could smell her through two walls and a cellar, and that I was a coward, and that it had known better commanders in the Fourth Crusade."

"Did it name them?" said Korroc.

"Several."

"It does that." Korroc turned the axe in his hands. "I'm sorry for the trouble, Commander."

"It kept me honest." Irabeth picked up her pen. "Go away, Korroc. Take it with you."

Outside in the corridor Korroc stopped and looked at the axe. He held it a while. Nageru waited.

"Should've had it," Korroc said at last. "In the Sanctum."

"You did not want it near her."

"No. And I was right not to. It'd have gone for her the first hour." He ran his thumb along the flat of the blade, along Torag's mark cut in the steel. "But there was a hall full of things that were not her, and I went into it with a hammer." He was quiet. "There's a lot of demons left, Nageru. I'll not leave it on a wall again."

He slung it across his back, beside the shield, and Nageru saw his jaw tighten as the voices settled in.

"It says hello," Korroc said.

"Does it."

"No. It said something else." He started walking. "I'm not repeating it."

---

Thane came in from the town two days after that with something folded under his arm and the look of a man who has made an excellent bargain and intends to be asked about it.

Nobody asked. They were at the long table in the hall where the knights ate, and Korroc was eating, and Caleth was reading, and Nageru was sitting. Thane stood at the end of the table for some time.

Then he unfolded it.

It was a circle of black cloth. Silk, or something like silk; it did not catch the light at all. It was perhaps six feet across, and he spread it on the flagstones beside the table with the care of a man laying out a map, and stood back.

Korroc looked at it. "That's a rug."

"It's a hole," said Thane.

"It's a black rug."

"It's a hole." Thane picked up an empty tankard from the table and dropped it onto the middle of the cloth.

It did not land. It went *in*. It went down past the floor, down past where the floor was, and they heard it hit something a good way below — ten feet, perhaps — with a small, flat, faraway clank.

Korroc put down his bread.

"A trader in the lower town," Thane said, with tremendous satisfaction. "Came up the road with the Queen's last supply train. Wanted gems. I had gems. He wanted the two glaives off the Sanctum rack, and a pile of the other stuff Caleth didn't want, and some of the forge's off-cuts, and three days of my company, which he did not enjoy." He nudged the edge of the cloth with his boot. "Ten feet deep. Folds up to a handkerchief. You can carry a wagonload of anything in your belt."

"You can carry a *hole* in your belt," said Korroc.

"Yes."

"An open hole. In your belt. That goes down to—" Korroc waved the bread. "Where does it go down to?"

Thane opened his mouth, and paused, and looked at Caleth.

Caleth had put his book down. He was looking at the black circle on the floor with an expression Nageru had last seen on him in the Sanctum, outside a room lined with lead.

"Thane," he said. "Where is the bag?"

"What bag?"

"My bag. The bag." Caleth was very calm. "The bag of holding. With everything in it."

"In your room," said Thane. "Where you left it."

"Good." Caleth breathed out. "Never put that—" he pointed at the floor "—inside that. Or that inside that. Either way. Not ever. Not for a moment. Not to see what happens."

"What happens?"

"A hole opens into somewhere else, and takes everything nearby with it, including the person holding it, and nobody ever sees any of them again." Caleth picked up his book. "Including the bag. Which has Yaniel's— which has the Blackwing books in it."

There was a pause.

"Good to know," said Thane.

Korroc looked from the hole to Thane. "And you were going to find that out *how?*"

"I wasn't going to put the bag in the hole, cousin."

"You weren't going to *not* put the bag in the hole."

Nageru leaned forward. He looked down into the black circle. It was quite dark. Far down at the bottom of it, a tankard lay on its side.

"It is a hole," he said.

They all looked at him.

"I only wished to agree with Thane," Nageru said. "Someone should."

Korroc laughed so hard he had to put his head on the table.

---

The letter came the next morning, and Caleth read it to them standing, because there was a seal on it and the seal was the Queen's.

She was coming to Drezen. She did not say when, exactly; soon. She wished to speak with the four knights. That was all, three lines in a clean hand and her name at the foot, and Caleth folded it up again along its creases and put it in his coat and stood for a moment with his hand on it.

"Then we'll want it done before she comes," he said.

Nageru did not have to ask what *it* was.

It had been in Caleth since the night of the rescue. Nageru had watched it in him. Through the long days by Aravashnial's bed, through the debriefing, through the Council men and Irabeth's ruling and the conversation in the study that had made the half-elf someone's nephew in front of three witnesses. Under all of that there had been something else, something that came up in his face whenever the talk went near the empty study, the cooling cup, the chair pushed back. A thing had sat in that chair for two months with Aravashnial's face on it. It had sat in the command room with them. It had heard everything. And then it had stood up and walked out, and the citadel had searched itself all night and found nothing at all.

Caleth wanted it found.

"The real one told us what it looked like," he said. "Gray. Featureless, before it changes. Aravashnial says the word is *doppelganger*, and that they are very hard to find, because they are very good at not being found." He reached into his coat, the other side, and drew out something folded small. "But I have this."

A shirt. Linen, plain, the kind the elf wore under his dark coat. It had been on the back of the chair in the study, the morning the thing walked out. Folded neatly over the rail. Nobody had touched it.

"It wore this," Caleth said. "For days, perhaps. I don't know if that's enough. I'm going to try."

---

He did it in a small room off his own quarters, a storeroom with one high window and a table and nothing else, and he wanted the three of them there. He did not say why. Nageru thought perhaps he did not know why. Perhaps he only did not want to look into the water alone again.

The basin went on the table. The shirt went beside it. Caleth sat, and set his hands on either side of the basin, and began.

Nageru had watched him scry before. He had never watched it go on so long.

The first quarter hour was ordinary. Caleth's breathing slowed and lengthened, and his eyes went to the water and stayed there, and the water did nothing. The second quarter hour, a line of sweat came out along his hairline and ran down past his ear and he did not wipe it. The third, his hands began to tremble on the table, very slightly, the way a man's hands tremble holding a weight a little past what they were made for.

Korroc shifted his feet. Nageru heard the axe on his back whisper against the shield, and heard Korroc set his jaw against whatever it said.

The room was small. The four of them stood close, shoulder to shoulder almost, Thane at Caleth's left, Korroc behind, Nageru at the right where he could see the half-elf's face. It was gray now. Not pale. Gray, like ash in a cold grate, and his lips had gone thin.

"Caleth," said Korroc quietly.

"No," said Caleth, not looking up. "I have it. I almost have it. It's—"

An hour. It was very nearly an hour.

And then Caleth's eyes went wide.

Nageru leaned in and looked into the water, because he could not help it, and the water showed him a street.

A street in the lower town, outside the gate, in the morning light. Broken houses on either side, the ones nobody had yet cleared. Three figures walking down the middle of it, side by side. An elf with a staff. A woman with folded wings and a bow over her shoulder.

And between them, walking easily, a tall man in white wraps with bronze skin and short dark hair tied back, and prayer beads at his wrist.

Nageru looked at his own face in the water.

Caleth swore. It was not *saint's mercy.* It was a word Nageru had not known the half-elf knew.

And then he turned in his chair, and lifted his hands, and did not ask.

The world folded.

---

It was the small cold half-second of nowhere, and then stone under Nageru's bare feet, and wind, and the smell of the lower town — old ash, wet plaster, the latrines by the east wall — and morning light, and fifteen feet ahead of him, turning at the sound of four people arriving out of the air, three people in the road.

"*Take it alive,*" Caleth said.

Nageru saw himself.

He had seen his face in water, in mirrors, in the polished steel of Korroc's shield. He had never seen it *move.* He had never seen it turn toward him with the eyes coming up and the weight shifting onto the back foot — the back foot, the left one, the way he himself shifted when something came at him out of nowhere. The beads at its wrist swung with the turn, and they were right. He had worn those beads every day since Horgus had pressed them into his hand, and they were right, every one, the worn place on the third bead and the knot where the cord had broken once and been retied.

Its face showed surprise. *His* face showed surprise.

He felt it go across his own face at the same moment, and knew the two of them looked exactly alike, for one heartbeat, in the middle of a ruined street.

Then he moved.

He did not decide to. The decision was already made somewhere below deciding, in the place the Sunken Fist had spent twelve years building in him. He was across the fifteen feet before the thing had finished turning, and his right fist was already going, low and straight, at the place below the ribs where a man's breath lives.

It was not there.

It moved like nothing he had fought. Not fast, exactly; *wrong*, as though it had stepped out of the place before his fist had decided where to go. His knuckles passed through air. He felt the miss all the way up his arm, the small jarring nothing of a blow that finds no weight to meet it.

*Again.*

Left hand. He had already turned the miss into the next strike, his whole body coming round on the ball of his foot, and this time he did not aim where it was. He aimed where it was going.

It was there.

His fist came into its side, and he pulled it. He felt himself pull it — the turn of the wrist at the last instant, the force spilling out sideways instead of driving through — because Caleth had said *alive*, and because some cold, clear part of him had looked at that face and refused to break it. Even so the thing folded around the blow. Its breath went out in a grunt, and the grunt was his voice.

Third strike. Up, under the chin, open palm, and he pulled that one too, and still its head snapped back and its eyes went loose and unfocused.

Stunned. It stood swaying in the road with its hands half raised, and its legs did not quite know what to do.

Behind him Caleth's voice, a single hard syllable, and something went past Nageru's shoulder that he felt rather than saw, a pressure in the air. The thing stopped. Not fell; *stopped*, mid-sway, one hand still lifted, frozen in place as completely as if it had been cut from stone.

And Korroc went past Nageru on his left.

The hammer came round. Once. The sound of it striking was a dull wet sound, a sound Nageru had heard a thousand times and never heard like this. Twice. Korroc's feet planted, his weight going down through them, the hammer coming round flat and measured. Three times.

The thing fell.

It fell the way any man falls when the last of him gives out — knees first, then sideways, then still, face down in the dust of the road. Nageru's face. Nageru's wraps. Nageru's beads, lying across the back of its wrist.

Korroc stood over it with the hammer hanging from his hand.

Nageru looked at him. He could not help looking. The dwarf had struck exactly hard enough. Three blows, each one placed, each one stopping short of what Korroc could do with that hammer when he meant it. It was a beautiful piece of control. It was the work of a man who had decided before the first blow precisely how far he would let himself go.

But Korroc did not look at it. Korroc looked at nothing. He stood very still with the hammer and breathed through his nose, slow, and his eyes were somewhere over the thing's head, at the broken house behind it.

*And it's the second time.*

Nageru had heard the words in the gorge outside the Sanctum. He had not asked. He did not ask now. He watched his friend stand in a ruined street over the thing that had walked past his sight for two months, and he let him stand there.

Behind them, a sound.

Aravashnial was looking from one Nageru to the other. His staff had come up off the ground and across his body in both hands, the way a man holds a quarterstaff, and his eyes went from the Nageru standing in the road to the Nageru lying in it, and back, and back again. His mouth was a little open.

Arueshalae had an arrow on the string. Nageru did not know when she had drawn it; she had been fast, she was always fast. The bow was half bent, the point wavering between the man standing and the man lying down. And then, slowly, she lowered it. All the way. The arrow's point touched the dirt.

Her mouth opened. It worked. Nothing came out of it.

"It's all right," Nageru said to her. And then, because he could see that it was not, and that she was looking at his face as though she were not sure it was his: "It is me. The one standing."

She closed her mouth.

Caleth had already turned. There was a soldier at the end of the street, a young man in Drezen colors with a spear, frozen in the act of walking past, gaping. "You," Caleth said, and the young man straightened as though he had been struck. "Go and get the Commander and the command council. All of them. We need them here, now."

The soldier ran.

Nageru looked down.

There was nothing for it. Korroc was still standing with the hammer. Thane had come round to the thing's other side and was crouched by its head, looking at its face very closely, without touching it. Caleth was gray to the lips and leaning, Nageru now saw, one hand on the broken wall beside him.

Nageru bent, and took hold of himself under the arms, and lifted.

It was heavier than he expected. He did not know what he had expected. He got it across his shoulders the way he would have carried any wounded man, one arm over his neck and his hand locked around its wrist, and its head lolled against his shoulder, and he felt its breath on his ear, and the breath smelled like nothing at all.

He started walking.

Behind him he heard Aravashnial say, quite faintly, "Which—" and stop.

"The one carrying," said Thane.

---

The lower town had been half empty when they arrived. It was not half empty by the time they reached the gate.

People came out of doorways. They came out of side streets and from behind carts and down off scaffolding where they had been working on the walls. They saw the four knights coming up the road, the dwarf with the hammer, the dwarf with the knives, the half-elf in his white robes walking a little unsteadily, and they stepped aside, as people in Drezen had learned to step aside for those four. And then they saw what the tall one was carrying, and they stopped.

Nageru felt the stares settle on him. On both of him.

He kept walking. He looked straight ahead at the gate. There was a woman with a basket of laundry on her hip who had stopped dead in the middle of the road, and he went around her, and as he passed he heard her say, to nobody, in a small voice, "There's two of them."

"There's one of him," said Thane, behind him. "The other one's luggage."

The gate was open. Irabeth was standing in it, with Aron at her shoulder, and both of them were breathing as though they had come down the citadel road at a run. Nageru saw Irabeth's eyes go to him, and to the face lying against his shoulder, and go wide — and then, almost at once, he saw her understand. He watched it happen. She was a good commander. It took her about as long as one breath.

She did not say anything. She looked at Caleth, and Caleth nodded once, and she turned on her heel and waved them through, and up, and into the command room.

Nageru put the thing down on the floor.

He did not set it down gently. He did not drop it either. He let it go off his shoulder onto the flagstones in front of the long table, and it lay there on its back with his face turned up to the ceiling, and everyone in the room looked at it.

Irabeth looked at it for some time.

"Well," she said. "That takes care of one problem, I suppose." She looked up at the four of them. "Take it to a cell. Put it behind bars. I don't want it in this room one minute longer than it has to be."

Nageru bent and picked it up again.

He settled it on his shoulder. He turned toward the door. Then he stopped, and turned back, because it was a real question.

"Magic cell," he said, "or not magic cell?"

Thane looked at him. "The one that blocks all magic?"

"Not magic," said Caleth at once.

Korroc's head came round. "You're sure?"

Caleth said nothing. Nageru saw his face. He was looking at the floor, and there was a small line between his eyebrows, and Nageru knew what he was seeing because he was seeing it too: a collar, black, with a green stone, and needles, and a man on a slab who could not reach his own magic and could not be reached by anyone else's. Caleth did not like the anti-magic cell. Caleth did not like anything that took a wizard's hands away, and had not liked it for some weeks now.

"We don't know much about it," Korroc said. "That's all I'm saying. Can it teleport? Can it change its shape to fit between the bars? Turn into a rat and walk out?"

Caleth's frown deepened.

"We don't know," said Thane.

Silence.

"Magic cell," said Nageru.

Nobody argued.

---

The anti-magic cell was at the bottom of the citadel, cut down into the rock. Aron's engineers had found it in the first month and put a new door on it, and a new lock, and new bars, and it was the strongest room in Drezen.

He carried the thing in through the door.

He felt the field take hold as he crossed the threshold. It was not a feeling, exactly. It was an absence of one, a quiet in a place he had not known was making noise — the faint warmth of his own blood, the hum under his skin that had been there since before he could remember, gone, as though someone had laid a hand over a bell.

He bent and set the thing down on the stone floor.

And as it touched the floor it stopped being him.

He watched it happen. It did not take long. The bronze went out of the skin first, from the hands up, like color leaving a dyed cloth in water, and what was underneath was gray. The face went next. His face — the nose and the brow and the set of the mouth — slackened and smeared and ran, and the features under it were not features. A suggestion of a nose. Two lidded eyes too far apart. A slit for a mouth. The hair drew back into the skull. The wraps stayed his wraps, but now they hung on something thinner and longer, with too many joints in the fingers, and the prayer beads on its wrist were only a loop of brown cord with a few dull lumps on it.

Nageru straightened up and stepped back out of the cell.

It was ugly. It was the ugliest thing he had ever seen, he thought, and he had seen a great many ugly things in the last year. Not because of what it was. Because of what it had been a moment before.

Caleth was standing well back from the bars. Well back from the edge of the field. "Hm," he said. "The anti-magic stops the changing." He sounded, despite everything, interested.

Thane turned the key. Korroc set two guards on the door, and then a third, and then thought about it and set a fourth.

They went up into the daylight, and none of them spoke.

---

It woke an hour later. A runner brought the word, and they went down again, all four, and the guards stood back from the bars, and it was sitting up on the stone with its back against the far wall and its long gray hands on its knees, looking at them.

It looked at Nageru longest. He could not tell what it thought. It had no face to think with.

"Who do you work for?" said Caleth.

It shrugged. The shrug was very human. "Highest bidder?"

"Who is the bidder?"

"There's still a bounty on the demon," it said.

Nageru felt it before he understood it. Something in his chest went tight and then went very quiet, and he heard himself say, "Arueshalae."

The gray head turned toward him. "That's the one."

Thane leaned on the bars. "So. Vang? Or Jerribeth?"

"Both?" Another shrug. "Sort of. Depends how you count." Its voice was light. It had a pleasant voice, Nageru thought, and wondered whose it was. "Guess that's one job I won't be getting paid for, huh? Shame. It's still on, you know. The bounty. It's pretty high. I'd have got that money."

"Who was going to pay you?" said Caleth.

"Jerribeth put up the original. A thousand platinum."

Caleth made a sound of contempt. "That doesn't seem worth it. And she's gone."

"Her employers would pay it." The slit of a mouth moved; it might have been a smile. "I'd bet on it. They're the paying kind."

"What was Vang's plan for Aravashnial?"

Shrug. "Don't know. Wasn't my end. My job was to stop you looking for him." The lidded eyes went from one of them to the next. "It worked."

Nobody answered that.

Nageru watched Korroc. The dwarf stood a little behind the others, with his arms folded across his breastplate and the axe's haft over his shoulder, and he had not moved since they came in. He did not move now.

"How did you get past our tests?" said Caleth. His voice had gone very even. "Detect evil. Detect magic. I dispelled you. I dispelled you myself."

"I'm good at what I do." The thing spread its long hands. "And I'm not a demon. I'm a mercenary. There's a difference. You'd be amazed how many people forget it."

"Our lie detection," said Caleth. "Thane cast it on you. To your face. You lied for an hour and it never caught a word."

And for the first time, the thing smiled. There was no mistaking it this time. The slit opened and there were small gray teeth behind it.

"Like I say," it said. "I'm good."

Caleth spat.

Nageru had never seen him do it before. The half-elf leaned and spat on the stone floor of the corridor, just short of the bars, and straightened, and his face was gray and closed and cold.

"You can rot in there," he said, and turned, and walked away up the passage.

Thane went after him. Korroc stood a moment longer, looking at the thing on the floor of the cell. Then he went too.

Nageru stayed.

The thing looked at him. He looked at it.

He thought: *it wore my face to go to her.*

He thought of the bench in the west court. The elf with his staff and his questions. The woman at the other end of the bench, very straight, letting herself be asked. She had sat that way since the tower. She had sat that way in the command room on the night Caleth put her under his protection, and on every day since, and the only times Nageru had seen her sit any other way were the times he was sitting beside her.

She had told him why, in the tower, when the others were out of earshot. Why it had been him she called, in the dream, and not one of the others. She had not been able to trust the dwarves. She had known Staunton, and what a dwarf of Drezen had been willing to sell. So she had asked for someone who might listen first, and something had answered, and the someone had been him.

And this thing had found that out. Or guessed it. Or simply looked about the citadel for the face that the demon walked beside without her wings drawn in, and put it on.

It had walked her out of the gate wearing his face, and she had gone.

"Do you want to know what it is like?" the thing said. "Being you?"

Nageru turned to the guards.

"Watch him," he said. "Always. Two of you awake at every hour." He looked at the four of them, one by one, until each of them had met his eyes. "And do not talk to him. Not one word. Whatever he says. Whatever face he shows you, if he finds a way to show you one." He paused. "Especially then."

"Yes, sir," said the eldest.

He went up the passage and did not look back.

---

Aravashnial was waiting at the head of the stair.

He was leaning on his staff with both hands, and Arueshalae was beside him, a step back, her bow unstrung now and slung across her body. When the four of them came up into the light the elf's eyes went past them, down the dark of the stair, and stayed there a moment as though he expected something else to come up behind.

"I really thought," he said, "that was Nageru."

Nobody laughed.

"What did it want?" said Caleth. "With you. What did it say?"

"It came to the study. It said—" Aravashnial frowned. "It said you needed to speak with me. About something you had found in one of the abandoned buildings in the lower town. It said you had asked for me particularly, and for Arueshalae, because it was a thing about demons. It said it would take us." He looked down at his own hands on the staff. "I believed it entirely. I walked out of the gate beside it and I talked to it about the Sanctum the whole way down the road."

"It was going to collect the bounty," said Caleth. "On her."

Aravashnial nodded slowly. He did not look surprised. He looked like a man adding a figure to a column he already knew the sum of.

"Can you be sure," he said, "that it won't get out?"

"Anti-magic," said Thane. "The best lock in the citadel. And the strongest cage."

"Yes." The elf was quiet a moment.

He looked down the stair once more. Then he turned, and went with them.

Arueshalae fell in beside Nageru. She did not speak for the length of the courtyard. Then, very low, without looking at him:

"It walked like you."

"Yes."

"It *stood* like you. It stopped at the gate and looked at the sky, the way you do. Before it went down the road." Her hands had closed on the strap of her bow. "I did not even wonder. Not for a moment. It said *come* and I came."

Nageru walked.

There was nothing to say to it that was true and helpful both. He had told Korroc once that he was not good at the second kind. He did not try now. He walked beside her across the court, at the pace she was walking, and after a while her hands let go of the strap.

---

Two days later a horn sounded at the gate.

Not an alarm. The long falling note that meant *arrival*, and then two short ones that meant *of rank*, and the whole citadel heard it and stopped what it was doing and went to look.

The Queen of Mendev came into Drezen without ceremony.

There was no herald. There were no banners but the one. She rode in at the head of a column so small that Nageru counted it from the wall walk before it had finished passing under the gate: two men in officers' coats, two in the plain dark robes of wizards, and a few dozen paladins behind them in Iomedae's colors, riding two by two. That was all. That was all of it.

"Saints and stones," muttered Korroc beside him. "She's riding round the Wound with *that?*"

"We hold the whole road," said Thane. "Kenabres to here. Every fort on it."

"There's an army of demons out there would cross the whole Wound on their knees for the chance to see her dead." Korroc shook his head. "A few dozen. Torag's beard."

Nageru watched her ride in. She sat her horse the way she had sat it on the road north a year ago, at the head of the army, coming up against the gray edge of the world. Shoulders square. Nothing wasted.

"You must remember, brother," he said, "she is a crusader queen." He watched her dismount, and hand her reins to nobody, and stride across the court toward the citadel doors with her people hurrying after her. "I think if they came for her she would give them a fight they did not want."

Korroc grunted. It was, Nageru thought, an agreeing sort of grunt.

---

She saw them within the hour, in the command room, with the map table cleared and Irabeth at her right hand and the rest of Drezen's command along the walls. Her two officers stood behind her. One was a lean, weathered man with a soldier's close-cropped gray hair whom Irabeth named as General Robard Kessin. The other was introduced only as the Queen's Riftwarden advisor, and gave no name, and Nageru saw the gray-haired Council man along the wall look at him once and then very carefully not look at him again.

The two wizards stood together at the back, side by side, in identical dark robes, with identical neat beards and identical expressions of patient attention. Nageru heard Korroc, at his elbow, breathe "Which one's which?" and Thane breathe back "Doesn't matter."

The Queen did not sit.

"Word has come back to me," she said, "that you have cleared the Ivory Sanctum." She looked at each of them in turn. Nageru remembered the look; it had not changed. A quartermaster weighing goods. "Word has also come back to me that you found a demon there, and brought her here. Into my citadel."

"Yes," said Caleth. "On both counts, Your Majesty."

"Where is this demon?"

Caleth turned his head. "Guard. Find Arueshalae. Tell her I've asked for her."

The guard went.

The Queen watched him go. Then she turned back, and her voice did not change at all.

"She is not with the horde. Why? What makes you trust a demon? How do you know she can be trusted?"

It was a fair question. It was the question Nageru would have asked, in her place. Caleth opened his mouth to answer it and Nageru felt himself step forward before the half-elf could, and he did not know why, except that it was his to answer.

"She has been touched by Desna, Your Majesty."

The Queen's eyes came to him.

He did not explain. He did not tell her about the priestess, or the dream with no dreamer, or *even demons can dream.* He had thought about it on the wall, watching the column come in, and had understood that if he began to explain he would be asking her to believe it, and she would not be asked. She would decide. So he said what was true, and stopped, and let her look at him.

She looked at him for a long moment.

"Then," she said at last, "there may be a use for her." She reached inside her surcoat. "Because I have got my hands on something rare and dangerous, and I need someone who can do a thing with it."

"What thing?" said Caleth.

She laid them on the table.

Two plates of copper. Thin, the size of a large book's pages, the edges hand-cut and a little uneven. They lay side by side on the bare wood, and the light from the high windows lay across them, and Nageru saw that they were covered on both faces with writing — tiny, close, and in several hands.

"Some of you will have heard," the Queen said, "of the Lexicon of Paradox."

Aravashnial, along the wall, made a very small sound.

"It is a book of forbidden rites," the Queen said. "The book that opened the Worldwound, a hundred years ago and more, and swallowed Sarkoris whole. Areelu Vorlesh used it. And afterward, when the Wound was open and Sarkoris was dying, she tore it in half and destroyed the half that told how she had done it. So that no one could ever walk her road backward." She touched the edge of one plate with a gauntleted finger. "For a century that was the end of it. Then a small company of mercenaries went looking for it. A venture-captain of the Pathfinder Society named Varian Jeggare, and his man Radovan, and a half-elven paladin of Iomedae called Oparal. I will not tell you everything they went through. They found it. They found all of it. The book is whole again, and it is in crusader hands, and the Scholars of Ozem are studying it under guard." Her mouth thinned. "They have not yet understood it. They are not close."

Nageru looked at the copper plates.

"So you want us to use the book," he said slowly, "that caused all of this. To begin with."

"Yes."

Caleth was looking at the plates too. "Those are not a book."

"No. Those are a test." The Queen set her palm flat beside them. "There is a ritual on them. A ritual to close a rift between this world and the Abyss."

Korroc frowned at the plates. "There's a fair few of those already, aren't there? Spells, rites. Aravashnial closed one at the Ahari while we fought off vrocks. What makes this one different?"

"Copper plates," Nageru said, very quietly, to nobody.

"Most of those are for small things," said the Queen. "Small rifts. Small portals. Holes that were never meant to be there and want to close. This one is meant to work at a greater scale. On a rift that was made to stay open." She stepped back from the table. "Look at them, if you like."

Caleth stepped forward.

Nageru watched him bend over the plates. He watched the half-elf's eyes go along the first line, and the second, and on down, unhurried, the way they went along the spine of any book he had not yet read. His lips moved once. The small line between his brows that meant he was working came, and went, and did not come back.

"It is in several languages," Caleth said. "Six. Seven. They change from line to line. Sometimes in the middle of a line."

"Yes," said the Queen. "The scholars have three of them between them. They have been very unhappy about the rest."

"I know all of them," Caleth said.

He said it the way Korroc might have said he knew which end of a hammer to hold. Nageru was not surprised. In a year he had heard the half-elf talk to dwarves in Dwarven and to demons in their own tongue, and read books in a half-dozen more; he collected languages the way Thane collected knives, and with about as much fuss.

He straightened up. "Whoever wrote this," he said, "wanted it read by someone who had studied a great deal. Or by several people at once." He tapped the edge of the nearest plate. "I can read it."

The Queen inclined her head, as if that was what she had been told she would hear.

"The ritual is not complete," the Queen said into the silence. "What is on these plates is part of it. But the plates themselves are infused. The magic is in the metal. With a great enough source of power behind it, it will close a rift."

"So," said Nageru, "a crystal."

"No," said the Queen. "You."

They all four looked at her.

Caleth recovered first. "We are the power."

"You are." She looked at each of them, and Nageru understood that she had known this before she ever left the south, and had ridden all the way north to watch their faces when she said it. "What happened to you at Kenabres. What the wardstone did to you, and has gone on doing. That is power, of a kind the scholars have no other source of. If you can use these plates to close one rift — one — we can take what you learn back to the Scholars of Ozem, and they may have the key to the rest of the Lexicon."

"So what rift do we close?" said Caleth.

Thane spoke up from the end of the table. "Wait." He looked at Caleth. "That journal. Didn't it talk about a rift? At the Midnight Fane?"

"I have seen that rift."

They turned.

Arueshalae had come in. Nobody had seen her come; she was standing just inside the door, behind the last of the officers, as though she had been there for some time and had been waiting for the right moment to stop being a shadow. Now she stepped forward. The officers moved aside from her without seeming to decide to. She came to the end of the table and stood there with her wings folded and her hands still.

"They use it to bring the crystals through," she said. "From the Abyss to here. It is the only way they come."

The Queen looked at her.

It was not long. Nageru would remember afterward that it was not long. But he saw it, and he thought perhaps no one else did: the Queen of Mendev, who had fought demons for longer than Nageru had been alive, looked at a succubus standing at her map table, and for one moment forgot to be a queen. She only looked. As though she were seeing a thing she had been told did not exist.

Then she was a queen again.

"A map," she said.

Papers were pushed aside. A map came out of the rack and unrolled across the table and was weighted at the corners with whatever was nearest, a candlestick, Thane's knife, the Queen's own gauntlet. Arueshalae leaned over it. Her finger came down, and moved, and moved, and kept moving — west, across the whole stained breadth of the Worldwound, across places that had no names on the map at all, only the mapmaker's careful nothing — and stopped, very near the far edge.

Aron leaned in and looked at the place and was quiet a while, measuring with his eye.

"Two hundred and forty miles," he said softly. "At the least."

"We will need a plan," said Nageru, "for that."

"Yes." The Queen straightened. "And before we make one, I want to understand it. All of it. What is this Midnight Fane? What is this about the crystals being moved?" She pulled out the chair at the head of the table at last, and sat in it. "Start at the beginning. Leave nothing out."

They did not leave anything out.

It took two hours. Nageru had not known there was two hours of it until it was told. The bell in the hallowed tower and the hag. The Ivory Sanctum and the laboratory and the man on the slab. Vang, and how he burned. The doppelganger in the cell below the cistern, and the thousand platinum, and the face it had worn two days ago. The journal. The murdered gods in the crystals. The Isles. A demon lord called Nocticula who had not yet said yes.

At the end of it the Queen sat back in her chair and did not speak for some time.

"That," she said, "is a great deal to consider." She stood. "Get some rest. All of you. We will need you ready for the test."

---

They were summoned again late the next day.

The Queen looked as though she had not slept. Her face had the gray, scraped look of a woman who had spent the night at a table with lamps and papers and the two men in officers' coats, and Nageru saw the general's eyes were red-rimmed too, and the Riftwarden's.

"This is bigger than I first thought," she said.

She did not sit.

"Closing that rift is not enough. If we close it they will find another way, or make one. We need to destroy that factory. All of it. The mine, the packing, the road. We need to end the delivery of those crystals." Her frown deepened. "And Nocticula. If the Templars are pressing her for an alliance, if she is being courted to join the war on their side — we need to break that. Or—" She stopped. "Or make our own."

Nageru felt it go through the four of them. Korroc's hand went to the haft over his shoulder. Thane went perfectly still. Caleth's lips parted.

"Did I just hear you say," said Nageru, "an alliance? With Nocticula?"

"You did." The Queen met his eyes. "The enemy of our enemy is sometimes useful. Perhaps not an alliance. Perhaps only an agreement. Something that keeps her out of the war against us."

Nageru stopped.

He did not answer at once. He let the question sit in him, and turned it over, and looked at the underside of it the way Master Borin had taught him to look at the underside of any blow before he decided to meet it.

A demon lord. Not a demon; a lord of them. A thing with its own realm in the Abyss and the blood of murdered gods in its ground.

And yet. A woman had stood at that table an hour ago and pointed across the Worldwound for them, a demon, whom Desna had woken, who remembered. He had defended her to Thane in the gorge. *She is no longer an evil demon. Do not assume.* He had meant it. He meant it still. If one could turn—

No. That was the wrong road, and he saw that it was wrong even as he set his foot on it. Arueshalae had not been bargained with. She had been *reached*. A god had put a hand into the deepest place in her and woken what was asleep there, and she had wanted it, under everything; that was the whole of her story. That was not a thing you could do at a table. You could not hope a demon lord into a good one.

But you could bind one.

"We will need to be very careful," he said slowly. "An alliance with such a creature is bound to be an undoing. Sooner or later. It is in her nature." He looked at the copper plates, still lying on the table where she had left them. "But if we could bind her to a promise — a true one, a sworn one — it might hold. Even so." He looked up at the Queen. "It would have to be a promise that served her. We cannot expect a demon to keep faith with us. We can only expect her to keep faith with herself. So it must be something that is in her own interest to keep."

The Queen inclined her head, very slightly. Nageru thought it was the inclination of someone who had arrived at the same place by another road, some hours before dawn.

"It sounds," she said, "as though you might be making a trip to the Abyss yourselves."

"*What?*"

It was Caleth. It came out of him too sharp, too fast, and Nageru saw him hear it, and close his mouth on whatever was behind it.

The Queen did not answer. She turned her head and nodded to the Riftwarden.

He stepped forward. He was a narrow man with ink on his fingers and the careful voice of someone who has explained difficult things to people who did not want to hear them.

"For the ritual to take," he said, "the rift has to be closed from both sides at once. Someone must be on the far side of it. In the Abyss. Holding a copper plate."

Silence.

"And how," said Thane, "does that someone get back?"

The Riftwarden nodded, as though Thane had asked exactly the right question, which he had.

"That is the difficulty," he said. "The rift will be closed behind them. Whoever is on the far side would need their own way home."

Nobody spoke.

Nageru heard the room. He heard the lamps, and somebody's breath along the wall, and his own heart, slow and even, which surprised him. He looked at the copper plates. He thought of the Abyss. He had fought its creatures for a year; he had never thought of it as a place a man could walk. A place with ground. With a sky, perhaps. With a road home that you had to find yourself.

"But," said the Queen, "since Nocticula is on that side of the rift — it gives you the chance to find her. And finish both tasks at once."

Caleth's mouth opened. It closed again. He sat down, slowly, in the nearest chair, and put his elbows on the table and his fingers together in front of his face, and Nageru could see the futures running behind his eyes, one after another, too many to count.

The Queen reached into a pouch at her belt.

"If you take this on," she said, "you should be ready for a long journey. A very long one. I know what I am sending you into. One of the most dangerous places in the Abyss." She laid four small things on the table among the papers: four discs of plain silver on chains, unmarked, the size of a large coin. "These are Talismans of True Faith. They will help you there. They will help you not to do anything there that would cost you your faith." She looked at them. "That is not a small thing, in that place."

Korroc picked his up first. He looked at it, and turned it over, and dropped the chain over his head — and the disc moved on his chest. Nageru saw it. The plain silver *ran*, the way metal runs in a crucible, and came still again, and it was a hammer and an anvil. Torag's mark. Korroc looked down at it and touched it with one thick finger and said nothing at all.

Caleth put his on, and before anyone could see what it became, he had tucked it down inside the white robes, out of sight, and buttoned the collar over it.

Nageru put his on. He felt the silver move against his breastbone, cool, and then warm, and he looked down. Irori's mark. He had seen it every day of his life, cut over doorways and stitched on sleeves and drawn in the dust of the practice yard by a boy who did not yet know what it meant. The mark of the Master of Masters, lying on his chest in Mendevian silver.

He stood very still for a moment with his hand over it.

Thane put his on last. Nageru watched it run, and settle. A mask with two faces, one looking each way. Alseta. The goddess of doors.

Thane looked at it, and his mouth tightened at one corner, and he did not say whatever he thought of a door-goddess's mark on a man being sent through a door that would close behind him.

"I will be coming with you," said the Queen, "as far as the Fane."

Caleth looked up. "Coming with us?"

"Someone must be on this side of the rift to complete the ritual. I will be there."

"Your Majesty," said Caleth, "we cannot guarantee your safety."

"I don't expect you to guarantee my safety." She did not smile. "I will guarantee my own." And she turned, without any change of tone at all, to the end of the table. "You say you have been to the Midnight Fane."

"Several times," said Arueshalae.

---

They argued the road for an hour.

Teleportation was the obvious answer and the first to go. Nobody in the room had seen the Fane; you could not fold to a place you had never seen. Caleth asked Arueshalae if there was anyone there he might scry, someone whose face she knew, and she shook her head — people came and went there all the time, demons and Templars and slaves, and it had been too long since she had been inside. They talked about folding as far west as anyone had been and riding the rest, and Aron put his finger on the map at the last fort anyone had held and drew it across the empty space toward Arueshalae's mark, and nobody liked how long his finger had to travel.

Then Arueshalae said, quietly, "I could take one."

They looked at her.

"I could fold, once. Before." She looked at her hands. "I lost it. When I— I lost it. But if you had a scroll with the working on it, I think I might be able to read it. And take one person with me. If I can make it work."

"I can have a scroll made by tomorrow," said Caleth.

"I'm sure there are several wizards in this citadel who could make you that scroll," said the Queen. "That isn't the difficulty. The difficulty is using it well." She glanced at the back of the room. "If she can take one person, take a wizard who can fold the rest. Take mine. Then you won't have to carry every soul in the company across two hundred miles yourself, Caleth Aranor."

She turned her head and lifted one finger, and one of the two identical wizards stepped forward with a rolled scroll already in his hand, which he had evidently been holding for some time.

The Queen took it from him and held it out to Arueshalae across the table.

"There," she said. "We didn't have to make one at all." She nodded to the wizard. "This is Merrec Tolland. He will go with you."

"And the other one?" Korroc murmured.

"His brother," the Queen said, without looking round. "Davor. He will go with me."

Korroc looked at the two of them for a long moment. "Right," he said.

---

Arueshalae studied the scroll for half an hour, sitting on the floor of the command room with her back to the wall and her lips moving. Then she stood up and took a breath, a long one, of the kind Nageru had been taught to take before a blow he knew was going to hurt. Caleth stood on one side of her and Merrec Tolland on the other, and she read it.

The air folded in on them like a closing hand, and the three of them were gone.

Nageru waited.

It was not long, though it felt long. An hour, perhaps. He spent it at the window, watching the light change on the court, not thinking about how a botched fold might go. Then the air in the middle of the room went tight and folded back out again, and there they were, Caleth and the wizard on their feet and Arueshalae on one knee with her hand on the floor, breathing hard.

"Fifty miles," Caleth said. "Out. We were fifty miles out. She chose it."

"On purpose," said Arueshalae from the floor. "So we would not be seen. And so if somebody made a mistake, the whole company wouldn't land inside the Fane."

"It stands on a rise," Caleth said. "You can see it from that distance. Even from fifty miles. We studied it for an hour." He looked at Arueshalae. "She has never folded inside it. Not once, in all the times she's been there."

"Why not?" said Thane.

"The rift." Arueshalae got to her feet. "It's big. It's very strong. I've seen people fold too close to it and it went—" She made a small motion with her hand. "Wrong."

"Wrong how?" said Korroc.

"They exploded."

Caleth closed his eyes. "She told me that on the hill," he said. "In exactly those words. I'd like it noted that I said *eww*."

"It's noted," said Thane.

---

The folding began an hour after that, and went on until dark.

Caleth did most of it. Merrec Tolland took one trip and his brother took another, and they were good — Nageru watched the first one fold and could not fault it — but that still left the half-elf doing it eight times. Out, with a full load. Back, empty. Out again. Each time he came back through the air in the middle of the court he was a little grayer, and stood a little longer before he lifted his hands again.

On the third trip Caleth turned to Arueshalae and asked her to come.

Nageru saw her face. He saw the fear go across it. It was very small, and very quick, and she put it away almost before it had arrived; she had been a spy for a very long time and she was good at putting things away. But he saw it. She looked west, where the Fane was, where the rift was, and on the far side of the rift the place she came from.

Then she nodded.

"I will go," she said. "To this side. As far as this world goes." She did not say *not through*. She did not have to.

"That's all anyone's asking," said Caleth.

The four knights, Arueshalae, Aravashnial, who would not be left behind and whom nobody had had the heart to try. The Queen and her general and her Riftwarden and her two wizards, and every one of her paladins. Long after dark, the last of them stood on a cold hillside fifty miles east of a rise in the dark with a light on it, and Caleth sat down on a rock and put his head in his hands.

"We rest for the night," the Queen said. "No fires."

Caleth nodded without lifting his head.

They made a cold camp. Nageru sat with his back against a boulder and his hands open on his knees, and listened to the Worldwound's wind come over the hill, and to the paladins settling, and to a sound he did not at first understand — a low, steady muttering from where Korroc lay rolled in his blanket, as though the dwarf were arguing with someone very quietly in his sleep.

He was not asleep. Nageru saw his eyes, open in the dark.

"It knows," Korroc said, when Nageru came and crouched beside him. "Her. She's thirty feet off. It's been on about her since we landed." He rubbed his face with both hands. "It wants me to *go and look*. It's offering to tell me where. It's describing her. It won't stop." His voice dropped. "If it starts out loud, Nageru — if it starts talking out *loud*, in a camp full of the Queen's paladins—"

"Thane," said Nageru.

Thane, a dark shape a little way off, sat up. Nageru heard him understand. He heard Thane get up and come over, and heard the rustle of cloth from his belt, and saw the black circle unfold on the ground between them, a round of deeper darkness in the dark.

Korroc looked at it. Then he unslung the axe, and held it a moment over the hole, and dropped it in.

There was a faint clank, far down.

Then nothing at all.

Korroc lay back on his blanket and let out a breath so long Nageru thought it would never end.

"Saints," he said. "Quiet."

"You're welcome," said Thane, folding up the hole.

"I'll get it out in the morning." Korroc closed his eyes. "Maybe."

---

The Queen of Mendev did not come out of her tent in the morning.

A woman came out of it instead, in plain soldier's mail with no device on it, a dented helm under one arm and a plain cloak over everything, and walked to where the horses would be summoned and stood waiting there with her arms folded. It took Nageru a full breath to know her. She looked like what she was supposed to look like: a sellsword, or a sergeant, a woman who had been at war a long time and expected to be at war a long time yet.

Her paladins had changed too. Not much. They were still the Queen's men; you could see it in how they stood, and nothing could be done about that. But the sunburst was gone from their tabards, and in its place were other marks, painted carefully: the signs of the Templars of the Ivory Labyrinth, taken stroke for stroke from Jerribeth's six pages. Anevia's whole year of work, on two dozen chests.

The five who were going in wore them too. Thane had painted Nageru's himself, on a strip of cloth bound around his upper arm, and had stood back and looked at it and said, "You still look like you," and Nageru had said, "I will try to look like someone else," and Thane had said, "Don't. You're bad at it."

Caleth summoned the horses.

They rode west, Arueshalae in front.

It was not long before people began to wonder where she was taking them. Nageru saw the general look at the rise, and then at Arueshalae's back, and then at the rise again; she was not riding toward it. She was riding past it, well to the south, along a dry wash that bent away from the light they had all seen from the hill.

"Illusions," she said, when the general finally rode up beside her and asked. "The whole hill. What you can see from here is not where it is. Trust me." She did not turn round. "You will see it soon."

And they did.

It came on slowly. Nageru saw it first as a wrongness in the shape of the land, a ridge whose shadow fell the wrong way for the hour, and then the rise ahead of them seemed to waver — not much, the way air wavers over a road in summer — and then he was looking at two hills at once. The one he had been seeing, with the light on top. And another, a mile to the south and west of it, lower and darker, with something squat and black on its crown.

The first hill went thin, and thinner, and was gone.

"Huh," said Korroc. He did not sound pleased.

A mile out from the real hill, in a fold of ground where nothing on the crown could see them, the Queen raised her hand and the column stopped.

"We'll make our camp here," she said. "And wait. The five of you go on."

They left the horses with the paladins and went on foot.

---

The building on the hill was black stone, low and heavy, with no windows that Nageru could see. Arueshalae did not lead them up to it. She led them along the base of the hill, through rocks and dry scrub, until she stopped in front of a slope of earth that looked exactly like every other slope of earth on the hillside, and pointed.

Nageru looked. It took him a long moment. Then he saw the line in the turf, too straight to be a crack. And another, meeting it. Two doors, tall, double, painted and planted to look like the hill.

"Well," said Thane. He looked at the doors. He looked at the Templar mark on his own arm. "I suppose we go in as if we belong. Right?"

Nobody had a better idea.

Thane walked up to the doors and pushed.

They swung open. Freely, on oiled hinges, without a sound. Beyond them was a long hall of dressed stone, lit low and red from somewhere Nageru could not see, empty from end to end.

They went in as if they owned it. Thane first, then Caleth, then Korroc, then Nageru, and Arueshalae last. Nageru closed the doors behind them himself, and heard them settle, and felt the light from outside go off his back.

The hall was empty. The air was warm, and smelled of hot metal and something sweeter under it that he did not want to put a name to. Their footsteps went ahead of them down the stone and did not come back.

There was a stair at the far end, going up, and they climbed it, and the room at the top of it held a statue.

It was a demon. That was plain even before Nageru had seen the whole of it — something huge and many-legged, carved in black stone with its wings half spread, rearing up off a plinth with its forelimbs raised and its head bent down to look at whoever came up the stair. A cloud of carved things swarmed about its shoulders. Locusts. Thousands of them, every one cut separately, every one with its legs.

They were halfway across the room toward the second stair when it spoke.

The voice came out of the stone. It was enormous. It shook in Nageru's chest and in the soles of his bare feet, and it said something in a language he did not know, a language that sounded the way the Worldwound's wind sounded when it came through broken stone. He knew only that it was Abyssal, because he had heard enough of it in the last year to know the sound of it, and that he could not understand one word.

He looked at Korroc. Korroc looked at him. Neither of them understood one word.

Thane's face had gone very still. Thane understood.

And Caleth turned, without hurrying, to face the statue, and answered it.

He answered it in the same language. His voice was calm and level and carried well, and it went on for a sentence and then another, and there was a rhythm in it, a lift at the end, like the end of a prayer. Nageru did not know the words. He knew the shape. It was the shape of a response. Something said back to something said, the way a congregation answers.

Then Caleth stopped.

The statue was quiet.

They stood in the red light and waited for it to speak again. It did not.

"What did you say?" said Korroc, out of the side of his mouth.

"Later," said Caleth.

"What did *it* say?"

"Later," said Thane.

Nageru turned his head.

He had not heard anything, exactly. He had felt it: a change in the air of the room, a draft along the floor and then not a draft, the way the air in a house changes when a door somewhere opens and closes. He looked across the statue room. There was another pair of doors on the far side of it, double, iron-bound.

"A door," he said quietly. "Just opened. And closed."

The double doors opened.

A giant stood in them.

A hill giant, by the size and the shape of him — twelve feet and more, broad and brutish, in a filthy leather jerkin, with a pick as long as Korroc slung across one shoulder and grit in the hair of his forearms. He filled the doorway. He stood in it and looked at the five of them, and did not move, and did not speak. He only looked, with small dull eyes, the way a dog looks at something it has been told to watch.

Nobody moved.

Then there was movement behind them, in the statue room itself, where there had been nothing a moment ago. Nageru turned and a man was standing in the middle of the floor beneath the stone demon's raised legs.

Not a man. Tall and beautiful and dark-haired, in fine clothes, with a pair of great dark wings folded neatly down his back. An incubus. He smiled at them.

And bowed.

It was a deep bow, a courtier's bow, one hand to his heart. He came up out of it smiling still.

"Ah," he said. He spoke in Common. His voice was warm and pleased, the voice of a host. "You have arrived. The mistress of the Midnight Fane has been expecting you." He spread one hand toward the doors behind him. "If you would care to accompany me, I will take you to her. She will grant you an audience."

The five of them looked at one another.

It was a very quick look. Nageru saw Thane's eyes go to the giant and to the door behind the incubus and to the stair they had come up, the three ways out, and count them. He saw Korroc's hand flex at his side, empty, where the axe should have been. He saw Arueshalae's wings draw in, very slightly, tighter against her back.

He saw Caleth make up his mind.

"Lead on," said Caleth.

The incubus inclined his head. "Follow me." He turned, without the smallest concern for having five armed strangers at his back, and walked toward the far door he had come from.

Caleth looked round at the others. He shrugged, very slightly. Then he followed.

"I do not like this," Arueshalae said, very low, at Nageru's shoulder.

"Unless you have a different plan," Nageru said.

She did not.

The giant fell in behind them. Nageru heard his feet on the stone, heavy and slow, and the faint scrape of the pick's head on the iron of the doorframe as he ducked through.

---

The incubus led them through the door and onto a balcony.

It ran around three walls of a great room, a story above its floor, with a stone rail at waist height. The incubus walked along it at a pleasant stroll, and they walked behind him, and Nageru looked over the rail as he went because there was no way not to.

Below them was a workroom. A long one, high-ceilinged, lit by the same low red from no place he could find. Down one side ran a table, and on the table lay chests. Dozens of them. They were open, and empty, and he could see from up here that they were lined — thick gray lead along every inside face, and over the lead, padding, quilted and soft as a cradle. Along the far side of the room ran another table, crowded with machines. Brass and iron and glass, gears and clamps and things like the arms of scales, arranged with great care, and Nageru could not tell what a single one of them was for.

Eight incubi were working down there. They moved between the tables quickly, without speaking, carrying things in both hands that were too small to see from above, setting them down in the padded beds of the chests with a gentleness that was worse to watch than any violence Nageru had seen in a year.

"They're packing the crystals," Thane said, very low.

Nobody answered him. Nobody had to.

They went on around the balcony, and through another door at its far end, and the incubus stepped through ahead of them and turned neatly to one side, to let them pass, the way a steward steps aside at a banquet door.

They came into the room.

It was smaller. There was an alchemist's table along one wall, crowded with retorts and burners and long glass coils, and something on the burners was bubbling, and the smell of it was the sweet smell under the hot metal that Nageru had noticed in the entrance hall, very strong now. A man stood at the table with his back half to them. Red-skinned, broad through the shoulders, with a pair of curved black horns sweeping back from his brow. He was pouring something from one vessel to another with great care, and he did not look up.

"Making elixirs," said Caleth, under his breath.

There was a guard. It floated near the ceiling in the corner, and Nageru's eyes went to it and did not want to stay. A great swollen mass of flesh, lumped and knotted with growths, held up by two leathery wings that did not look strong enough to hold it. No legs. Arms, a cluster of them, hanging down. Eyes, too many eyes, scattered across the tumorous hide without any order. And a mouth, a huge one, splitting the bulk of it nearly in two, crowded with teeth.

"The floating thing is a gibrileth," Caleth said, still low. "The red one's a cambion."

And there was a woman in the room.

She stood in the middle of the floor with her back to them, as though she had been looking at the alchemist's work. She was in armor. Full plate, polished, close-fitted, worked all over in a fine raised pattern of vines. A sword at her hip. On the back of the plate, between the shoulder blades, a sunburst. And Nageru knew that sunburst, and knew that armor, or armor like it; he had seen it a hundred times on the paladins of Drezen and the paladins of Kenabres. It was Iomedae's.

She heard them come in.

She turned.

Nageru had seen her face. He had seen it on the wall of the cathedral in Kenabres, in a painted window, a long time ago now, on the morning of Armasse before the world broke. He had seen it again in Drezen, cut in stone among the heroes of the Fourth Crusade, worn by a century of Worldwound wind and still plain enough to know. He had walked past it most mornings for months. The same brow. The same set of the mouth, and the line along the jaw, and the steady eyes.

It was the face of a woman who had been dead for decades.

The five of them stopped where they stood.

Nobody breathed. Nageru felt it go through all of them at once, like cold water: Korroc's weight settling back onto his heels, Thane's hand going to his belt and stopping there, Arueshalae's wings drawing in so tight he heard the leather of them creak. And Caleth, beside him, gone completely still, with Radiance at his hip and one hand halfway to its hilt.

The woman in Iomedae's armor looked at them.

"Yaniel," Caleth whispered.
