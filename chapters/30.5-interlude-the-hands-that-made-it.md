# The Hands That Made It

*Interlude Three — Repaired, Not Recovered · The Broken Moons*

---

*The Ivory Sanctum had fallen. Xanthir Vang was dead. The four knights had accomplished what they had set out to do.*

*But bringing Aravashnial home was proving to be something very different from rescuing him.*

### I. The First Night

*Korroc*

He had removed his armor hours ago, but he still felt as though he were carrying its weight. His hammer rested against the wall, his shield beside it. He had eaten something because Sosiel had insisted, although he could not have said what it was.

Now, well past midnight, he sat in a chair beside Aravashnial's bed.

The infirmary continued its usual routine around them. An attendant moved between patients, checking bandages and administering medicines. A wounded crusader complained when his injured leg was examined. Somewhere near the entrance, two healers discussed supplies that would need replenishing in the morning. Korroc sat in the chair beside Aravashnial's bed and listened to every sound. He paid little attention to any of it.

His attention remained on the elf lying in the bed. Aravashnial had not woken. His chest rose and fell. Occasionally his fingers twitched against the blanket. Once he had turned his head slightly toward the wall. But he had not opened his eyes.

Korroc leaned forward, resting his elbows on his knees. He had healed the elf. He knew he had. He could still remember the moment the collar had come free. The needles withdrawing from Aravashnial's neck, the blood, the terrible relief of finally being able to reach him with divine magic. Then the crystals. Five of them. Korroc looked at Aravashnial's arm. There was no scar. That was what troubled him. He had watched the incisions close beneath his hands. He had felt torn flesh knit together, damaged organs repair themselves, the dreadful unnatural corruption retreat before the power Torag had granted him. He had done everything he could.

And the elf lying in front of him looked as though none of it had happened. His skin was still pale. His face was drawn, the bones sharper than Korroc remembered. He had lost far too much weight. But there were no wounds. Nothing to show what had been done.

Korroc rubbed both hands over his face. "Should've found you sooner," he muttered. Aravashnial did not answer. "Aye. I know. Not much use saying it now." He sat back.

For a long while he watched the elf breathe. He had been thinking about what Thane had said in the Sanctum, standing three feet back from the table with the man on it. *We don’t know if that’s the real Aravashnial.*

Korroc had been furious with his cousin. Not because Thane had been wrong to ask. Because Korroc had been afraid of the same thing.

They had been fooled once. Completely. They had welcomed a creature into Drezen wearing the face of someone they trusted. They had examined it with magic. They had questioned it. They had watched it walk the halls of the citadel. And none of them had known. Now they had found a man in a cage, wearing a collar designed to prevent any magic from reaching him, with crystals buried in his body and weeks of suffering written into every movement.

Korroc wanted that to be enough. He wanted to look at the man in the bed and know. But Thane's question remained. Korroc reached out, then stopped with his hand halfway to Aravashnial's shoulder. He withdrew it. "You're home," he said quietly. "Whatever else happens, you're home."

He settled deeper into the chair. And kept watch.

---

*Caleth*

The next morning Caleth found Korroc exactly where he expected. The dwarf was sitting beside the bed, arms folded, chin resting against his chest. He was asleep. Or something close to it. Caleth stopped in the doorway. For a moment he simply looked at them.

Aravashnial lay beneath a clean blanket, his long hair loose against the pillow. Someone had washed the blood from it. Someone had found him a fresh linen shirt. He looked almost ordinary. That was the first thing Caleth noticed. The second was how much older he seemed. Not in years. Elves did not age that way.

But there was something in the sharpness of his face, in the hollows beneath his eyes, that had not been there before. Caleth approached quietly.

Korroc opened one eye. "Morning."

Caleth asked him, "Did you sleep?"

Korroc answered, "Aye."

"When?" said Caleth.

"Just now," Korroc said.

Caleth pulled another chair closer. "Has he woken?"

Korroc shook his head. "Sosiel checked him twice. Says there's nothing more he can do with healing. Body's sound enough, considering."

"Considering," said Caleth.

"Aye," Korroc said.

Caleth sat. He had spent much of the night going through the things they had recovered from the Sanctum. Documents, journals, books, pieces of correspondence. Evidence of experiments that should never have been conceived, much less performed. There was enough material to occupy the crusade's scholars for months. And Aravashnial would have understood more of it than any of them.

Caleth looked at him. He had questions. Hundreds of them. About Vang. About the Sanctum. About the crystals and the elixir. About a journal that had been kept locked in a drawer. About two letters that had survived only because they had hidden them where a demon had not thought to look. About Aelariel. About Talia. About himself. He had spent weeks imagining the conversation that would follow Aravashnial's rescue. Now he could not imagine beginning it.

"He's going to be all right," Korroc said.

Caleth looked at him. "Is he?"

The dwarf considered that. "He's alive."

"That wasn't what I asked," said Caleth.

"No," Korroc admitted. "It wasn't."

They sat together in silence. It was late morning when Aravashnial moved. At first Caleth thought he had merely shifted in his sleep. Then his fingers curled against the blanket. His brow tightened. His eyes opened.

"Aravashnial?" Caleth said.

The elf stared upward. For one moment there was no recognition in his expression. Then his eyes widened. He drew a sharp breath. And recoiled. His hands flew to his ears.

Caleth stood. "Aravashnial—"

The elf squeezed his eyes shut. His breathing came fast, shallow and increasingly desperate.

Korroc was on his feet. "Easy, lad. Easy. You're—"

"Stop," said Aravashnial. The word was barely audible.

Both knights froze.

Aravashnial curled toward the wall, pressing his hands harder against his ears. "No. No, please—"

Caleth looked around. The sunlight coming through the high windows was bright. An attendant was moving a tray of instruments. Somewhere beyond the door, two soldiers were speaking. A cart rattled across the courtyard. Ordinary sounds.

Aravashnial flinched at every one. Aravashnial made a small, strangled sound.

Aravashnial was shaking.

"Easy," Korroc said. "Easy, you're safe." He reached toward the elf's shoulder.

Aravashnial jerked away so violently that Korroc stopped with his hand in the air. "Don't—"

He tried to move away as Korroc approached. The blanket tangled around his legs. "Don't touch me!"

Korroc stopped immediately. "All right. All right, lad. Nobody's touching you."

But Aravashnial was no longer listening. His eyes remained shut. His hands covered his ears. He was shaking.

Caleth could see the muscles in his throat working as he struggled to breathe. "Aravashnial. Listen to me." Nothing. "It's Caleth." The elf drew another sharp breath. "You're in Drezen. We found you. You're safe."

Aravashnial shook his head. "No—too much—" His words dissolved into a gasp. Then his body went slack.

Korroc caught him before he could fall from the bed. "Sosiel!"

The cleric was already coming. He took one look at Aravashnial and stopped the attendant who had followed him. The other patients were startled, but Sosiel ignored them. "Korroc, lay him back. Carefully."

Korroc obeyed.

Sosiel examined the elf, then straightened. "He's unconscious."

"What happened?" Korroc demanded.

Sosiel looked around the infirmary. At the windows. At the lamps. At the patients. At the instruments on the tray. Then he looked at Caleth. "How long was he kept in darkness?"

Caleth swallowed. "Weeks."

"And silence?" Sosiel said.

Caleth answered, "Yes."

Sosiel closed his eyes briefly. "Then we have made a mistake."

Korroc's expression darkened. "What mistake?"

"We brought him somewhere that feels perfectly ordinary to us," said Sosiel. The cleric looked down at Aravashnial. "But to him, this room may as well be a battlefield." He turned to the attendant. "Prepare one of the private chambers. Cover the windows completely. Remove anything unnecessary. No visitors except those I authorize." He paused.

---

*Korroc*

By late afternoon the room was ready. It was small, with thick stone walls and a single window that had been covered with heavy cloth. The bed stood against the far wall. A chair sat beside it. A washbasin and pitcher occupied a narrow table near the door. The private room was almost completely dark. The only light came from a hooded lantern on the nightstand. Sosiel had insisted that the room remain quiet. No attendants entering without warning. No sudden light. No unnecessary conversation. Korroc had agreed to all of it. He sat beside the bed, watching Aravashnial sleep.

It was not peaceful sleep. The elf's fingers had begun moving against the blanket some minutes earlier. His breathing had changed. His head turned from side to side. Then came the words. "No."

Korroc leaned forward. "Aravashnial?"

The elf's brow tightened. "No. I told you—" His hand jerked against the blanket. "Please—"

Korroc felt something cold settle in his stomach. He had heard enough in the Sanctum to recognize the sound of someone pleading.

Quietly, Korroc said, "Aravashnial. Wake up."

The elf twisted sharply.

Korroc reached toward his shoulder, "Aravashnial. It's Korroc."

The elf's eyes opened. For one heartbeat he stared at the ceiling. Then his gaze found the dwarf. His entire body went rigid.

Korroc held perfectly still. "Easy."

Aravashnial tried to move backward. There was nowhere to go. His shoulders struck the wall. He pulled his knees toward himself, wincing as the movement strained muscles that had barely begun to recover. "Don't—"

Korroc held his hands up, "Nobody's going to hurt you."

The elf shook his head. His hand rose suddenly to his throat. He felt along the skin beneath his jaw. Once. Twice. His fingers moved faster.

Korroc understood, "The collar's gone." Aravashnial stopped. "You hear me? We took it off."

The elf's fingers remained pressed against his neck.

"All of it," Korroc continued. "Every piece."

Aravashnial stared at him. Then his gaze dropped. He looked at his arms. Slowly, cautiously, he pushed back one sleeve. His skin was unmarked. He touched the place where one of the crystals had been. Pressed gently. Then harder. Nothing. He pushed back the other sleeve. Another smooth stretch of skin. His breathing had begun to slow.

Korroc remained where he was. "We got them out," he said. "All five."

Aravashnial looked up. His eyes moved over Korroc's face as though searching for something. Recognition came gradually. Not the simple recognition of a familiar face. Something deeper. Something that made his expression change. "Korroc?"

"Aye."

A long silence. "You're—" His voice broke. He swallowed and tried again. "You're real?"

Korroc felt his throat tighten. "Aye, I'm real."

Aravashnial looked toward the covered window. Then the lantern. Then the door. "Where—"

Korroc answered, "Drezen."

He stared.

Korroc continued, "We brought you home."

Aravashnial's eyes returned to his arms. He touched the unbroken skin again. "The crystals? The collar?"

Korroc answered, "Gone."

For several seconds Aravashnial did not move. Then he lowered his head. His hands fell into his lap. Korroc waited. He had expected questions. Perhaps anger. Perhaps relief. Instead the elf sat in silence, looking down at his own hands. Finally he whispered, "I thought—" He stopped. Korroc did not press him.

After a while Aravashnial looked up again. "You found me." It was not a question.

Korroc nodded. "Aye."

Aravashnial stared at him. And very slowly, as though some terrible calculation had finally reached its conclusion, he began to believe it.

Korroc remained in the chair. He did not reach for him. He did not ask what had happened. He simply stayed.

*Thane*

The first thing Thane did upon returning to Drezen was ask for the guard reports. The second was ask for every record of a man who had entered or left the citadel since the false Aravashnial had disappeared. The third was make someone very unhappy by insisting that the records were incomplete. He was not particularly troubled by the unhappiness.

"You're asking for every soldier?" Anevia said.

"Every soldier. Every servant. Every messenger. Every wagon driver. Anyone who had reason to come through the gate," Thane said.

"That's hundreds of people," said Anevia.

"Aye."

"Thane."

He looked up.

Anevia folded her arms. "We're already searching."

"Then search better," Thane said.

Her expression changed. Thane knew that expression. He had seen it on people who were deciding whether to remind him that they outranked him. Anevia decided against it. "Tell me what you're thinking."

Thane spread the reports across the table. "The creature knew Drezen. It knew the people here. It knew where Aravashnial worked, who came to see him, who was likely to notice if something changed."

"We know."

"It also knew we were coming."

Anevia nodded.

"And it had time to prepare."

"Yes."

"So why would it run?"

She considered that.

Thane tapped the table.

"Vang's dead. The Sanctum's fallen. But the creature didn't need Vang to tell it what to do. It had its own reasons for being here."

"You think it's still inside."

"I think we've no reason to assume it isn't."

Anevia looked down at the reports. "Then we keep looking."

Thane nodded. He had no intention of stopping. There was a real Aravashnial lying in the infirmary. He wanted to believe that. He had seen the collar. The crystals. The laboratory. He had watched Korroc pull things from the elf's body that no living creature should have survived. It was difficult to imagine anyone constructing such a deception. But difficult was not impossible. He had learned that lesson. And until he knew where the other one had gone, he was not going to forget it.

*Nageru*

The next several days passed slowly. Nageru had never been particularly troubled by silence. He could sit for hours without speaking, and he had always considered that a useful quality. Aravashnial seemed to appreciate it. Sometimes Korroc sat with him. Sometimes Caleth. Nageru took his turn when the others were needed elsewhere. Thane came occasionally, although he rarely stayed long. The elf slept through much of the first two days. When he was awake, he spoke little. Sosiel examined him every morning, checking his strength, his reflexes, his ability to concentrate. He had explained to the knights that magical healing could restore flesh and mend injuries, but it could not simply erase weeks of starvation, exhaustion, and terror.

"His body has been repaired," the cleric told them. "That is not the same thing as recovered."

Nageru looked toward the closed door. "How long?"

Sosiel answered carefully. "I don't know. Days. Weeks. Possibly longer. Some of the physical weakness will improve quickly now that he's eating. The rest..." Sosiel paused. "We cannot measure all of it with a spell."

There was another difficulty. Aravashnial did not like being touched. He tolerated Sosiel's examinations when he knew they were coming, but even then he sometimes stiffened when the cleric reached toward him. Once an attendant approached from behind to adjust his pillow. Aravashnial nearly fell from the bed trying to get away. After that, Sosiel gave explicit instructions that nobody was to touch him without warning and permission.

Nageru had seen the aftermath of torture before. He had seen men who could not bear the sight of chains, women who could not sleep with doors closed, soldiers who flinched whenever someone raised a hand. He had never seen anyone who seemed afraid of the ordinary world itself.

He had seen men recover from wounds that should have killed them. He had seen bodies restored by divine magic in the space of a heartbeat. He had also seen men who could walk and fight again long before they could bear to sleep. Aravashnial was not yet ready to do either. Nageru sat beside the bed with his hands resting loosely upon his knees. The lantern had been opened a little farther. It was the third adjustment Sosiel had permitted. The room was no longer completely dark, though the windows remained covered.

By the fourth day the lantern could remain uncovered while he was awake. It was progress. Small progress. But progress nonetheless.

On the fifth morning, Nageru arrived to find Aravashnial propped against several pillows, a cup of broth resting on the blanket. He had managed half of it. That was an improvement.

"Good morning," Nageru said.

Aravashnial turned. "Morning." His voice was still rough, but stronger than it had been.

Nageru took the chair beside the bed. For a while neither spoke.

"How long?" the elf asked.

Nageru looked up. "How long what?"

"How long have I been gone?" said Aravashnial.

Nageru answered carefully, "Several weeks."

The elf considered that. Nageru learned that the elf often needed time to decide whether to ask another question. Sometimes he asked. Sometimes he closed his eyes and went back to sleep.

Then Aravashnial asked, "The others?"

Nageru looked at him. "Which others?"

"The soldiers." Aravashnial frowned, searching for the words. "The four who were taken with me."

Nageru straightened.

Aravashnial looked down at his hands. "Jerribeth meant to leave them with the dragon." His expression tightened. "I remember the cave. The soldiers were there. Two were injured." Nageru waited. "Then you and the others appeared." Aravashnial looked at him. "Caleth was fighting Jerribeth. I remember that. She cast something, and then..." He shook his head. "I was no longer in the cave." His fingers tightened around the blanket. "I never knew what happened afterward."

Nageru understood. "They survived," Nageru said.

Aravashnial went very still. "All four?"

"All four." The elf stared at him. Nageru continued. "We fought the dragon and the Templars. We brought the soldiers back to Drezen."

Aravashnial looked down.

"They were treated. All recovered," Nageru said.

Aravashnial stared at him. Then he nodded. "Good."

Nageru asked, "You have been worrying about them?"

Aravashnial's mouth tightened. "I was responsible for the wards. The dragon broke the tower."

"You stayed to hold it. And those men stayed with you," said Nageru.

Aravashnial looked away.

Nageru continued, "None of that makes what happened your fault."

The elf did not answer.

Nageru watched him. There were some things a man could be told. And some things he would have to believe for himself. But the elf looked more at ease than he had at any point since Nageru entered the room.

*Nageru*

The sound came from the private room. A heavy thud. Then something smaller struck the floor. Nageru was already moving when Korroc looked up. Sosiel was carrying a tray of fresh bandages. He set it down and followed them. Nageru reached the door first. Aravashnial was on the floor. He had made it several steps from the bed. One hand was braced against the stone, the other gripping the edge of a small table that had evidently failed to support him. A cup lay overturned beside him. The elf was trying to push himself upright. His arms were trembling.

"Aravashnial." Nageru crossed the room.

The elf looked up. "I am quite all right."

Korroc arrived behind him. "You're on the floor."

"I am aware," Aravashnial said.

"Just checking," said Korroc.

Sosiel knelt beside the elf. "Did you strike your head?"

"No," Aravashnial said.

"Are you dizzy?" said Sosiel.

"Not now."

"That wasn't the question."

Aravashnial looked irritated. Nageru took that as a promising sign. Sosiel examined him carefully. Then he looked toward the window. "What were you attempting to do?"

Aravashnial hesitated. "Walk."

"To where?" Sosiel said.

The elf glanced toward the covered glass. "The window."

Korroc followed his gaze. "Why?"

Aravashnial looked at him. "Because I wanted to see outside."

There was enough frustration in the answer that Korroc did not immediately respond.

Nageru understood. For weeks the elf had been confined to a cage too small to stand in, surrounded by darkness and silence. Now he had been brought to another darkened room, with the windows covered and his movements limited by weakness. This room was safe. That did not make it easy to remain inside.

Nageru crouched. "May I help you stand?"

Aravashnial looked at him. Then he nodded. Nageru offered his arm. The elf took it. The moment he tried to put weight on his legs, his knees began to buckle. Nageru caught him. Aravashnial made an angry sound.

"Easy," Nageru said.

"I can stand," said Aravashnial.

"Yes," Nageru said.

"I merely need a moment."

"Then take one."

Korroc brought the chair over. "Sit."

Aravashnial looked at it. Then at the bed. "I was trying to walk."

"And you did. Three steps. Now sit," said Korroc.

Sosiel helped settle him into the chair. Aravashnial was breathing harder than the effort should have warranted. Nageru remained beside him until the trembling stopped. Then he crossed to the window. He looked back at Sosiel.

The priest considered the elf for a moment. "A little light," he said. "No more."

Nageru loosened the covering. A narrow band of daylight appeared. Aravashnial flinched.

Nageru stopped. "Too much?"

The elf closed his eyes. For a moment Nageru thought he would ask for the curtain to be replaced. Instead Aravashnial took a slow breath. "No." He opened his eyes again. "Leave it."

Nageru stepped aside. The window overlooked part of the inner courtyard. Gray daylight filled one corner of the room. Beyond the glass lay the western courtyard. Soldiers crossed it in small groups. A wagon stood beside the supply stores. Somewhere below, a blacksmith was working. Ordinary life. Aravashnial stared. Nageru watched his expression. The elf had spent weeks in a place where he could neither see nor hear the world around him. Now he was looking at soldiers carrying sacks of grain. And for several minutes, that seemed to be enough.

After a while Korroc said, "You could have asked us to open it."

Aravashnial did not look away. "I know."

"Then next time, ask," Korroc said.

A pause. Aravashnial answered quietly, "Very well."

Sosiel checked his pulse once more. "You are not ready to walk unassisted. You have lost a considerable amount of strength."

"I am aware," said Aravashnial.

"And if you continue falling, you will make my work considerably more difficult," Sosiel said.

Aravashnial gave him a tired look. "I shall endeavor not to inconvenience you."

Sosiel smiled. "I would appreciate that."

They helped him back into bed. As Nageru adjusted the pillows, Aravashnial looked toward the window again. "Walking would be easier with my staff."

Korroc stopped. Nageru saw the change in his expression.

Aravashnial did not. "Would someone bring it to me?"

Korroc looked at Sosiel. Then at Nageru. The elf frowned. "What?" No one answered immediately. "Where is it?"

Korroc pulled the chair closer. "Aravashnial." The elf looked at him.

*Caleth*

Now, a week later, Caleth stood outside the private room with the remains of the staff wrapped in cloth. Korroc had told him what Aravashnial asked. He had also told him that nobody had explained the damage. Caleth looked down at the bundle. There were three pieces of the main shaft. The wood had split badly where the tower collapsed. Several fractures ran along the grain, and one end had been crushed beneath fallen masonry. The moons were worse. The carved wooden ornament at the top had broken into several pieces. Some had been recovered from the rubble. Others were missing. He had never considered the workmanship particularly remarkable. It was elegant, certainly, but simple. A staff carried by a man who valued function over ornament.

He knocked softly. "Come in."

The others were already there. Korroc stood beside the window. Nageru sat against the far wall. Thane leaned near the door, his eyes moving from Caleth to the bundle in his hands. Aravashnial was sitting upright. He had been awake for most of the morning, which Sosiel considered encouraging. There was more color in his face, though his cheeks remained hollow and his hands were still unsteady. He looked at Caleth. Then at the bundle. For a moment his expression was that of confusion.

Caleth approached the bed. "I brought it." Caleth placed the wrapped pieces on the bed.

Aravashnial looked at the bundle. Then he reached forward and unfolded the cloth. His hands stopped. For a moment he simply stared.

He picked up the longest section of the shaft. Turned it slowly. His thumb passed over a familiar worn place in the wood. Then he reached for the broken moon. He lifted one fragment. It was a curved piece of carved wood, small enough to fit in his palm. He turned it over. His expression changed. Caleth had seen Aravashnial angry. He had seen him irritated, impatient, exasperated, and occasionally amused. He had never seen that expression before. It was not simply grief. It was recognition of a loss that could not be measured by the object's value.

"I'm sorry," Caleth said.

Aravashnial did not look up. "Where are the rest?"

Caleth answered, "That's everything they recovered."

The elf examined the fragments again.

"All of it?" said Aravashnial.

"Yes," Caleth said.

Aravashnial set the piece down very carefully. Aravashnial closed his eyes. For a moment Caleth thought he might be angry. Then his hands began to tremble. Aravashnial ran one finger along the broken edge of the moon.

Caleth sat beside the bed. "I know losing your arcane bond must be difficult."

Aravashnial looked at him. For a moment Caleth thought he had said something wrong. Then the elf gave a faint shake of his head. "The bond can be restored. That is not—" He stopped. His gaze returned to the wood.

Caleth waited.

Aravashnial picked up the surviving section of the largest moon. "My father carved this." Caleth looked up. Aravashnial was still staring at the wood. "The shaft is from Kyonin. My parents gave it to me before I left." He turned the fragment in his hand. "My father made the moons."

Nobody spoke. The elf gave a small, humorless smile. "He complained about the carving for weeks. Said the grain was difficult. Said he should have chosen another piece." He stopped. His fingers closed around the fragment. "I told him it was fine."

The room remained silent. Caleth looked at the broken staff. He watched Aravashnial gather the fragments closer. He understood now that the staff was not merely a tool. It was not merely the object through which Aravashnial had focused his magic. It was something the elf had carried through years of service, through Kenabres, through the fall of Blackwing, through the Worldwound. Something that had survived everything until the tower at Drezen. Something from a home he never discussed.

Aravashnial carefully placed the fragment beside the others. "Thank you for bringing it."

Caleth nodded. There was nothing else to say.

Sosiel arrived and took one look at the elf's face. "That is enough visitors for today."

Korroc made a face. "We've barely been here."

"And you may return tomorrow," said Sosiel.

Nageru rose. Thane pushed away from the wall.

Aravashnial was tired, and Sosiel had made it clear that he was not to be kept awake simply because the knights had questions.

Caleth began gathering the broken pieces into the cloth.

Aravashnial's hand closed over the largest section. "Leave it."

Caleth stopped. "The staff?"

"Yes," Aravashnial said.

"It would be safer if—" said Caleth.

"Please," Aravashnial said. The word was quiet.

Caleth looked at him.

Aravashnial's fingers remained wrapped around the wood. "Leave it with me."

Caleth slowly released the piece. "Of course."

Caleth followed the others out. Aravashnial was still looking at the broken moon when the door closed.

*Thane*

Thane returned that evening. The corridor was empty. The lantern in Aravashnial's room had been lowered, and the elf was asleep. Thane stood in the doorway for a moment. Then he crossed to the bed. The broken staff lay at the foot of the bed still wrapped in cloth. He looked at it. He had heard what Aravashnial said about his father. He understood more than he cared to explain. A weapon was not always just a weapon. A tool was not always just a tool. Sometimes a thing mattered because of whose hands had made it.

He looked at the sleeping man. Then at the staff. Thane knew what it meant to have something left from home. He also knew that sometimes the thing itself mattered more than anyone else understood. You could replace a blade. You could replace armor. You could replace a staff. But you could not replace the hands that had made it. He carefully lifted the section from the blanket. Aravashnial stirred. Thane froze. The elf's fingers moved against the blanket, but his eyes remained closed. After a moment his breathing settled. Thane gathered the remaining fragments. He wrapped them securely, making certain none of the smaller pieces could escape. Then he slipped out.

He went directly to the forge. He cleared a worktable, spread out the cloth, and laid the pieces in order. Three sections of shaft. Fragments of the triple moon. Not enough to restore the carving. He studied the breaks. The main shaft was possible. Difficult, but possible. The moon was another matter. Thane turned one fragment over. The original carving was delicate. Precise. Every curve had been worked by hand, and the wood had aged differently from one section to another. He could replace the missing pieces. But without knowing the original design, he would be guessing.

Korroc found him there the next morning. Thane had already laid the fragments across the workbench. He had measured each piece, marked the breaks, and begun drawing a plan.

Korroc stopped in the doorway. "What's that?"

Thane did not look up. "Staff."

"I can see it's a staff," said Korroc.

"Then why'd you ask?" Thane said.

Korroc came closer. He looked at the pieces. Then at Thane. "You took it."

"Yes," said Thane.

"From his room," Korroc said.

"Yes."

"Without asking."

Thane set down the measuring rule. "Yes."

Korroc stared at him. For several seconds neither spoke. Then Korroc said, "He asked us to leave it."

"I know," said Thane.

"And you took it anyway," Korroc said.

"Yes."

Korroc's expression darkened. "Thane—"

"Look at it," said Thane.

Korroc stopped.

Thane pointed to the shaft. "Three main pieces. Two clean breaks, one crushed. The grain's sound along most of the length. We can reinforce it."

"The moons?" Korroc said.

"That's the problem," said Thane.

Korroc looked down.

Thane continued. "He doesn't need a new staff."

"No," Korroc said.

"He needs this one," said Thane.

Korroc's anger did not disappear. But it changed. He picked up one of the broken sections. The wood was old, dry, and carefully polished from years of use. "Can we save it?"

Thane nodded. "Most of it."

"How?" Korroc said.

"Core," said Thane.

Korroc looked up.

Thane tapped the drawing. "Drill through the shaft. Run a narrow mithral rod down the center. Join the wood around it. Strengthens the whole thing without making it much heavier."

Korroc studied the plan. "Could work."

"It will work," Thane said.

"You're very certain," said Korroc.

"I'm a very good smith."

Korroc gave him a look.

Thane ignored it. "The wood can be mended once the core's fitted."

"Aye," Korroc said. "I can do that."

"The moons are another matter," Thane said. He laid the surviving pieces out. "There isn't enough left."

Korroc picked up a fragment. "Could carve new ones."

"Yes. But they wouldn't be the same," said Thane.

The two dwarves stood over the bench.

Then Korroc sighed. "All right."

Thane looked at him. "All right what?"

"We fix it," Korroc said.

Thane nodded.

Korroc pointed a finger at him. "But when he asks where it went, you're the one explaining. And if he's angry, I'm not defending you."

"Wouldn't expect you to," said Thane.

Korroc looked at the pieces again. Then he reached for his tools. "Let's get started."

The Purity Forge had become crowded. Not with people. With projects. He had other work waiting.

Radiance needed another enchantment. Caleth wanted a keener edge laid into the blade, and Thane had already agreed to do the physical work while Caleth supplied the necessary magic. His own weapons needed attention as well. And there were the items recovered from the Sanctum. One of them lay on a nearby bench. An unholy longsword. Thane had been thinking about it since they returned. The Purity Forge had once been used to corrupt good items. They had reclaimed it. Purified it. But they had never truly tested what that meant. Not like this. He looked at the sword.

He laid the unholy longsword across the workbench. The weapon had come from the Sanctum. It was well made. That irritated him. The balance was good. The steel was excellent. The enchantment was strong. And the thing had been made to serve evil.

Thane turned it over. "All right," he muttered. "Let's see what you do."

He had spent months using the forge to strengthen weapons and armor. He understood its ability to support enchantments, and he had begun to recognize the peculiar way its magic responded to the work done upon it. But this was different. This was what the forge had been made for. Not improving a blade. Changing it. He set the longsword upon the forge and began. The first attempt failed. Not dramatically. There was no explosion. No burst of abyssal fire. The magic simply refused to change. Thane stood over the blade, frowned, and tried again. The second attempt was worse. For a moment the enchantment shifted. He felt it through the working, a resistance followed by a brief yielding. Then it snapped back into its original pattern. Thane swore.

"You're approaching it incorrectly." He did not turn around. Joran stood a short distance away.

Thane had known he was there. He had been ignoring him on principle. "Didn't ask."

"No." Joran watched the sword. "You are attempting to strip the corruption away."

"That's generally what you do with corruption," Thane said.

"Not with this," said Joran.

Thane looked at him.

Joran approached the workbench. "When I used the forge to corrupt an item, I did not simply remove what was good." Thane's expression hardened. Joran noticed. He continued anyway. "I took the existing enchantment and turned it. The power remained. Its purpose changed."

Thane glanced toward the longsword. "You're saying I have to work with what's already there."

"Yes," Joran said.

"Rather than break it," said Thane.

"Precisely."

Thane considered that. Then he looked back at Joran. "You know how to do it."

"I know how I did the opposite," Joran said.

"That's not the same thing," said Thane.

Joran lowered his eyes to the forge. "But perhaps it is close enough."

Thane did not like Joran. He had not forgotten what the man had done with the forge before they reclaimed it. He had not forgotten the weapons that had come out of this room, or the people those weapons had been used against. He also knew the man had been working here longer than any of them.

"Show me," Thane said.

Joran looked up.

Thane pointed at the sword. "You tell me what you did. I decide whether it's useful."

Joran nodded. They worked through the next hour together. It was not comfortable. Joran explained the pattern of the corruption, the way the enchantment had been turned inward upon itself, and the points at which the forge could be made to reverse that turning. Thane listened. He asked questions. Occasionally he argued. Joran answered.

At last Thane looked at the blade and stepped aside. "Your turn."

Joran stared at him. "What?"

"You said you knew how it worked," Thane said.

"I did," said Joran.

"Then try."

Joran looked toward the forge. For the first time since Thane had known him, the man's expression held something other than caution or resignation. "Are you certain?"

"No." Thane folded his arms. "But I'm willing to find out."

Joran took his place. The attempt was slow. Thane watched the enchantment respond, saw where Joran guided it and where the forge resisted. Once, the unholy magic surged. Joran nearly lost the working. Thane stepped forward and caught the pattern before it collapsed. The forge answered. Light traveled along the blade. The oppressive weight of the old enchantment broke, not into fragments, but into something cleaner. Thane felt the change settle. He reached for the sword. Joran stepped back. The blade was still the same length. The same weight. The same excellent steel. But the magic within it had changed.

Thane examined it carefully. Then he smiled. "Well."

Joran watched him. "Did it work?"

Thane turned the blade toward the light. "Unholy to holy." He looked at the former corruption-forger. "Looks like you can learn a new trick."

Joran gave a quiet, uncertain laugh.

Thane set the sword aside. "Don't get too pleased with yourself. We've got more work."

For the first time, Joran did not seem to mind.

*Caleth*

The command room had not been this crowded since the preparations to retake the citadel. Books covered one end of the long table. Documents covered the other. Between them lay inventories, sketches, recovered correspondence, lists of prisoners, accounts of experiments, and several objects that nobody was particularly eager to touch. Irabeth stood at the head of the table. Anevia had already begun sorting the intelligence into categories. Aron was examining the plans of the Sanctum. Horgus was calculating the value of the recovered materials with the expression of a man who deeply resented being asked to assign monetary worth to something so unpleasant.

Caleth placed another stack of documents on the table. "These came from Vang's private chambers."

Anevia opened the top folder. "More?"

"Considerably more," Caleth said.

She sighed. "Of course."

Korroc entered carrying a bundle wrapped in cloth. "One more thing." He placed it before Irabeth. Caleth stepped forward and unfolded it. The armor beneath was full plate, bearing the symbol of Iomedae.

Irabeth stared. "Where did you find this?"

"In the Sanctum," Caleth said. "It belonged to Yaniel."

For a moment nobody spoke. Irabeth reached out and touched the armor.

Caleth continued, "We discussed what to do with it. Korroc is the only one of us who wears full plate, and we thought..."

Caleth nodded. "If you'll accept it."

Caleth knew she was right. He also knew how many of those answers might be painful. He said nothing.

*Thane*

The staff was proving to be more difficult than the holy sword. Thane found that insulting. He had spent several days working with Korroc on the shaft. The original wood was too badly damaged to support the stresses of continued use without reinforcement. Simply joining the pieces would restore its appearance, but not its strength. They needed something inside it.

"Mithral," Korroc said.

Thane looked up. "That's what I was thinking."

Korroc continued, "A narrow core. Light enough that he won't notice much difference in the balance."

"And strong enough that the wood won't split again," Thane said.

They worked out the dimensions together. The wood had to be opened carefully. The core needed to fit precisely, without forcing the pieces apart.

Joran watched them measure. "Cold iron," he suggested.

Thane looked over. "What?"

"Some of the inlays. Use cold iron."

Korroc frowned. "Why?"

Joran gestured toward the staff. "He's a Riftwarden. He works against demons. Give the inlays a purpose beyond decoration."

Thane considered that. "You're suggesting it would make the staff harder for a demon to take from him."

"That's the idea," said Joran.

Korroc looked at Thane.

Thane shrugged. "Can't hurt."

They worked for most of the afternoon. Once the mithral core was ready, Korroc fitted the wooden sections around it and worked a mending over the broken shaft. The magic drew the original wood together along the fractures. When it was finished, the staff was whole again. Mostly. The triple moon remained in pieces. Thane arranged the fragments on the table. He could reconstruct part of the design. But not all of it. There was too much missing.

"Without the rest," Thane said, "or a pattern, we're guessing."

"Could ask him," Korroc suggested.

Thane shook his head. "Not yet."

A voice came from the doorway. "What are you doing?" Both dwarves looked up. Caleth stood there. His gaze moved from the tools to the repaired shaft, then to the broken moon. For a moment he said nothing. "You took the staff. Did you ask?"

Korroc cleared his throat. Thane gave Korroc an irritated look. Korroc ignored him.

"I wasn't happy about it," Korroc continued. "But if we're going to help the man recover, perhaps we ought to give him something back."

Caleth approached the table. He examined the shaft. "The wood?"

"Original," Thane said. "All three pieces. Mithral through the middle."

"And the moon?" Caleth said.

Thane gestured toward the fragments. "That's the problem."

Caleth studied them. Then he reached into his bag. "I may have something." He withdrew a book. Dark leather. An emblem worked into the front. Three moons.

Thane was on his feet before he had decided to be. "Put that down here."

Caleth laid it on the workbench. Thane reached for one of the surviving fragments and laid it against the design. It fit. Not approximately. Exactly. He tried another. The same.

Korroc leaned closer. "Well, I'll be damned."

Thane moved the remaining fragments into position. The surviving portions of the carved moon matched the emblem on the cover precisely, even down to the small variations in the curves. There were gaps, but the design beneath them showed precisely what was missing. The moons on the book were the same size as the moons that had crowned the staff.

Thane looked up. He looked at Caleth. "Leave the book."

Caleth's hand settled on the cover. He hesitated. Thane noticed. "It's a pattern. That's all I need."

Caleth looked down at the book. For a moment Thane thought he might refuse.

Thane understood why. They had recovered that book from Vang's possessions. It had been carried through the Sanctum, protected from damage, and returned to Drezen with considerable care. It was also one of the few things Aravashnial had left that had not been destroyed.

"I'll take care of it," Thane said.

Caleth looked at him. Then at the staff. "Very well." He placed the book carefully on the bench. "Don't damage it."

Thane gave him an offended look. Caleth did not appear impressed. When the half-elf left, Thane fetched paper and a soft rubbing stick. He took an impression of the emblem. Then another. He measured the design carefully. The new moons would be carved from a single piece of suitable wood, shaped to match the original ornament. But he would not simply discard what remained of the old one. He marked the locations of the surviving fragments. Each would be fitted into the new carving. The old wood would remain part of the finished piece. It would not be exactly as it had been. Nothing repaired ever was. But the work of Aravashnial's father would still be there. Thane selected a block of wood. He examined the grain. Turned it. Examined it again. Then he reached for his carving tools.

When he finally assembled the pieces, the three moons were whole again. Korroc stood beside him, examining the work. "Think he'll notice?"

Thane looked at the carving. "Aye."

Korroc frowned. "That a problem?"

"No." Thane ran his thumb along one of the old sections. "He should."

The robes took longer. The black robes of the archmagi were not merely corrupted cloth. They were a powerful magical artifact whose enchantments had been shaped toward evil. The first attempt produced nothing useful. The second caused the forge to flare so violently that Joran ordered everyone back. Thane was not pleased.

Joran was considerably less pleased. "You cannot force that much magic to change all at once."

"Then how?" said Thane.

"Slowly," Joran said.

Thane stared at him. "That's your advice?"

"Yes. Slowly. Unless you'd prefer to destroy something that may be impossible to replace," said Joran.

Thane looked at the robes. He disliked admitting it, but Joran had a point. They began again. This time Thane worked with greater care, letting the Purity Forge strip away the old alignment of the enchantment before rebuilding it. Caleth assisted where his magic was needed. Korroc helped maintain the working. Joran guided them through the unfamiliar process. It was not quick. But eventually the black fabric began to change. The darkness faded through gray. Then silver. Then white. The damage Vang's destruction had left in the cloth closed as the enchantment reformed. When Thane finally lifted the robes from the forge, they were whole. White robes of the archmagi. He examined them for a long moment.

Then he turned to Caleth. "Here."

Caleth looked at the robes. "What?"

"Take them," Thane said.

"Thane—" said Caleth.

"They're yours."

Caleth took the fabric carefully. He examined the restored weave. "You succeeded."

"We succeeded," Thane said.

Caleth looked toward Joran. The former smith was standing near the forge, watching the white fabric. For a moment his expression was difficult to read. Then he looked away. Thane folded his arms. He still did not like the man. But he was beginning to think there might be something worth salvaging there. And considering what they had just done to Vang's robes, Thane found that thought rather appropriate.

*Korroc*

A week after their return, Irabeth called for a meeting.

Korroc objected. "No."

The commander looked up from her desk. "Korroc."

"No," said Korroc.

"I haven't told you what I intend to ask," Irabeth said.

"Doesn't matter."

Irabeth set down her pen. "Sit." Korroc remained standing. She sighed. "This isn't an interrogation."

"Then what is it?" said Korroc.

"A debriefing," Irabeth said.

"Call it what you like. The man's barely walking."

"I know."

"He's only just started sleeping without waking in a panic."

"I know that too."

"Then why—" said Korroc.

"Because he was held by Xanthir Vang for weeks," Irabeth said. Korroc stopped. Irabeth leaned forward. "Because the enemy had a Riftwarden in its custody. Because we have reason to believe that creature wearing his face had access to his study. Because we don't know what information Vang extracted from him, what secrets may have been compromised, or what other dangers remain."

Korroc's jaw tightened. "I know all that."

"Then you know why this must happen," said Irabeth.

He looked away.

Irabeth's voice softened. "Korroc, I am not going to put him in front of a room full of soldiers and demand he relive everything that happened. But he is part of this command. He understands the need for a debriefing better than most." Korroc said nothing.

"I've spoken with Sosiel. He believes Aravashnial is physically capable of attending a short meeting, provided we allow him to stop when necessary," Irabeth said.

"And if he doesn't want to?" said Korroc.

"Then we'll discuss that with him."

Korroc sighed. "Fine."

Irabeth nodded. "Thank you."

He turned toward the door. "Still think it's too soon."

"I know," Irabeth said.

Aravashnial did not object. That irritated Korroc almost as much as Irabeth's insistence. The elf listened to the explanation, asked who would be present, and requested the time and location. Nothing more.

"You don't have to agree to everything," Korroc told him.

Aravashnial looked up. "I'm aware."

"Then—" said Korroc.

"Korroc, I am a Riftwarden who has spent several weeks in enemy custody. A debriefing is necessary," Aravashnial said.

"You're still recovering."

"Yes."

"I am strong enough to sit in a chair and answer questions."

Korroc looked at him. Aravashnial held his gaze.

The dwarf sighed. "Stubborn elf."

"Frequently," said Aravashnial.

That was the end of the argument. Or so Korroc thought. The following evening Sosiel arrived with a cane.

Aravashnial stared at it. "No."

Sosiel set it beside the door. "Use it."

"I don't need a cane," Aravashnial said.

"You fell attempting to walk six feet," said Sosiel.

"That was several days ago," Aravashnial said.

"And yesterday you nearly fell again," said Korroc.

Aravashnial frowned.

Sosiel smiled pleasantly. "The cane will remain here." He left.

The next morning Caleth brought Aravashnial some of his own clothes. The elf refused to attend the meeting wearing infirmary linens. He insisted on dressing himself. It took longer than he expected. By the time he emerged from behind the screen, his hands were trembling slightly from the effort. But he was dressed in his familiar dark clothing. His hair was tied back. He looked, at least from a distance, like the man who had stood in Drezen's war room before the attack.

Korroc picked up the cane.

Aravashnial looked at it. "No."

"Take it," Korroc said.

"No," said Aravashnial.

"Fine," Korroc said. He brought it anyway.

They made it halfway down the corridor before Aravashnial's pace slowed. He tried to hide it. Korroc noticed. So did Nageru. By the next turn the elf was leaning against the wall.

Korroc stopped beside him. "Here."

Aravashnial looked at the cane. Then at Korroc. For a moment Korroc thought he would refuse again. Instead the elf took it. "Thank you."

Korroc nodded.

Nageru stepped closer. "Would you like an arm?"

"No," said Aravashnial.

They walked another few steps. Aravashnial stopped. Nageru waited.

The elf looked at him. "...Yes."

Nageru offered his arm without comment. They continued. At the door to the meeting room, Aravashnial stopped again. He released Nageru's arm. Straightened his shoulders. Adjusted his clothing. Then, with the cane in one hand, he entered.

Korroc watched him go. The change was remarkable. The weakness remained. The weight loss remained. The exhaustion was plainly visible. But Aravashnial held himself like a Riftwarden again. And Korroc realized that the elf was not simply preparing to answer questions. He was preparing to defend his place among them.

*Caleth*

The room was smaller than the main command chamber. A long table occupied its center. Irabeth sat in the middle, with Aron and Anevia on either side. Sosiel and Horgus were present, along with several officers and scribes. Three unfamiliar figures occupied chairs against the opposite wall. Caleth noticed Aravashnial's reaction immediately. The elf stopped. Only for a moment. But his shoulders stiffened. His gaze moved from one stranger to the next. Then he continued to the chair facing the command table. Caleth followed the look. The strangers wore no obvious insignia. Their clothing was formal but unremarkable. The eldest of the three was a gray-haired human man with a narrow face and a carefully trimmed beard. Beside him sat an elven woman whose expression revealed nothing. The third was a younger human woman, perhaps in her thirties, with a leather folio resting across her knees. They watched Aravashnial with the detached attention of people who had come to evaluate something. Caleth disliked them immediately.

The four knights took their designated seats against the side wall. Aravashnial sat alone opposite command. The arrangement bothered Caleth. He understood the purpose. A formal debriefing required a clear record, witnesses, and an orderly exchange of questions. But the single chair looked terribly isolated.

Irabeth began. "For the record, please state your name."

"Aravashnial."

"Your affiliation?" said Irabeth.

"Riftwardens."

"Rank and assignment?"

"Field Agent. Assigned to Kenabres, currently serving as Riftwarden advisor to the command of Drezen."

"Do you understand the purpose of this meeting?"

"Yes," Aravashnial said.

"Do you consent to proceed?"

"Yes."

Irabeth nodded to the scribe. "Very well." She folded her hands. "We are here to establish what happened following your abduction, what information may have been obtained by the enemy, and what threats may remain. You are not accused of wrongdoing."

"I understand," said Aravashnial.

"You may request a pause at any time," Irabeth said.

"Thank you."

Caleth watched Aravashnial. The elf's expression had settled into something familiar. Calm. Controlled. Almost impersonal. He had conducted debriefings before. Caleth knew that. Aravashnial had spent years in dangerous assignments, often returning with intelligence that had to be recorded and evaluated. He knew how to answer questions. He knew how to separate what he had seen from what he believed. He knew when to say *I don't know.*

Irabeth turned a page. "We know the circumstances of the attack upon Drezen. We also know that you and four crusaders were carried to a cave near the Gray Road. Begin with what happened after you were separated from those soldiers. Please begin with the moment Jerribeth removed you from the dragon's cave."

Aravashnial nodded. "I woke up in an unfamiliar area. I quickly learned I was at the entrance to the Ivory Sanctum." He spoke evenly. He described the arrival. The guards. The initial confinement. "I was brought before Xanthir Vang shortly afterward."

"What was his reaction?" said Irabeth.

For the first time, Aravashnial paused. "Delight."

Anevia looked up. "Delight?"

"Yes. He was particularly pleased that Jerribeth had brought him a wizard. More pleased when he learned that wizard was a Riftwarden. And considerably more pleased when he learned I had come from Kenabres," Aravashnial said.

"Why?" Anevia asked.

"He was responsible for the destruction of the Blackwing Library. He had considerable interest in the Riftwardens operating there," said Aravashnial.

"Did he recognize you?" Anevia said.

"Not initially."

"What changed?"

"My spellbook."

Caleth felt his attention sharpen.

Aravashnial continued. "Jerribeth brought it to him. Once he understood whose book he possessed, his attitude changed."

"How?" said Anevia.

"He became... enthusiastic," Aravashnial said. The word was delivered without emotion. "He spoke at length about the opportunity Jerribeth had presented him. A Riftwarden from Kenabres, alive. He assumed he had wiped them all out. He considered it a significant achievement."

Caleth remembered the vision. Jerribeth examining the book.

He glanced toward the strangers. They were listening very carefully.

Irabeth continued. "Describe the experiments."

Aravashnial paused. Then he began. He described the collar. Its ability to suppress magic. The needles. The restraints. The isolation chamber. The surgical procedures. He identified the creature responsible for implanting the crystals as a neh-thalggu. He explained the locations of the implants and what he could remember of their effects. He described the Nahyndrian elixir. The seizures. The voices. The attempts to alter him. He did not describe the pain unless asked. Even then, his answers were brief.

"Was the procedure painful?" said Sosiel.

"Yes," Aravashnial said.

"Did the crystals affect your ability to think?"

"At times."

"In what way?"

"Confusion. Intrusive thoughts. Periods of impaired awareness."

"Did you lose consciousness?" said Sosiel.

"Frequently."

"Do you remember everything?"

"No."

Caleth watched the scribe write. There was something terrible about the neatness of it. A man had been taken apart and experimented upon. And now it was becoming a series of orderly statements in a ledger. Across the room Korroc sat rigidly, his hands clenched on his knees. Nageru remained still. Thane watched the strangers.

Irabeth asked another question. "Did Vang attempt to obtain information from you?"

"Yes," Aravashnial said.

"What information?" said Aron.

"Riftwarden operations. Safehouse locations. Personnel assignments. Defensive wards. Methods of identifying demonic infiltration."

"Did you provide any of it?"

"Not voluntarily."

The room went quiet. The gray-haired stranger made a note.

Anevia leaned forward. "Explain."

Aravashnial looked at her. "I was subjected to several forms of magical and physical coercion. Some periods are not clear in my memory. I cannot guarantee what information may have been obtained."

One of the strangers shifted. Caleth noticed.

Irabeth continued. "What about the creature that impersonated you?"

For the first time Aravashnial looked uncomfortable. Not frightened. Something closer to disgust. "I saw it several times."

"Where?" Irabeth said.

"In the laboratory. It usually remained near the back of the room," said Aravashnial.

"Did it speak to you?"

"Not initially."

"Describe the first direct interaction."

Aravashnial lowered his eyes briefly. Then looked up. "Vang was speaking about Drezen. He had learned that the knights were searching for me." Caleth felt his stomach tighten. "He appeared greatly amused by the effort. He said there was no reason for the search to continue." Aravashnial paused. "Then he called the creature forward."

The room was silent.

Aravashnial continued, "It approached the table, stood before me, it had no features of its own. Its skin was gray but then it began to change."

"Into what?" Irabeth asked.

"Me," Aravashnial said.

Caleth remembered the creature they had encountered outside Drezen. The injuries. The torn clothing. The collar.

Aravashnial continued. "The transformation was complete. Height, facial structure, hair, even the apparent injuries."

"Could you distinguish it from yourself?" said Irabeth.

"Visually? No," Aravashnial said.

"And its voice?"

Aravashnial's expression tightened. "It spoke." He paused. "I heard my own voice."

Korroc shifted in his chair.

Aravashnial continued. "Vang was delighted. He told me the knights would soon believe they had found the man they were searching for." He looked toward Irabeth. "That was the last time I saw the creature."

Anevia made a note. "We know it reached Drezen," she said. "And gained access to your study."

Aravashnial's expression changed. "How much access?"

"We are still determining that," said Anevia.

He looked toward the strangers. Then back at Anevia. "I see."

Caleth did not like the way he said it. The questioning continued. They discussed Vang's laboratory. The defenses of the Sanctum. The other prisoners. The known Templar personnel. Aravashnial answered what he could. When he did not know, he said so. He did not speculate. He did not embellish. And he never once asked to stop.

Eventually Irabeth closed the final folder. "One last question."

Aravashnial looked at her.

"Do you believe you have been compromised?" Irabeth said.

The silence that followed seemed longer than any of the others. Caleth watched him. There were several possible answers. A proud man might say no. A frightened man might say yes. A man who wanted to protect his position might offer assurances he could not prove.

Aravashnial did none of those things. "I don't know."

Irabeth held his gaze. "Can you elaborate?"

"I resisted what I could. I remember much of what was done to me, but not all of it. I cannot account for every period of unconsciousness or impaired awareness," said Aravashnial. He paused. "I would prefer to tell you that nothing was obtained. I cannot do so honestly."

Irabeth nodded. "Thank you." She closed the record. "That concludes the debriefing. You are excused."

Aravashnial rose carefully. For a moment Caleth thought his legs might fail him. But the elf steadied himself with the cane.

He inclined his head to Irabeth. "Commander." Then he turned and left.

Caleth began to stand. "Knights," Irabeth said. He stopped. "Remain, please." Caleth looked toward the door. Aravashnial was already gone.

The first stranger spoke before the door had fully closed. "This confirms our concerns." Caleth turned.

The man was tall, gray-haired, and dressed in dark blue. His voice carried the practiced authority of someone accustomed to being obeyed.

Irabeth looked at him. "Explain."

"The field agent has been compromised."

"He said he doesn't know," Korroc snapped.

The stranger regarded him coolly. "Which means he cannot guarantee otherwise."

"And neither can you."

"Korroc," Irabeth said.

The dwarf fell silent.

The stranger continued. "The impersonator had access to his study. Potentially to his records, correspondence, and arcane materials. We must assume the Drezen safehouses are compromised, along with any associated personnel and operations."

Anevia leaned forward. "We've already begun reviewing those locations."

"That is insufficient."

Caleth felt his irritation rising. "What exactly are you proposing?"

The man looked at him. "Field Agent Aravashnial must be removed from Drezen and placed under Council supervision until a full investigation can be completed."

The room went still.

Caleth stared at him. "Removed?"

"Yes."

"For what purpose?" Caleth said.

"Further interrogation."

The word landed like a blow.

Korroc stood. "No."

The stranger ignored him. "His memory must be examined. Every possible disclosure must be identified. The Council has facilities suited to such work."

Caleth rose slowly. "What sort of facilities?"

"That is not your concern."

"It is now," said Caleth.

The man turned toward him. "You are not a Riftwarden."

"No," Caleth said.

"Then you do not understand the security implications."

Caleth took a step forward. "I understand that you want to take a man who has just spent weeks being tortured by Xanthir Vang and subject him to another interrogation."

"That is a gross mischaracterization."

"Is it?" said Caleth.

The stranger's expression hardened. "Field Agent Aravashnial has obligations to his organization."

"And this organization has obligations to him," Caleth said.

The words came out sharper than he intended. He did not care.

The stranger turned toward Irabeth. "Commander, this is not a matter for debate. The Council has authority over its agents."

Irabeth sat very still. Then she said, "Not in this matter."

The man frowned. "Excuse me?"

"Aravashnial serves as my Riftwarden advisor. He was taken while defending this citadel. His recovery is under the care of my high priest," Irabeth said.

"His affiliation—"

"Does not give you the right to remove a member of my command without my consent," said Irabeth.

The stranger's expression darkened. "You would obstruct a Riftwarden security investigation?"

"No." Irabeth stood. "I will cooperate with your investigation. I will permit a review of the safehouses. I will allow you access to the relevant records and personnel." She placed both hands on the table. "But Aravashnial is not leaving Drezen."

The stranger stared at her. "You are making a serious mistake."

"Perhaps." Her voice remained calm. "But it is my decision."

Caleth looked at her.

Irabeth continued. "He's been held by the enemy for weeks. He has cooperated fully with this debriefing. He has not refused a single question." She looked toward the door. "And he has barely recovered enough strength to walk down a corridor."

The stranger opened his mouth.

Irabeth raised one hand. "No." The room fell silent. "That is my ruling. You may report it to your Council."

The man sat back. Caleth could see the anger in his face. He did not particularly care. The meeting was adjourned shortly afterward.

---

*Korroc*

They found Aravashnial sitting at the far end of the corridor. Not near the meeting room. Not beside the door. He had made his way to a narrow bench beneath an unlit wall sconce, well away from the traffic moving between the command offices. His elbows rested on his knees. His head was bowed. For a moment Korroc thought he was asleep.

Then the elf spoke. "When do I leave?"

Korroc stopped. "What?"

Aravashnial lifted his head. "With the Council representatives."

Caleth came to a halt beside him. Nageru remained a step behind. Thane looked back toward the meeting room.

Korroc frowned. "You're not going anywhere."

Aravashnial stared at him. "Korroc."

"I'm serious," Korroc said.

"The Council will require—" said Aravashnial.

"Irabeth told them no."

The elf went still. "What?"

"She refused," Korroc said.

Aravashnial looked from Korroc to Caleth. Aravashnial leaned back against the wall. He seemed to be trying to understand something. His expression changed.

"You expected them to take you," Korroc said.

Aravashnial looked at him. "Yes."

"Why?" said Korroc.

The elf hesitated. "Because under the circumstances, it would be the expected procedure."

"Expected by whom?" Korroc said.

"The Council," said Aravashnial. He paused. "I am a Riftwarden. My position carries responsibilities, including the protection of information that cannot be permitted to fall into enemy hands."

"And you think that means they can simply take you?" Korroc said.

"They have authority to investigate a compromised agent," said Aravashnial.

"You're not a compromised agent."

Aravashnial looked at him. His expression was weary but serious. "I do not know that."

None of them gave an immediate answer.

The elf looked toward the floor. "I told Irabeth the truth."

"I know," Korroc said.

"Then you understand why the Council is concerned," said Aravashnial.

"I understand why they're concerned."

Caleth crouched in front of him. "We do not agree that the answer is to take you away."

Then Nageru stepped forward. "Would you like help getting back?"

Aravashnial looked up. This time he did not argue. "Yes," he said.

---

*Nageru*

Two days later, Irabeth summoned them again. This time the meeting was shorter. Aravashnial attended, although he was permitted to sit beside the knights rather than alone before the command table. The three Council representatives were present. Nageru noticed that none looked particularly pleased.

Irabeth began without ceremony. "I have reviewed the Council's concerns and consulted with my officers. My decision is as follows." She looked toward the three strangers. "The Riftwarden Council may assume direct oversight of the safehouses and other sensitive locations currently operating under its authority in Drezen. Those locations will be reviewed for possible compromise."

The gray-haired representative inclined his head. "Acceptable."

"I will also permit one Council representative to remain in Drezen as a Riftwarden advisor to this command," Irabeth said.

Aravashnial sat very still. Nageru noticed his hand tighten around the cane.

Irabeth continued. "However, Field Agent Aravashnial will remain in Drezen."

The stranger's expression hardened. "Commander—"

Irabeth raised a hand. "I haven't finished." He fell silent. "Aravashnial will retain his standing as a member of this command. His knowledge, both magical and demonic, is imperative to the security of Drezen. His duties will be adjusted as necessary during his recovery, but he will not be removed from his position."

She turned toward the Council representatives. "Furthermore, he will not be excluded from the organization to which he has devoted his service."

The gray-haired man frowned. "That is an internal Riftwarden matter."

"Not while it affects the operation of my command," said Irabeth. She folded her hands. "Any Council representative assigned here will be required to work with him. You may review his reports, examine the safehouses, and establish whatever reasonable safeguards are necessary. But you will not isolate him from Riftwarden operations in Drezen." She paused. "If your representative cannot accept those conditions, I will not accept the appointment."

The silence stretched.

Finally the gray-haired man said, "I will convey your terms to the Council."

Irabeth said flatly, "Good." She turned toward Aravashnial. "Do you understand the arrangement?"

The elf inclined his head. "Yes, Commander."

Irabeth asked, "Do you accept it?"

"Yes," Aravashnial said.

"Then the matter is settled," said Irabeth.

Nageru watched Aravashnial. The elf's expression remained controlled. But his grip on the cane had loosened. And when Irabeth dismissed them, he remained seated for a moment longer than necessary. As though he needed time to understand that the decision had truly been made.

---

*Thane*

The staff was finished three nights after the debriefing. Thane examined it one last time. The shaft was straight. The mithral core gave it strength without adding much weight. The cold iron inlays were subtle, worked into the original design rather than imposed upon it. And the triple moon stood whole at the top. Not entirely original. But close. The surviving pieces of the old carving had been preserved, fitted into the replacement wood with care. Thane turned it in his hands.

Korroc stood beside the workbench. "Think it'll hold?"

"Aye," Thane said.

"And the bond?" said Korroc.

Thane shrugged. "That's his business."

Korroc nodded. They had restored the staff. Whether Aravashnial could restore his arcane bond to it was another matter. Thane wrapped the staff in cloth.

Korroc looked at him. "Where are you going?"

"To return it," Thane said.

"Now?" said Korroc.

"He's asleep."

Korroc considered that. Then nodded. "Good."

Thane carried the staff through the quiet citadel. The private room was dark when he arrived. Aravashnial was asleep, one arm resting outside the blanket. Thane entered without waking him. He placed the staff beside the bed, where the elf would see it when he opened his eyes. Then he stood back. For a moment he looked at the man lying there. The elf had been through more than Thane cared to imagine. And Thane still had questions. About the impersonator. About what Vang might have learned. About what remained hidden. But none of those questions had anything to do with the staff. Thane looked at the carved moons. He thought of a father in Kyonin, working a piece of wood for his son. Of a gift carried for decades. Of something that had survived everything until a dragon brought down a tower. He nodded once. Then he left. He did not leave a note. He did not need one.

---

*Caleth*

It was nearly two weeks after their return when Sosiel finally permitted Aravashnial to return to his own rooms. He was still weak. He still tired easily. But he could walk short distances without assistance, and the headaches that had troubled him since the rescue were becoming less frequent. He had been warned not to overexert himself. Caleth suspected that warning would last approximately as long as it took Aravashnial to reach his study. The four knights were on their way to see him. Caleth carried Aravashnial's spellbook openly. It had been recovered from the Sanctum, and he had intended to return it for several days. He also carried something else. The journal. And the letters. He had not decided how to begin that conversation. He was still considering it when they reached the hall to his study.

The door to Aravashnial's study stood slightly open. Voices came from inside.

Raised voices. "...not a question of whether I am willing to cooperate!" Aravashnial.

Caleth stopped.

Another voice answered. "You are being deliberately obstructive."

"I am asking you to explain precisely what authority you believe permits you to remove my records."

"The Council has already determined—"

"The Council has determined nothing of the sort."

There was a sharp scrape of a chair. "You are insufferable."

"So I have been told."

Footsteps approached. The door swung open. One of the new Riftwardens emerged so quickly that he nearly collided with Nageru. He stopped abruptly. Nageru looked down at him. The man looked up. For a moment neither moved.

Then the Riftwarden stepped aside. "That man is absolutely insufferable."

He stalked away down the corridor. They watched him go.

Caleth then looked toward the study. "Well," he said.

Korroc grinned. "Sounds like he's recovering."

Caleth pushed the door open. Aravashnial was seated behind his desk. The study was in considerably better order than it had been immediately after the attack, although several stacks of papers remained in unfamiliar places.

The repaired staff rested within reach beside the desk. Caleth noticed it immediately.

Aravashnial looked up. His expression was still irritated. "If you've come to tell me I should be resting, you may join the queue."

Korroc laughed. "Good morning to you too."

Aravashnial leaned back. "Korroc."

His gaze moved to Nageru, then Thane. Finally Caleth.

And the spellbook in his hands. His expression changed. "Ah."

Caleth approached. "We thought you might want this back."

He placed the book on the desk. Aravashnial looked at it. For a moment his anger seemed to disappear. He reached forward and rested one hand on the cover. "Thank you."

Caleth nodded. "You're welcome."

Aravashnial opened the book and examined several pages. Then he closed it. His gaze moved across the study. "Apparently I should be grateful that anything remains."

Korroc frowned. "What happened?"

"The Council representative has decided that my records are a security concern," Aravashnial said.

"Well," Thane said, "some of them probably are."

Aravashnial gave him a sharp look. "I am aware of that."

Thane shrugged. "Just saying."

"The problem," Aravashnial continued, "is that he seems to believe every piece of paper in this room belongs to the Council."

Caleth glanced toward the desk. "Some of it does."

"Yes. And some of it does not," said Aravashnial. He opened one of the drawers. "Which brings me to another matter."

Caleth felt something tighten in his chest.

The elf looked up. "My journal is missing."

Nobody spoke. Aravashnial continued. "It was in a locked drawer. The lock has been opened, and the journal is gone." His expression darkened. "I assume the impersonator took it."

Caleth remained still. "Aravashnial—"

"The creature had access to this room. It apparently knew enough about my habits to locate things that were not left in plain sight," Aravashnial said. He looked toward the open drawer. "That journal contains decades of personal records. If Vang obtained it—"

Caleth reached into his bag. He withdrew the journal. And placed it on the desk. Aravashnial stopped speaking. He stared at the book. Then looked up. "Where did you get that?"

Caleth met his gaze. "Here."

The elf's expression hardened. "Explain."

"We were looking for information," said Caleth.

"That was in a locked drawer," Aravashnial said.

"Yes."

Aravashnial looked toward Thane. The dwarf folded his arms. "I opened it."

The elf stared at him. Then back at Caleth. "You had no right."

Caleth felt his own temper rise. "We were trying to find you."

"That does not give you permission to search my private journal," said Aravashnial.

"We knew Vang had taken you," Caleth said.

"I am aware."

"We needed information. Anything that might tell us where he had brought you, what he intended, or how to reach you."

"And you thought my private journal would contain that?"

"We didn't know what it contained."

"Precisely." Aravashnial's voice sharpened. "That is why it was private."

Caleth stepped closer. "We found information in your study that identified Vang. We found notes about the Woundworm. We found records that helped us understand what we were facing."

"Those were working documents," said Aravashnial.

"And we had no way of knowing what else you had recorded," Caleth said.

Aravashnial placed one hand flat on the journal. "Caleth, there are things in this book that have nothing to do with Vang, the crusade, or Drezen."

"I know," said Caleth. The words came out before Caleth could stop them. Aravashnial went still. Caleth felt Korroc shift behind him.

The elf looked down at the journal. Then back at Caleth. "What do you mean?"

Caleth swallowed. He had imagined this conversation. Not like this. Never like this. But the moment had arrived, and there was no useful reason to delay it. He reached into his bag again. "I found something else."

Caleth placed the first letter on the desk. Then the second. Aravashnial looked at them. His expression changed so suddenly that Caleth stopped moving. The elf stared at the aged parchment. For several seconds he did not speak. Then he reached toward the first letter. His fingers stopped just short of touching it.

"Where..." His voice was barely audible. "Where did you get these?"

Caleth glanced at Korroc. Then back at Aravashnial. "The quasit."

The elf looked up. "What?"

"Vang sent a quasit to Drezen. It brought a message demanding that we abandon the citadel." Aravashnial's eyes remained fixed on him. "It carried your field journal." The elf closed his eyes. Caleth continued. "The creature tore it apart and threw the pages over the battlements. We recovered what we could."

He touched the letters. "These were hidden inside the damaged cover." Aravashnial looked down. For a long moment he did not move. Then he carefully lifted the first letter. Aelariel's. Caleth watched him read. He had read that letter himself. He knew the words. *Brother, For once, I shall attempt brevity. You are an uncle.* He knew about the birth. The name. The Seeker's Spiral. He knew that Aelariel had been afraid. That he had asked his brother to come home.

Aravashnial's hand tightened slightly around the parchment. Then he set it down. He picked up Talia's letter. Caleth watched his expression change again. There was something almost unbearable about seeing the elf recognize those words. The humor. The affection. The invitation to come home. The small note Aelariel had added beneath his wife's signature. Aravashnial read it slowly.

Then he lowered the page. "I thought these were destroyed."

Nobody spoke. He looked toward the journal. Then back at the letters. "Vang had them?"

"He had the field journal," Caleth said. "We don't know whether he realized the letters were hidden in the cover."

Aravashnial stared at him. Caleth continued. "We found them after the quasit threw the shredded journal at us."

The elf looked down again. For a moment he seemed to have forgotten the others were in the room. Then his eyes moved to the private journal. And back to Caleth. Something changed. Caleth saw the moment it happened. The calculation. The realization. The understanding of what Caleth must have read. "Caleth..." His voice was different now. Quieter. Almost uncertain.

Caleth held his gaze. "Your brother was Aelariel." Aravashnial did not answer. "His wife was Talia Aranor." Silence. Caleth felt his own heartbeat. Aravashnial looked at him. The room seemed suddenly very small.

Caleth continued. "I found the birth certificate." The elf's eyes closed. Only for a moment. But Caleth saw it. He reached into his pocket and withdrew the folded, fire-damaged document. He placed it beside the letters. Aravashnial did not touch it. Caleth had carried the certificate for weeks. He had read it so many times that he knew every line. He had imagined asking about it. He had imagined anger. Denial. Explanations. He had not imagined the silence.

Finally Aravashnial spoke. "How much have you read?"

Caleth felt his temper stir again. "Enough."

"Caleth—" Aravashnial said.

"Enough to know that you knew who I was," said Caleth. The elf looked away. "Enough to know that you've been watching me for years."

Korroc looked between them. Nageru stood very still. Thane said nothing.

Caleth took a breath. "Why didn't you tell me?"

Aravashnial looked up. For a long time he did not answer. Then he said, "Because I was afraid of what would happen if I did."

Caleth stared at him. "To whom?"

"To you," Aravashnial said. The answer came quietly.

Caleth felt the anger rise again. "You don't get to decide that for me."

Aravashnial flinched. It was a small movement. But Caleth saw it. "I know." The answer surprised him. Aravashnial looked down at the letters. "When I found you, the people who had taken you in were good people. They cared for you." Caleth said nothing. "They had given you a home. A family." He paused. "I knew what would happen if I attempted to claim you." Aravashnial continued. "The Riftwardens do not permit family ties. Not for field agents. Not for officers. We surrender those things when we join."

Caleth finally spoke. "But your brother…"

A faint, sad smile appeared. But then he grew serious. "The Council did not know we were brothers. His marriage was never official." He looked at Caleth. "The Council had already erased your identity as the son of a Riftwarden. And right now those who are here do not know who you really are."

His face closed again. "If I had claimed you, the Council would have intervened. I had already seen what happened to your parents' home. Their possessions. Their records." He stopped. "I would not allow them to take you from another family."

Caleth swallowed. "You could have told me later."

"Yes," said Aravashnial.

"At the Academy," Caleth said.

"Yes."

"When we met in Kenabres."

Aravashnial looked down. "Yes."

"After Drezen," said Caleth.

The elf closed his eyes. "Yes."

Caleth stared at him. "Then why?"

Aravashnial remained silent for several seconds. "At first, because I believed it was safer." He looked up. "Later..." He stopped. Caleth waited. "Later, I no longer knew how." The answer was so simple that Caleth had no immediate response. Aravashnial looked at the letters. "Every year it became more difficult. Every time we spoke, there was something else I should have told you." His expression tightened. "And eventually I had kept the truth from you for so long that telling it seemed..." He shook his head. "There is no adequate excuse."

Caleth looked at him. He had wanted an explanation. He had wanted the truth. Now he had both. And neither made the anger disappear.

"You should have told me," Caleth said.

"Yes," said Aravashnial.

"You should have trusted me."

"Yes."

Caleth looked away. He had not expected agreement to hurt quite so much.

It was Nageru who finally broke the silence. "There is another book."

Caleth looked toward him. Nageru nodded at the bag. Caleth remembered. He reached inside and withdrew the second spellbook. The one recovered from Vang's chambers. He placed it on the desk beside Aravashnial's own. The elf looked at it. His expression changed immediately.

Caleth noticed. "You recognize it."

"Yes," Aravashnial said.

"Whose is it?" said Caleth.

Aravashnial looked at the cover. "Aelariel's."

Caleth studied him. "The arcane mark inside is yours."

"I know," Aravashnial said.

"Why?" said Caleth.

For the first time since the argument began, something resembling genuine amusement crossed Aravashnial's face. "My brother had a habit." Caleth waited. "Of borrowing things."

Korroc raised an eyebrow. "Borrowing?"

Aravashnial looked at him. "That was the term he preferred."

Thane gave a short laugh. "Sounds familiar."

Aravashnial turned the book over. "It was originally mine. A blank spellbook I had prepared for future use." He ran one finger along the cover. "It disappeared."

Caleth frowned. "Your brother took it?"

"Yes," Aravashnial said.

"Without asking?" said Caleth.

"Naturally," Aravashnial said. Korroc chuckled. Aravashnial continued. "I discovered it several months later. He had already begun using it and had modified the cover."

Caleth examined the emblem. Three moons. Similar to the design on Aravashnial's own book. But not identical. The arrangement was slightly different. The lines were more elaborate. "Why the same symbol?"

Aravashnial looked at the cover. For a moment he seemed uncertain how much to say. Then he answered. "It was our family crest."

Caleth went still. The three moons. The emblem on the books. The carving on the staff. All connected.

Aravashnial continued. "My brother altered it. Said he preferred his version." He gave a faint smile. "I told him it was unnecessarily elaborate."

Caleth looked at the book. "And he kept it anyway."

"Of course." Aravashnial opened the cover. The first pages were written in a hand Caleth recognized. Precise. Compact. Short annotations, each carefully arranged. Then the handwriting changed. The letters became more hurried. The notes longer. The margins crowded with additional observations.

Aravashnial turned several pages. "My brother was a brilliant abjurer." He paused. "And an appallingly untidy scholar."

Caleth almost smiled. Almost. Aravashnial looked at the pages for a long moment. Then he closed the book. "May I keep this?"

Caleth stared at him. "It's yours."

Aravashnial shook his head. "No." He rested his hand on the cover. "It was his."

Caleth looked at the book. Then at the letters. Then at the man seated behind the desk. His uncle. The word still felt strange.

He had known Aravashnial for years. He had trusted him. Argued with him. Fought beside him. And all that time, the man had known.

Caleth took a breath. "Keep it."

Aravashnial looked up. "Caleth—"

"Keep it," said Caleth.

The elf nodded. "Thank you."

They remained in the study for some time. Korroc eventually moved toward the door, muttering something about finding food before Sosiel came looking for them. Thane followed him. Nageru lingered. Caleth stood beside the desk, looking at the books. Aravashnial had grown tired. It showed in the way he leaned back against the chair, in the slight tremor of his hands when he reached for a cup of water. But he made no attempt to dismiss them.

Caleth looked toward the repaired staff. "You have it back."

Aravashnial followed his gaze. "Yes."

"Does it work?" Caleth said.

"The staff itself? Yes," said Aravashnial.

"Your arcane bond?"

The elf hesitated. "I haven't tried to restore it yet."

Caleth looked at him. "Why?"

Aravashnial remained silent.

Caleth glanced toward the door. "Thane did good work."

Aravashnial looked at the carved moons. "Yes."

His expression softened. "Remarkably good work."

Caleth studied the staff. "The original pieces are still there."

"I noticed," Aravashnial said.

"Thane insisted," said Caleth.

Aravashnial nodded. For a moment neither spoke.

Then the elf said, "My father would have approved."

Caleth looked at him. Aravashnial's gaze remained on the staff. "He was very particular about his work." A faint smile. "He and Thane would probably have spent several hours arguing about the proper way to reinforce the shaft."

Caleth almost laughed. He could imagine it. Then the silence returned.

Aravashnial looked toward the journal. "I am still angry that you opened it."

Caleth met his gaze. "I know."

"It was private," Aravashnial said.

"I know," said Caleth.

"And there are things in it that I never intended anyone to read."

Caleth nodded. "Yes."

Aravashnial studied him. Then he sighed. "But I understand why you did."

Caleth looked down. "We thought we were going to lose you." The words came out more quietly than he intended. Aravashnial said nothing. Caleth continued. "We knew Vang had taken you. We knew what he was capable of. And every day we couldn't find you..." He stopped. For a moment he could see the Sanctum again. The laboratory. The cage. The collar. The crystals. The man lying motionless on the table. He looked up. "We were running out of time."

Aravashnial's expression changed. "I know."

Caleth shook his head. "No. I don't think you do." The elf remained silent. "We found your boots in a pile of discarded clothing. We found the records of what Vang was doing. We saw enough to know what had happened to you." His voice tightened. "And when we finally found you, Korroc had to cut those things out of your body while Thane tried to decide whether you were even real." Aravashnial lowered his eyes.

Caleth drew a breath. "So yes. We opened your journal. We searched your study. We read things you didn't want us to read." He looked directly at him. "And I would do it again if it meant finding you."

For a long moment Aravashnial did not speak. Then he nodded. "Understood."

Caleth waited. There were still questions. So many questions. About Aelariel. About Talia. About the Council. About the Spiral. About why his parents had died. About why the only surviving member of his birth family had spent twenty-five years watching from a distance. But Aravashnial was exhausted. And Caleth had waited this long. He could wait a little longer.

Nageru moved toward the door. "We should let him rest." Caleth nodded.

Aravashnial looked at him. "Caleth."

He stopped. "Yes?"

The elf hesitated. Then said, "I am glad you found me." Caleth stood very still. Aravashnial looked down at the desk. "All of you."

Caleth swallowed. For a moment he could not think of anything to say. Then he nodded. "So am I."

He turned toward the door. Nageru waited for him in the corridor. Behind them, Aravashnial remained seated at his desk. His spellbook lay before him. Beside it rested Aelariel's. The two letters were carefully folded. The journal was closed. And within reach stood the staff his father had made, its broken moons restored. Caleth paused outside the study. He looked back through the open door. For the first time since the dragon had carried Aravashnial away, the room looked almost as it should. There were books on the desk. Papers in untidy stacks. A cup of water that would probably be forgotten. And an elf who was already reaching for another document despite having been ordered to rest. Caleth shook his head.

Nageru glanced at him. "What?"

"Nothing," Caleth said. He looked down the corridor. Then, after a moment, he added, "He's going to drive Sosiel mad."

Nageru smiled. "Probably."

They walked away together. And for the first time in weeks, Caleth found himself thinking about a conversation that might happen tomorrow. Not a rescue. Not a battle. Not another desperate attempt to find someone before it was too late. Just a conversation. There were still secrets between them. There was still anger. There were questions that might never have satisfactory answers. But Aravashnial was alive. He was home. And this time, when Caleth returned to the study, there would be someone there to answer.
