# The Graves at Sesker's Gully

*Chapter Twenty-Four — Two Humans Came Back*

---

The riverbed did not care who walked it.

Thane had been reading it since first light, from the back of a horse he did not trust, and that was the whole of what it had told him. Cracked mud in plates the size of tabletops. River stones gone pale and round and smooth, every one of them the same as the last. Here and there a drift of gravel where water had last run, years ago, and decided not to bother again. A dry riverbed was the best road in the Worldwound for exactly the reason it was the worst trail: nothing stayed on it. No print. No scuff. No broken stem. Whatever had come this way had come this way, and the stones had closed over it like water closing over a dropped knife.

*Look at the floor first, lad. Walls lie. Floors don't.*

His mother had never said anything about floors that had been scrubbed by forty years of wind.

He looked anyway. It was what his eyes did. And somewhere in the second hour he caught himself doing a thing he had not decided to do, which was walking it. Not the horse — the horse walked it, badly, with a great deal of complaint about the footing — but himself, inside his own head. Two dwarves, out of a hole in a riverbank in the dark, one of them hurt. *Borin was injured.* North, the dead man had said. North up the riverbed. So: north. At night, because you would not move by day with every demon in the country out looking for you. Slow, because one of you was carrying the other, or was being leaned on, or was walking beside him with a hand out and pretending not to. How far would you get by dawn, with a hurt man and twenty feet of stone still in your shoulders?

Not far.

And where would you lie up, when the light came?

He looked at the bank on the left. Cut-earth, sheer, too low to hide under. He looked at the bank on the right. A spur of rock, a shadow under it. *There.* Maybe there. Or the one after it. Or nowhere at all, because they had kept going, because his father had never once in his life stopped a thing halfway when he could finish it badly instead.

He did not know. That was the point, and he kept arriving at it. A year ago he had not even known there was a road to walk. Now he knew the road and the direction and the reason, and none of it was any more use than a key cut for a door somebody had bricked up.

*He dug twenty feet with a stolen tool,* he thought, for perhaps the hundredth time since the tunnel. *Better than a year. On faith that it would come out.*

*I could not have done that.*

Beside him, a long way down, his cousin swore at his horse in Dwarven, steadily and without heat, the way a man prays.

"It's not doing anything," Thane said.

"It's *thinking* about doing something. I can feel it thinking."

"It's a horse. It doesn't think."

"That's worse." Korroc shifted in the saddle, a man trying to find a part of himself that did not hurt and failing. "A thing that big that doesn't think. Torag's beard. My father walked this. On his own feet. Like a dwarf."

He said it without seeming to hear himself say it.

Thane did not answer. Up ahead, Nageru ran on in that long easy stride of his along the edge of the bank, bare feet on stones that should have cut them, breathing like a man walking to a well; and a length behind him rode Caleth, upright and silent in the saddle, with one hand resting flat against the front of his coat.

He had ridden that way all morning. Hand on the coat. Thane had noticed it the day before, in the citadel, and the night before that, coming back across the broken ground from the drakes, and he had noticed that it was always the left side and always the same place, a little below the collarbone. He noticed things like that the way other men noticed weather. It did not mean anything. He had not asked.

The half-elf had spent four days in an absent man's study and come out of it with the face of somebody who had been in a fire, and nobody had asked him anything about that either, and that had been Thane's call as much as anybody's. A man who has decided to carry a thing alone has decided it for reasons. You can take the reasons off him. You do not get the man back afterward quite the same shape.

He knew a little about that.

---

It was a little after midday when the riverbed bent west and the bank on that side fell away, and Thane saw the roofs.

Not many. A dozen, perhaps, low and dark against the dun of the country, gathered in the lee of a long fold of ground the way sheep gather in the lee of a wall. Some of them had fallen in. Some of them had not, yet. No smoke. Nothing moving.

"Sesker's Gully," said Caleth.

Everyone looked at him.

"It was in his book." The half-elf did not elaborate, and did not need to. There was only one *his* any of them would have meant. "A village that grew up around the waystation, after Khar-Zadûn. Travelers stopped there, and then some of them did not leave." He looked at the roofs. "Then the Worldwound came, and all of them did."

Korroc grunted, which meant he had been told the same thing in some other form, by some other person, a long time ago, and was only now putting the two together.

Thane put a hand on the pommel of his saddle and stood up in his stirrups, which the horse objected to, and looked past the roofs.

He knew what he was looking for. He had known since the tunnel. A dwarven graveyard is not hard to find if you know how dwarves bury: on rising ground, facing the mountains if there are mountains and facing home if there are not, and always, always marked in stone that will outlast the people who set it.

There. Beyond the far end of the village, up the gentle slope of the fold. A long field of standing stones, low and square and gray, more of them than he could count from here, running away up the hill in rows that had been straight once.

"There's the cemetery."

He heard his own voice say it and did not much like the sound of it.

They rode in. Or three of them rode, and Nageru jogged, and at the edge of the first houses they stopped and got down — Caleth with a light step, Thane with the brief ungainly hop of a dwarf leaving a thing built for taller people, and Korroc by stages, in a long negotiation with gravity and the stirrup that ended with him standing on the ground holding the saddle with both hands and saying, to the horse, "*There.* Was that so hard," in a tone that made it unclear which of them he was addressing.

They stood for a moment and looked at Sesker's Gully, and Sesker's Gully did not look back.

Thane read it.

It was old work over older work. The houses were human, timber on fieldstone footings, and the footings were dwarven — he could see that at a glance, the long fitted courses under the rotten sills, the stone set by somebody who expected it to be there in two hundred years and had been right. A waystation's foundations, with a village built on top of it by people who had not known or cared whose they were. A well in the square with a dwarven well-head, and a human windlass gone to splinters. A street that had been paved once, and then had been mud, and then had been paved again by somebody worse at it.

Doors. That was what he looked at, after the stone. Every door in the street was either shut or gone. None of them hung open. That meant the place had been left, not taken; people who are fleeing shut their doors behind them out of habit, as though they will be back, and people who are being dragged out do not get the chance. And the doors that were gone had been taken off from the outside, later. By something looking for what was left.

He did not like it. He did not like any of it. But he had not expected to.

They walked in.

Caleth stopped.

It was in the middle of a stride, and it was sudden, and his head turned — not toward anything Thane could see, but the way a hound's head turns, half a heartbeat before the sound has arrived anywhere a dwarf's ears could follow it. The rest of them stopped with him. Korroc's hand went to his hammer.

And then Thane heard it too.

A scream.

Thin, and very far off, so faint it was almost the wind. But the wind did not hold a note like that. The wind rose and fell. This did not fall. It went on, one long unbroken scream with no breath in it, the sound of something that did not need to breathe, and as Thane listened it got louder — not rising in pitch but *nearing*, the way a cart nears on a road.

It was coming this way.

He did not decide. His feet decided, and he was saying it before he had finished hearing it.

"We should hide."

He stepped sideways, into the black slot of shadow between two houses, and was gone into it the way he had been going into shadows since he was a boy in a Mendevian camp with a sergeant shouting at him to be *quicker, lad.* The wall at his back. The dark at his front. Good.

Nageru came in after him, a pale shape and then not one.

Caleth started to follow — and stumbled.

It was not much. A foot coming down wrong on a broken paving stone, a bad step from a man who had not slept properly in five days. He would have recovered it himself in another heartbeat. But Korroc was beside him, and Korroc did what Korroc always did when a man beside him stumbled, which was catch him.

And so when the thing came around the corner at the far end of the street, the two of them were standing in the middle of it.

It was a cloud.

That was the first thing Thane's eyes gave him, and then they gave him the rest, and he wished they had not. A cloud the size of a hay wain, dark, roiling in on itself like smoke in a closed room — and in it, faces. Scores of them. Coming up out of the dark of the cloud and going back into it, rolling over one another, each one there for a heartbeat and then another in its place. Eyes. Mouths. The mouths were all open. The mouths were full of fangs. And every one of them was screaming.

"*Drocha swarm,*" Caleth said, flat, as if naming a mineral, and swore.

"Two of them, actually," said Korroc.

He was right. A second rolled around the corner behind the first, and the scream doubled.

Thane had his knives out. He had not drawn them; they had come out. He was measuring — the distance, the angle, where the thing would be in two heartbeats, where he would have to be — when the shadow beside him was suddenly empty.

Nageru had not held his place. He had looked out of the dark and seen two of his friends standing in the open street and that had been the end of the deliberation, if there had ever been any. He was across the gap and into the nearer swarm before Thane had finished understanding he had gone, and his fists went into it, and the cloud *closed on him*.

Thane saw it bite him. There was no other word. The faces came around him, all of them at once, and the mouths closed on the aasimar's arms and shoulders and the side of his neck, and Nageru's face went gray — not with pain, with something worse than pain, as though every bite took a little more than flesh.

Thane went out of the shadow.

The swarm had come past him. It had gone straight down the street at Korroc and Caleth without seeing him, and so he came up behind it, out of the dark, with his mother's blade leading — and it kept moving. It was a cloud. Clouds do not stop to be stabbed. He had to run after it, three steps, four, and reach into it, and the knife went into something that was not quite there and came out wet with something that was not quite blood.

Some of it hit. A great deal of it did not. Like stabbing smoke with a weight in the middle of it.

Behind him a voice spoke a word and the air went *hot*.

Caleth had come forward. He had come forward *toward* it, toward the second swarm, close enough to choose his ground, and he flung out his hand and a bead of light went off his palm and burst in the middle of the second cloud in a great roaring bloom of fire.

When the fire was gone, the second swarm was gone with it. There was nothing there but smoke, and a smell like burned hair, and a scream cut off in the middle.

*Good,* Thane thought. *Now the other one.*

Nageru was swinging. He was inside the first swarm, and the faces were on him, and he was hitting them — and the fists did damage, Thane could see it, faces going out like snuffed candles. But a great deal of what he threw passed straight through the cloud as though there were nothing there, because mostly there was not.

Korroc had moved up the street when the swarms came through, had not followed Caleth as the fireball was cast. Now he was coming down it with his heavy determined roll, shield up, saying something about clouds in Dwarven that Thane did not need to hear the end of.

Thane went in again on the swarm's flank. One. Two. Three. Knives in, knives out, and every time the blade found less than it ought to have. It was like gutting a fish made of fog. Then he was clear, and it had not even turned to look at him.

It turned on Nageru again. The faces went at him. Thane saw the aasimar take it, saw the gray go deeper in his face, saw him stand in it anyway.

*It's nearly done,* Thane thought, watching the cloud thin, watching the faces come up slower and fewer. He could see Korroc thinking the same thing from twenty feet away; he could see it in the set of his cousin's shoulders. *One more.*

Nageru hit it once more.

The swarm came apart. Not like the other — no fire, no roar. It simply went thin all at once, like smoke when a door is opened, and the faces went out of it, and the scream stopped, and there was nothing in the street but four men breathing hard and a long silence where the sound had been.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Caleth walked up to Nageru, and took a small vial out of his coat, and held it out.

Nageru looked at it. Then at Caleth. Thane watched him understand what it was for — watched him feel inside himself for whatever the swarm had taken, find the place where it had been, and understand.

"Restoration," Nageru said. "Thank you."

Caleth nodded once, and Nageru drank it, and some of the gray went out of his face.

Thane wiped his knives. Both blades, both faces, on the rag from his belt. There was not much on them. There was enough.

*Restoration.* He had seen the half-elf do that once before, a year ago, in a cave under Kenabres — knelt by a burned and half-blind elf and pressed a potion into his hand. Thane had thought at the time it was the act of a man who did not count what things cost.

He had since revised that. Caleth counted everything. He just paid anyway.

---

The cemetery began where the village ended, and the mausoleum stood in the middle of it like a man standing in a crowd who has stopped listening.

They came up the slope between the stones. Thane read the stones as he went — he could not have stopped himself — and they were dwarven, every one of them, low and square and deep-cut. Some had names. Most had only a rune, or a clan-mark, or nothing at all: a stone set to say *here is one of us* by people who had not known who. They were very old. The weather had gone into them and taken the edges off, and lichen had gone into the cuts. *The ones who died when Khar-Zadûn fell,* Dagna had said. *We buried a great many of them there.*

A great many. The rows ran away up the hill and over it.

And in the middle of all that dwarven stone, somebody had built a house for their own dead in a human fashion — big, and square, and tall, with a peaked roof and columns either side of the door. It was the largest thing still standing anywhere in Sesker's Gully, and Thane could see why: it had been built to be. Good dressed stone. A proper roof.

It had been made, once, to keep something in.

The twin doors had been stone faced with bronze, and they had been smashed. Not opened — *smashed*, the bronze buckled and torn away from the stone and the stone itself broken across, by something that had not cared about the doors at all and had only wanted what was behind them. Above the lintel a family name had been chiseled deep into the stone, and somebody had splattered it with blood and filth until it could hardly be read.

There was a low iron fence around the whole of it. Thane went toward the gate in it first, because he was always first through anything with a gate in it, and he had his hand out for the latch when the air in front of him went cold.

He stopped.

A crusader was floating in front of him.

That was all it was, at first. A man in old armor of a good Mendevian pattern, a long surcoat over it, a helm on his head — and his feet a hand's width off the ground, and all of him faint, like a man seen through dirty glass. Thane could see the mausoleum through his chest. He would have been hard to see at all, in the flat white light of midday, but for his eyes.

Under the brow of the helm, where the eyes should have been, there were two motes of red light. Not the red of fire. The red of something that had been fire once and had gone wrong.

Thane did not move. Not back, not forward. You do not move first in a doorway when you do not know what is standing in it.

Korroc moved. Of course Korroc moved. He came up past Thane with his shield up and Torag's hammer and anvil on the face of it, and planted himself, square and solid, between the dead thing and his cousin.

The specter's head turned. The red lights found Korroc.

"*And now the child comes before me,*" it said.

Its voice was terrible. Not loud. It came from no direction, the way the drocha's scream had, and it had a hollowness in it, like a voice heard at the bottom of a well. Thane felt the hair stand up along his arms.

Korroc frowned up at it.

"Why are you here?"

Something happened then that Thane did not see, but saw the edge of. His cousin's head tipped — very slightly — and his jaw set, and there was a moment in which Korroc stood perfectly still as though leaning against a wind nobody else could feel. Then he shook his head once, short and hard, a dog coming out of water, and the moment broke.

Whatever the thing had pushed at, it had pushed at the wrong dwarf.

"I am the last of my line," the specter said, "and I will protect our resting place."

Beside Thane, Caleth had shifted half a step to the side, the better to see past the floating shape, and was reading the defiled name over the door. Thane saw his lips move on it.

"Harnaste," Caleth said quietly.

The red lights did not turn toward him. They were on Korroc and they stayed on Korroc. When the thing spoke again its voice dropped, and went inward, and Thane had heard men talk that way in Kenabres alleys at the end of a long bad night.

"There is a darkness in the ground below," it said, "festering in the place forever barred to me. I am a monster made such by this land, yet the monster below is far worse!" The red lights flared. "Remove it from my proscribed crypt, that only its proximity may vex me — for one more night with the demon squatting within may well drive me to seek out your kin, slay them, and bring their souls back to revel with me!"

*Seek out your kin.*

Thane's hand had gone to his knife. He took it off.

Caleth was frowning. He was not frowning at the threat; Thane could see that. He was frowning the way he frowned at a page with a hole in it — at *proscribed*, at *barred to me*, at whatever sum that made in the half-elf's head. Then he stepped forward, past Korroc, close enough to the dead thing that the cold of it must have been on his face.

"On our oath," he said, "it will be done."

And he walked through the gate, and past the specter, and in through the broken doors, as if he had been invited.

The specter did not stop him.

Korroc looked at Thane. Then at Nageru. Then he shrugged, enormously, the whole plate of him going up and down, and followed the half-elf in.

Nageru inclined his head, a small grave bow to the floating thing, and went after.

Thane stood in the gateway for a moment longer.

*On our oath.* There was a reason, he thought, that he did not let other people make his promises for him, and he was looking at it. They had come up this hill for two dwarves. Not for a ghost, and not for its family's quarrels, and not for whatever was down in the dark under its house, eating.

He looked at the sky. It was the same color it always was out here: gray going on yellow, like an old bruise. It offered nothing.

He followed them in.

---

The first room was a chapel, or had been one.

The walls were carved. A woman, over and over, in long panels down every wall — tall, armored, a longsword in one hand and a shield on the other arm, and something about the set of her shoulders that would have told Thane her name even if he had never stood in a crusader camp in his life. *Iomedae.* The Inheritor. Somebody's idea of her, done by good hands, in good stone, so that she looked out from all four walls at once.

Somebody had gone at every one of her with claws.

Deep gouges, three and four together, raking down through her face and her sword arm and across her shield. Some of them so deep the stone had split. And high up on the east wall, above the reach of a man's arm, written in something that had been blood once and was now caked and black and stinking, a line of writing.

Thane could not read it. It was not any hand he knew — not Common, not dwarven, not the thieves' marks of Kenabres. The letters were all hooks and barbs, crowded against each other, cut in at an angle like something written by a claw. He did not need to know the language to know what kind of language it was.

Caleth stood under it with his head tipped back, and Thane watched his lips move on it, the way they had moved on the name over the door. Once. Then again, slower, as though the second time he wanted to be sure.

"*Filthy usurper,*" Caleth said. He said it quietly, to the wall, and did not look around.

Thane did not need the rest of it. He knew a trespasser's mark when he saw one. Something had come in here — through a door that had been made to keep it out — and had not been satisfied with breaking in. It had needed to write on the walls. It had needed the house to know who owned it now.

*Not for long,* he thought, with a small hard flare of something he did not examine.

Stairs went down from the middle of the room. They went down.

At the bottom, a set of double doors hung open on broken hinges, and beyond them a long low room, colder than the one above. Six stone doors ran along the east wall in a row, each one carved with the same woman, smaller, saluting with her sword. Statues of her stood in three of the four corners. The fourth corner was empty, and the floor there was scarred, and Thane did not want to think about where the fourth statue had gone.

Each door had a plaque. A name on each. Six names, one family.

Caleth went to the first and pushed. It moved, grinding, and he looked in.

A coffin. The lid shoved half off and cracked. Inside — nothing. A few scraps of cloth. Dust.

He went to the second. The same. The third. The fourth. Each one opened, each lid torn aside, each coffin empty. Thane stood in the middle of the room and watched him do it and read the floor while he did, and the floor told him what the coffins had already told him: drag marks. Old ones, and newer ones on top. Long, uneven drag marks, in and out of every door, going away toward the far end of the room.

Something had been taking the dead out of their beds and carrying them somewhere.

The fifth door. The same.

The sixth door was different. Caleth stopped before he touched it, and Thane saw why. The stone door stood open already — not wrenched, not broken, simply standing a little ajar, the way a door stands when it was opened from inside by somebody who did not trouble to close it after. Inside, the coffin lid lay to one side, set down whole. Not thrown. *Set down.* The coffin was empty. Nothing had been dragged out of it.

Nothing had ever been in it.

Caleth looked at the plaque.

"Arlys," he read.

Nobody said anything. Thane looked at the empty coffin that had never had anyone in it, and the open door, and thought about the specter outside, floating at its gate, *the last of my line*, and filed it in the place where he kept things he did not yet have the shape of.

Two more doors at the end of the room hung open, and beyond them, a smaller flight of stairs going down again. They took it. At the bottom a short passage, and another set of double doors, these ones shut.

Thane stopped.

He did not stop because he had heard something. He stopped because he had heard something *before* he knew he had heard it, and had learned a long time ago to trust the part of him that worked faster than the rest. He turned his head and looked back up the stair, and found Caleth's eyes already on him, and Caleth nodded.

They had both heard it.

A sound, behind the last doors. Low, wet, steady. Patient.

Gnawing.

It was the sound a dog makes with a bone it has had a long time and does not want to finish. Thane listened to it and thought about drag marks, and five empty coffins, and did not have to think very long.

They went down the last steps, quietly. Even Korroc, quietly, which was a thing to see.

At the doors, the gnawing stopped.

It did not trail off. It stopped, all at once, the way a sound stops when the thing making it has lifted its head.

*It knows,* Thane thought.

And on his back, against his spine, a voice said: *Oh.*

Then a great many voices said it.

*Oh, oh, oh, there it is, there it is —*

He had been carrying Fiendsplitter for most of a month. He had learned its moods the way you learn a horse's, or a cousin's. On the road it slept, mostly, and muttered in its sleep. In a fight it shouted. Near a demon it *wanted*, and its wanting came through the leather of the harness and the iron of the haft and into his back like heat off a forge. It had been muttering since the stairs. He had thought it was the dead.

It was not the dead.

*Let me at it.* A hall of old dwarves, all of them talking at once, all of them shouting the same thing. *Let me at it, let me at it, let's go, I want to bite it, I want to BITE it —*

Korroc put his shoulder to the doors and shoved them open.

---

Three sarcophagi stood against the north wall of the room beyond. Big ones, older than the doors upstairs, their lids carved with the faces of the dead who lay under them — a man, a woman, a man — sleeping in bas-relief with their hands folded on their chests. Statues of the same shield-and-sword woman stood in two opposite corners, both of them clawed.

The lids of all three sarcophagi had been pushed off.

There were bones everywhere. On the floor. In heaps against the walls. Gnawed, split, cracked for the marrow, the long bones broken across and sucked hollow and thrown aside. Thane's eyes went over it and counted, involuntarily, and stopped counting.

And at the far end of the room, in the middle of all of it, the demon stood up.

It was tall, taller than a man, and thin the way a starving thing is thin — all joints, all knuckle, the skin stretched over it like wet leather drying on a frame. Its head was long and narrow and too much of it was mouth. It had a great axe in one hand, held low, and something in the other that it had been gnawing on, and as Thane watched it let the something drop.

On his back, Fiendsplitter went mad.

It was not a voice any more. It was a roar. Every one of those old dwarves at once, a hall full of them on their feet and bellowing, and the haft of the axe *bucked* against his spine, twisting in the harness like a dog straining at a rope. *LET ME AT IT LET ME AT IT LET ME —*

Thane went very still.

He had to. It was the only way to hold the axe and hold himself at the same time. He stood in the doorway of the crypt with a demon in front of him and an axe screaming at the inside of his skull and made himself, for the space of one breath, go quiet. Into the place his mother had taught him. *Listen first.*

And in the quiet he found the other thing, the one he had carried longer than any axe, and he reached for it, and it was there.

One word. To her. Not loud.

The blessing came down over the four of them the way it always did, like a hand laid flat against a door — *mine; held; no further* — and in the instant it settled, he reached back over his shoulder and pulled Fiendsplitter out of its harness.

Nageru was already gone. He had gone the instant the doors opened, and now he was at the thing, and his fists went into it twice, hard, one-two, low in the body and then high — and Thane heard bone, or whatever it had instead of bone, crack.

Caleth went in at his shoulder. Radiance came up pale and bright and went into the demon's side.

Korroc turned his back on the fight.

He turned to Thane, and held out his hand.

He did not say anything. He did not need to. Thane was standing in the doorway with a battleaxe in his hands, a weapon he had carried a month and used, in that month, for nothing at all — because he was not an axe-man, had never been an axe-man, had never once in his life wanted anything in his hand longer than his forearm. And there was his cousin, who had been swinging heavy things at other heavy things since he was old enough to lift a hammer, with his palm out.

Thane threw it.

Korroc caught it by the haft one-handed, the way he would have caught a tool tossed across a forge, and Thane saw the moment the thing got into him. His cousin's eyes went wide. His head jerked as if somebody had shouted directly into his ear from an inch away, which, Thane supposed, somebody had. The voices went out of Thane's own skull all at once, like a door shut on a full hall, and the silence they left was enormous.

"Aye, aye," Korroc muttered, to nobody Thane could see. "*Yes.* Let's go. I heard you."

Korroc's mouth went flat. Whatever it was saying to him, Thane could not hear it now; he could only see it land, on his cousin's face, like a man being shouted at from an inch away. Then Korroc turned and went at the demon with the axe up.

It was not his weapon either. Thane saw that. It sat wrong in his hand, the balance a stranger's. But he swung it anyway, once, a long flat stroke with the weight of his whole body turning behind it — and the axe went into the demon's flank and *bit*, and Korroc's beard split in a grin that had nothing to do with him, and Thane knew exactly who was cheering.

The demon's head turned.

It had been looking at Nageru, at the fists, at the thing that had hurt it first. Now it looked at Korroc. Thane saw its attention shift the way you see a cat's shift, from one mouse to a better one, and so did Korroc — Thane saw his cousin see it, saw him understand what was coming, and saw him *set*.

He did not raise the shield higher. He went down. Into the stone. His feet spread, his weight sank, and something went out of the soles of his boots and into the floor of that crypt and the floor answered — Thane could not have said how he knew it, but he knew it — and Korroc Stonevein stood there rooted like a pillar under a roof.

The demon came at him.

Thane had seen things fight fast. This was faster. A cascade — the axe, a claw, the other claw, the axe again, the jaws — coming down on his cousin all at once and from every side, like hail. The shield took the first. The shield took the second. The shield took the third and the fourth, ringing like a struck bell, Torag's hammer and anvil turning everything that hit it. The armor took one. And one claw came in over the rim, too fast for anything, and opened a line along the side of Korroc's neck just above the gorget.

A scratch. A thin red line, beading. Not much more than a scratch.

Thane looked at that line on his cousin's neck and felt his whole body go cold and clear.

He reached down, past the blessing, past the knife-work, past everything he had learned in Mendev and Kenabres and the gutters, down to the place at the bottom of himself where the golden thing lived that the wardstone had left — and pulled. Pulled it up through his arm and into his hand. And threw.

He did not throw his mother's blade. He threw the one from his wrist, the third knife, the one that was his own habit and nobody's teaching. It went across the room in a flat bright line that he could not have thrown a year ago, that nobody could have thrown, and took the demon in the side of its long neck and went in to the hilt.

The demon made a sound. Not a scream. A sound like a branch splitting.

Nageru hit it again — one fist, square — and it staggered.

Caleth was behind Korroc. He could not get past the dwarf to it; there was no room. So he went *over* him — reached over Korroc's shoulder with Radiance and stabbed, and stabbed again, two quick thrusts past his friend's ear into the demon's chest, and the blade blazed white both times as if it were glad.

Korroc swung. Twice. Fiendsplitter going up and coming down, up and down, and his cousin's face lit with somebody else's joy —

And the demon was not there.

It did not dodge. It *went*. One moment it was there under the falling axe, and the next the axe was coming down through empty air into the bones on the floor, and Thane felt the cold breath of something arriving at his back.

*Behind me.*

He knew it before he turned. It had gone the way the babaus went in Drezen, the demon-trick, out of one place and into the other, and it had picked the other carefully. It had picked *him*. The one by the door. The one with no armor to speak of. The one who had just put a knife in its neck.

Inside Korroc's head, Thane did not doubt, Fiendsplitter was screaming with frustration.

He did not hear it. He did not hear anything.

The silence came down like a lid.

Not quiet — *silence*. Everything went out at once. The scrape of Korroc's boots on the bones. The ring of the shield. The sound of his own breath. Thane felt his mouth open on a word and nothing came out of it, nothing at all, and he understood in the same instant that the demon had done it to the room on purpose, and why. You cannot speak a spell in silence. You cannot call to your friends. You cannot hear the axe coming.

He felt it coming.

It came down across his back and shoulder, and it was a big axe and a strong arm, and it went through the chain shirt like the chain was cloth. Pain, white and enormous. He felt himself go to one knee and come up again. And he felt — he could not have said how — something go *out* of him through the wound. Not blood. Something that went into the axe and up the haft into the thing holding it.

He saw the knife-wound in its neck close.

He saw the gash Nageru had opened in its belly pull together at the edges like a mouth shutting. He saw it stand up straighter. It had been hurt, and it had cut him, and now it was less hurt, and he understood that as clearly as he had ever understood anything: *it eats when it cuts.*

He looked at it.

He was angry. He noticed that the way you notice a fire has caught in the next room — a sound, a smell, a heat on the side of your face. He let it catch.

He took one step. Not back. *Sideways*, and forward, so that when he finished the step the demon was between him and the wall of the crypt and there was nowhere left for it to go that was not through him. A doorway. He had made himself a doorway, with the thing on the wrong side of it.

Then he let the knives go.

Mother's blade, low. The punch dagger, straight in. He did not count them. He did not have to count. He had been doing this since he was eleven years old in a camp yard with a sergeant whose name he could not remember, and his hands knew where the seams were in anything that walked. Into the joint under the arm. Into the hinge of the jaw. Into the soft place below the ribs where the long body narrowed. Three times the steel went in and found demon, and three times it came out black.

The demon's mouth opened wide and stayed open, and in the silence it was the most terrible thing Thane had ever not heard.

And then Nageru was there.

Thane had not seen him cross the room. He never did. He was simply beside the thing, and his fist went into it once — and twice —

And the demon came apart.

Not like the drocha, thinning into air. Like a rotten log does when you put your boot through it: all at once, inward, into a stink and a black slurry and then not even that. There was a smear on the wall where it had stood. There was Thane's wrist-knife on the floor, where it had fallen out of nothing.

Thane stood there in the silence with his two blades in his hands and his back on fire, breathing, and could not hear himself breathe.

He bent and picked up the third.

Then he wiped them. All of them. Both faces. In the silence the rag made no sound at all on the steel, and he found, to his surprise, that he missed it.

---

The silence lasted a long time.

It was strange, being in it with the other three. Korroc came and put a hand on his back, flat, over the axe-wound, and Thane felt the warmth go into him and the wound close under it — but there were no words with it, no *there, cousin* or *hold still*, only the hand and the warmth and then the hand taking itself away. Korroc stood back and touched his own neck where the scratch was, and looked at the blood on his fingers, and wiped them on his surcoat, and that was apparently all the attention he intended to give it.

Nageru stood at the place where the demon had come apart, looking at the smear on the wall, with his hands at his sides.

And Caleth walked slowly along the north wall, from one sarcophagus to the next, and looked into each of them.

Thane did not need to. He had read the room from the doorway. Each of the three great stone boxes had been opened and emptied, and the bones in them had been taken out and added to the rest, the heaps along the walls and the drift across the floor. And the five coffins in the room upstairs had been emptied, and *those* bones had been dragged down the stair and added to it too. Eight of a family, at least. Maybe more, if the stone boxes had held more than one. All of them here, in one room, gnawed and split and mixed together like kindling in a woodpile.

Caleth came back to the middle of the room and stood looking at it, and his lips moved, and nothing came out.

He stopped. Waited.

The silence went.

It did not fade. It simply was not there any more — and all at once the crypt was full of small sounds, the creak of Korroc's plate and the drip of something somewhere and four men breathing, and Thane heard himself let out a breath he had not known he was holding.

"There is no way," Caleth said, into the sound, "that we are going to be able to identify and separate these bones."

Nobody argued with him.

"Let's just collect them," he said. "Put them in a sarcophagus. All together." His mouth tightened. "Then we will go back up and talk to the specter."

So that was what they did.

It took a while. Thane did not mind the while. There was a thing he had learned in the Kenabres alleys, which was that you could tell almost everything about a man by the way he handled somebody else's dead, and he learned a good deal in that crypt about men he had thought he knew.

Korroc picked each bone up in both hands, every one, even the small ones, the way you pick up a tool that belongs to someone else.

Nageru did not hurry. He knelt in the bones and gathered them as though the gathering was itself the point, with an attention that never wandered, and when he found a skull he held it a moment in both hands and looked into its face before he set it with the others.

Caleth worked fast and very carefully, and once, when he came on a small bone that could only have come from a child, he stopped — just for a moment, kneeling, with it in his palm — and then went on.

Thane collected bones and watched them do it, and did not think about drag marks, and did not think about two dwarves out of a tunnel in the dark, and did not think about what a demon had been doing for years in a family's crypt, and failed at all three.

They put them in the center sarcophagus, the one with the woman's face on the lid. All of them, together. It was the largest. When they were done Korroc and Nageru lifted the lid between them and set it back on, and it went down with a long low grinding sound and settled, and the stone woman lay there with her hands folded on her chest over all of her family at once.

Korroc put his palm flat on the lid for a moment. He did not pray aloud. His lips moved.

Then they went up.

---

They came out into the flat white daylight blinking, Thane first, and the specter was not at the gate.

He looked for it. The fence, the gravestones, the long slope of the hill. Nothing. And then — at the bottom of the hill, where the cemetery met the village, a flicker. A shape going around the corner of a house at the end of the street. Gray. Floating. Gone.

Thane was running before he had decided to.

He heard Caleth behind him — the light quick step — and the two of them went down the slope between the stones and into the street and around the corner, and there it was again: forty yards on, going around another corner. Not hurrying. Not waiting either.

They went after it. The next corner. A narrow way between two houses, half blocked with a fallen beam. Thane went over the beam and Caleth went —

Caleth was not beside him any more.

Thane felt the small cool tug of it in the air, the fold of the world, and came out the far end of the alley to find Caleth already standing there, twenty feet ahead of him, having taken a short-cut through nowhere. Directly behind the specter.

The specter had stopped.

It had come out into a little yard behind the last houses of the village, overgrown, the ground gone to sedge, and it hung there a hand's width above the earth — over something.

Caleth spoke to it. His voice was level.

"We have cleansed the crypt. What else do you need from us?"

And he walked toward it as he spoke. And then he stopped.

Thane came up beside him and saw why.

There was a body on the ground under the specter. Or what was left of one. A skeleton, in armor — in good Mendevian plate gone brown with rust, a long surcoat rotted to rags over it, a helm still on the skull. It lay on its back in the sedge with its arms flung out, the way a man lies when he has fallen and never gotten up, and the grass had grown up through the gaps in it and out again. It had been there a very long time.

It was the same armor. The same surcoat. The same helm, the same pattern, the same notch on the left pauldron where something had struck it once and somebody had not had time to hammer it out.

The specter was hovering over its own body.

Caleth looked at it for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed. It had gone quieter.

"Does your body need to be laid to rest?"

The specter said nothing. The red lights in its helm looked down at the bones in the grass.

Nageru had arrived — Thane had not heard him come; he never did — and was standing a little apart, looking at the bones with his hands folded. Up the street behind them there was a heavy rhythmic clanking, getting slowly nearer, and a voice saying something in Dwarven about running, and specters, and the general unfairness of a world in which the dead could float and the living had to carry a hundred pounds of plate.

Thane looked at the dead crusader.

*The last of my line.* The sixth coffin, with the door left ajar and the lid set down whole, that nothing had ever been taken out of because nothing had ever been put in. A man who had come out of his family's house to fight something, and fallen in a yard behind a village, and never been carried home.

And had stayed, all this time, at the gate.

"Aye," Thane said. "We need to do that."

---

They carried him up the hill between them.

Thane had thought it might be hard. The bones had been in the ground a long time and in the weather longer, and he had seen what happened to bones like that when you lifted them wrong. But the armor held him together. They worked the whole of him onto Korroc's cloak — Korroc arriving at last, red-faced and breathing like a bellows, and taking the head end without a word — and carried him slowly, the four of them, up between the dwarven stones to the house he had built for his family.

The specter followed them.

It did not lead. It came behind, at a little distance, the way mourners walk. At the gate in the iron fence it stopped, and hung there, and did not come in. *The place forever barred to me.* It watched them carry its body through the broken doors and down the stairs, and did not follow, and Thane, glancing back from the top of the stair, saw its red eyes on them all the way down.

They took him to the sixth door. The one standing open. *Arlys*, on the plaque.

There was nothing in that coffin but dust, and they laid him in it. Armor and all. It was a tight fit; the coffin had been cut for a man and not for a man in plate, and it took Korroc and Nageru together to settle him, slowly, a hand under the helm, a hand under the knees. When he was in, Korroc stood over him.

He spoke to Torag. Thane had heard him do it over a hundred dead in a year, crusaders and refugees and once, in Drezen, six things in cages that had been men. He did it the same way every time. Old Dwarven first, the words that were older than any of them, and then the same again in Common, so that whoever was listening would understand it.

*A man is owed the words,* Korroc had said, once, when Thane had asked him why he bothered with the second part. *The Father does not check a name at the door.*

When he was done they took up the lid from where it had been set down, whole, all those years ago, and they put it on.

Thane watched it close. *Somebody opened this door from the inside,* he thought. *And then went out to fight. And meant to come back.*

He was very glad of the dark in the crypt, for a moment, and then he was not, and they went up.

The specter was still at the gate.

But it had changed.

It took Thane a moment to see how. It was the same shape, the same armor, floating at the same height. But it was *lighter*. It had been faint before; now it was fainter, and the faintness had a different quality to it, like the difference between a candle seen through fog and a candle burning down. The red in its eyes had dimmed. Not gone. Dimmed. As though the thing that had held it here had begun, at last, to let go.

Thane walked up to it.

He had not planned to. He had planned, on the stairs, to say nothing; to let Caleth or Korroc say whatever was said to a dead man who has been given back his bed. But his feet took him up to the iron fence and stopped him in front of it, in the gateway, close enough to feel the cold coming off the thing, and his mouth opened before he had decided what would come out of it.

"You have been here," he said, "for many years."

The red eyes turned to him.

"Have you seen dwarves pass through here?"

He heard Korroc stop moving behind him. He heard it the way you hear a cart stop on a road.

The specter looked at him. For a long moment it said nothing, and Thane felt the cold of it on his face, and waited, and kept his hands still at his sides by an act of will so complete that it hurt.

"You mean," the specter said, "the ones that came with the group? Years ago?"

Thane could not speak.

The specter turned away from him. It drifted off from the gate — not toward the village, but sideways, along the outside of the iron fence, and then away up the hill between the dwarven stones. Slowly. It did not look back to see if they followed.

They followed.

It took them up past the rows, past the old square stones with their worn runes and their lichen, over the brow of the hill to the far side, where the slope ran down toward a stand of dead trees. And there, in the last row before the trees, a little apart from the rest, it stopped.

There were five graves.

They were not like the others. The others were old, and square, and cut deep by masons who had all the time in the world. These were heaps of earth gone flat with years, each with a stone at its head — not a dressed stone, not a mason's stone, but a stone off the hillside, a flat one picked for being flat, stood up on its end and wedged. Somebody had scratched a name into the face of each. Crudely. With a knife, perhaps, or the point of a tool. The letters were deep where the stone was soft and shallow where it was hard, and all of them leaned, as though the hand cutting them had been tired, or hurt, or in a hurry.

They were dwarven runes.

Thane read the first stone, and the second, and the third.

He read the fourth.

He did not read the fifth. He did not have to. He knew the shape of that name the way he knew the shape of his own hand. He had seen it on a seventy-year-old blueprint letter, among the first four names on the page, in his father's own hand. He had seen it in a prison ledger, in two clerks' handwriting, over and over. He had seen it in a crusader accounting, in a column of the dead, written by a man who went home to his supper.

He had seen it a thousand times on the inside of his own eyelids.

*THOREK*

And beside it, the next stone along, so close the two heaps of earth ran together at the edges:

*BORIN*

---

He did not know how long he stood there.

He knew there was wind. He knew it because it moved the dead grass on the graves and he watched it do it. He knew the sky went on being the color it was. He knew that somewhere beside him his cousin had stopped breathing for a while and then started again, and that the sound he made when he started was not a sound Thane had ever heard Korroc make, and he did not turn his head to look, because some things a man is owed not to be watched doing.

He looked at the stones.

The runes were bad. Whoever had cut his father's name had cut it with something that was not meant for cutting stone, and had gone over each stroke more than once, to make it deep enough. The *R* had slipped. You could see where the tool had skidded on a hard grain and gone sideways, and where the cutter had stopped, and gone back, and made it right.

He looked at that slipped stroke for a long time.

It was a year. Something over a year since a dragon fell out of the sky on Kenabres and he had first seen the initials S. V. in a letter taken off a dead woman under the city. A year of knowing, and then not knowing, and then knowing something different. *Fallen in the Fourth Crusade, body never recovered.* Then: *captured.* Then: *they dug twenty feet.* Then: *neither is on the list of the dead.* He had carried each of those around inside his chain shirt in turn, and every one of them had been a door, and he had stood in front of every one of them with his tools.

This one did not open. This one was not a door at all. It was a stone stood on end in the dirt with a name on it, and there was nothing behind it but a dwarf.

*At least,* something in him said — some small, practical, inquisitor's voice that would not stop working even now — *at least it's an answer.*

He let it say it. He found, when he opened his mouth, that it was the thing that came out.

"At least they were buried."

His voice was rough. He cleared it.

"And at least we know now."

Korroc did not answer.

Thane turned his head, then, and looked at him. His cousin was standing at the foot of the graves with Fiendsplitter hanging forgotten from one hand and the other hand open at his side, and his face was wet, all the way down into the beard, and he was not doing anything about it. He was looking at the stone that said *BORIN.* He had the look of a man listening to something a long way off.

Behind them, Caleth and Nageru had stopped a good way back, at the edge of the last proper row, and were standing there with their heads bowed. Thane had not heard them stop. He was grateful to them for it in a way he would never in his life find a way to say.

"Should we take them home with us?" he said. "Or leave them here?"

Korroc stood very still for a moment.

"We can leave them here," he said, "for now." His voice was steady. It was only a little lower than usual. "And come back later."

Thane nodded. It was the right answer. It was the answer he would have given, if he had been able to give one.

"We can ask Dagna," he said. "What she wants."

"Aye," said Korroc simply.

And then neither of them said anything, for a while.

The wind moved the grass. Thane stood at the foot of his father's grave and did not pray, because he had never been a man who prayed for comfort, only for clarity, and he did not know what he would have asked to see more clearly than this. He thought instead about the words his mother had given him — *listen first; look second; touch third* — and found that for the first time in his life he did not want to do any of them. He did not want to listen to this. He did not want to look at it. He did not want to put his hand on that stone and feel how cold it was.

He did it anyway. He knelt, and put his palm flat on the face of the stone, over the slipped *R*.

It was cold.

*Hello, Da,* he thought. And then nothing else, because there was nothing else that was the right size.

After a while, he felt a weight on his shoulder. Korroc's hand. It stayed there. Neither of them said anything about it.

---

When at last they turned, the specter was waiting for them.

It had drifted a little way down the slope, and it came back up as they turned, and stopped in front of them. It was fainter still. Thane could see the dead trees through it now, clearly, and the red in its eyes had faded almost to nothing — two embers in a grate at the end of a long night.

It spoke. Its voice had changed too. The hollowness was still there, but the edge had gone off it.

"You have done for me," it said, "what your parents could not."

*Your parents.*

Thane went still. Beside him, so did Korroc.

"They came here, a time ago," the specter said. "And in my anger, I attacked them. They defeated me. They thought me vanquished." The faint red eyes moved from one of them to the other. "Yet I was not gone. My final release was beyond their abilities."

*And now the child comes before me.*

Thane heard it again, in that terrible hollow voice at the gate, and understood it now, all at once, the way you understand a lock when the last pin sets. It had looked at Korroc — square, stubborn, braided, standing in front of everyone with his shield up — and it had seen the dwarf who had done that, here, before. It had seen the father in the son. *The child comes before me.* The son of the man who had beaten it, come back to its gate after all these years, the way it had always known someone would.

He looked at his cousin and saw Korroc arrive at the same place a moment after him.

"When I returned," the specter said, "I watched them."

Its voice slowed.

"One was injured. And succumbed to his wounds." The ember eyes went past them, to the two stones, and stayed there. "They buried him here."

Korroc made a sound. It was not much of one.

*Borin was injured.* The dead man in the tunnel. *Borin was injured,* and Thane had stepped back and given his cousin the next question, because it was Korroc's father who was hurt and the question was Korroc's to ask. And Korroc had asked the practical one, *where were they going*, the one that brought them here.

Here, where his father had come with a wound in him, and lain down, and not gotten up.

And somebody had dug the grave. Somebody had found a flat stone on the hillside and stood it on end. Somebody had cut *BORIN* into it with a tool that was not meant for stone, and gone over every stroke twice, and been tired, or hurt, or in a hurry, or all three.

Thane looked at the stone with his father's name on it and thought: *You did that. You buried your brother.*

*You buried him, and you cut his name, and then —*

"They then left," the specter said.

Thane's head came up.

"But some time later," it said, "two humans came back. And buried more here."

He looked at the five stones. The two, side by side, the earth of them running together at the edges. The three beside them that he had read and not known.

*Two humans came back and buried more here.*

His father had not died here.

His father had stood at this grave and buried his brother and cut his name in the stone, and then he had *gone on*. Left. Walked out of this graveyard and away, alive, with whoever was left of the ones who had come up the riverbed with him. And somewhere — *some time later*, how much later, a week, a year, ten — somewhere out there in that country, he had died. And two humans, two strangers, had picked him up and carried him back here. All the way back. To lay him down next to his brother.

Because somebody had known this was where he would want to be.

*Who?*

The question went through him like a blade. He felt it go in and felt it find nothing to stop against.

*Who were you? Where did he die? How far did you carry him? Why here — how did you know to bring him here — did he tell you? Did he ask? Was he still alive when you started walking?*

He opened his mouth to ask the specter. All of it, in order, the way he asked anything.

And looked at it, and saw that there was almost nothing left of it to ask. The red was nearly out of its eyes. It was dissolving at the edges as he watched, the way frost dissolves off a window when the sun gets to it. It had been waiting at a gate for the length of a dwarf's lifetime, and it was going, and it had given them what it had. It had told them *two humans*, and if it had known their names it would have said them, and if it had known where they had come from it would have said that too.

He closed his mouth.

*At least we know now.*

He had said that a little while ago, and meant it. He still meant it. He knew where his father was. He knew his father had not died in a prison in irons, or in a dark tunnel with twenty feet of stone on top of him, or alone in a dry riverbed with nobody to cut his name. He knew that his father had buried his brother with his own hands, and gone on.

It was an answer.

It was the whole of the answer. The specter had said so. *The end of it,* Thane thought, and understood that it was true, and that he was going to carry the two strangers anyway, for the rest of his life, in the place where he kept things he would take out later when nobody was about.

He knew already that he would never get to put them down.

"But you have saved me," the specter said.

It was barely there now. A shape of armor in the air, and two dim points of red, and a voice that was almost only wind.

"So allow me," it said, "to gift a token of my gratitude. Go into the village… and find the light."

And it went.

It did not fade out. It *gathered* — all that thin gray shape drawing in on itself, smaller, brighter, until it was not a man in armor at all but a single small ball of light, pale gold, hanging in the air where its heart would have been. It hung there for one heartbeat.

Then it shot away down the hill like a thrown spark, over the stones and the roofs, and was gone into the village.

The four of them stood among the graves and looked at the place where it had been.

"Arlys Harnaste," Caleth said quietly, to nobody. "Go well."

---

The light was not hard to find.

It had come down at the edge of the village, in what might once have been a kitchen garden behind one of the half-fallen houses, and it had sunk into the earth there. Not gone — *sunk*, the way water sinks into dry ground, so that a patch of the dirt the size of a door lay glowing faintly gold in the gray afternoon, as if somebody had buried a lantern a little way down.

It was a mound. Thane saw that as he came up to it. A low rounded heap, longer than it was wide, grassed over and settled with years, that nobody walking past would have looked at twice. A heap of earth behind a house. It glowed.

He looked at it. Then he looked at his cousin, who still had Fiendsplitter hanging from one fist.

"Give me that axe."

Korroc looked at him.

It was a long look. It went from Thane's face to the mound and back to Thane's face, and somewhere along the way it acquired a squint, the squint of a smith being asked to lend his best chisel to somebody who wants to open a paint pot with it. Thane could practically hear Fiendsplitter's opinion, through the wall, on the subject of being used to *dig*.

Korroc did not say anything. He lifted his free hand and pointed.

Against the back wall of the nearest house, propped where somebody had left it forty years ago and never come back for it, there was a shovel.

Thane looked at the shovel.

He muttered something in Dwarven that he was fairly sure Caleth understood every word of, and went and got it.

The haft was half rotten and the blade was rust, but it was a shovel, and the dirt went easily — loose and soft and dry, the kind of dirt that had been dug once already and never properly settled. He dug. The others stood and watched him, which he did not appreciate, and nobody offered to help, which he appreciated even less, and he dug anyway, and it was strange: he found that he did not mind. It was a thing to do with his hands. It was a thing with an end to it. *Dig until you hit something.* After the graves, it was almost restful.

About three feet down, the blade struck something solid with a dull wooden *thunk*.

Thane scraped the earth off it. Wood. A lid. Iron bands.

Nageru dropped into the hole beside him without being asked, lightly, the way he did everything, and between them they got their fingers under the edges and heaved, and the chest came up out of the ground in a shower of dirt and they set it on the grass.

It was not large. A strongbox, really. Good oak gone dark, iron-bound, with a lock plate on the front.

Thane got down on his knees in front of it.

*Listen first.* He put his ear near the plate and tapped the lid with one knuckle and listened to how the sound went. Solid; full; no hollow false bottom, no rattle of a loose spring. *Look second.* The keyhole. Old. Unforced. No scratches around it, no other man's pick-marks — nobody had tried this before him. *Touch third.* The edges of the lid, the hinges, the bands, feeling for the little wrongness of a wire or a needle or a pressure-plate. There was nothing he could feel.

There was something, though. He could not have said what. A warmth in the wood, faint, like a stone that has been in the sun. He glanced up at Caleth, and Caleth was frowning at the box with his head on one side, the look he wore when there was magic in front of him and he was trying to read it — and then Caleth shook his head, slightly, a man admitting he could not make the shape of it out.

Thane looked back at the lock.

It had come from a dead man who had been grateful. That was the whole of what he knew about it. He found that it was enough.

He brought out his picks.

It was an old lock and a good one, and it did not take long. *Click.*

And as he lifted the lid, light came up out of the chest.

It came up over his hands and his arms and wrapped around him. All of him, all at once, like a cloak thrown over his shoulders — gold, and warm, and so bright for an instant that he shut his eyes against it. He felt it go over his skin and *look* at him. That was the only word for it. Something looked at him, all the way down, the way he looked at a lock.

And whatever it was looking for, it did not find.

It let go. The light was gone. He was kneeling in the dirt with the lid of a chest in his hands, and he was exactly as he had been a moment before, and his heart was going very fast.

"What," said Korroc, "was *that*?"

"I don't know." Thane looked down at himself. Arms, hands, chest. Nothing. He looked at Caleth. "Do you know?"

Caleth was still frowning at the place in the air where the light had been. "No," he admitted. "I don't."

Thane considered that. A light that wrapped around a man and looked at him and then went away, in a box left by a dead crusader of Iomedae, in a graveyard with a demon in it.

"Well," he said. "It didn't do anything."

"To *you*," said Caleth, slowly, in the voice of a man doing a sum, and then visibly decided not to finish it.

Thane looked into the chest.

It was not much to look at. A few bundles in oiled cloth gone stiff with age. But when he unwrapped the first of them, the thing inside caught the gray light and gave back more than it was given, and he knew what he was looking at, the way you know good steel by the sound it makes. Magic. Old, and good, and well made, the kind of thing a family puts by against the day the family needs it, and never gets to use.

Korroc took a ring. He turned it over in his thick fingers a long while first and looked at the inside of the band, a smith reading another smith's work, and then put it on, and flexed his hand, and grunted with something like approval.

Caleth took a horn — a hunting horn, or a war horn, bound in old metal — and held it up to the light and looked at the bands around it, and his eyebrows went up a fraction and stayed there, and he put it away without blowing it.

There were arrows, a bundle of them, fletched and bright-headed. Thane took some, for the shortbow he despised. To his surprise, Caleth took some too.

"You have a bow?" Thane said.

"I have *had* a bow." Caleth tucked the arrows away with the air of a man daring anyone to comment. "I may have one again."

Nobody commented. Thane had a dim memory of the half-elf with a bow in his hands, early on, in the first bad weeks after Kenabres, before he had found out what he could do with a sword and a spell and a fold in the world. He did not say anything about it, and put it in the ledger of things that were Caleth's business.

The last bundle was boots.

A pair of them, low and close-made, in a soft dark leather that had no business lasting however many years it had lain in the ground, and had lasted anyway. He turned one over. The sole was barely worn. Somebody's best pair, put by for a better day. They were not dwarf-made, and they were not made for a dwarf; they were too long in the foot and too narrow, and he very nearly handed them to Nageru.

Then he held one against his own boot, and it was not too long. He would have sworn it had been, a breath ago.

He looked at it a while.

Then he sat down on the edge of the chest and pulled his own boots off — Korroc made a noise; Thane ignored it — and pulled the new ones on. They went on the way a glove goes on a hand it was cut for. He stood up.

Nothing. Leather, and his own weight, and the ground.

He took a step, and his foot came down a good hand's width further on than he had sent it.

He stood very still. Took another. The same. It was not that the boots pulled him; nothing pulled. It was only that the ground seemed to pass under him more willingly than it ever had, as though it had decided, for once, to meet him partway. He walked a slow circle around the chest and every stride of it was a little too long, and by the end of the circle his body had stopped arguing with it.

He thought of that morning. The specter going around the corner, and the next corner, and Thane running his hardest down that rubbled street with his short legs pumping — and coming out the far end of the alley to find Caleth already there, twenty feet ahead of him, having not bothered with the street at all. Every footrace he had ever run with a human or an elf had ended the same way, all his life. He had stopped minding it a long time ago, the way you stop minding the weather.

He looked at the hole he had dug. Three feet deep, and a good stride across, with soft loose dirt at the lip.

He backed up two paces and went at it and jumped.

He had meant to clear it. He cleared it, and the dirt beyond it, and a fair stretch of grass beyond that, and landed light and balanced in a crouch with his hands not even needing to touch down — a long way further from the hole than a dwarf with a full kit on his back had any right to be. He stayed in the crouch a moment and looked back at the distance, measuring it.

When he looked up, all three of them were watching him. Nageru was smiling.

"Well," said Korroc. "You'll want to keep those."

Thane straightened up and brushed off his knees. He did not smile. He was aware that the corner of his mouth wanted to, and he did not let it.

"Somebody in this family," he said, "should be able to keep up."

Korroc, who had come up the street at the end of that chase a long minute behind everyone else, clanking, and complaining in Dwarven about plate, opened his mouth. Then he shut it again.

He closed the chest.

He looked at the hole in the earth for a moment, and at the shovel, and the house, and the village beyond it, where a crusader named Arlys Harnaste had buried his family's treasure in a garden behind a house and gone out to fight something, and not come back.

*Gift a token of my gratitude.*

He thought: *you were grateful to us for doing what you could not do for yourself.* And thought, before he could stop it, of two strangers on a road with a dwarf's body between them, carrying it a long way. To a place they had decided he would want to be.

He did not know if the dead could be grateful. He found that he hoped so.

---

They spent the afternoon in Sesker's Gully.

There was no reason to. There was nothing more they had come for. But nobody suggested leaving, and Thane understood that nobody wanted to be the one to suggest it, and that his cousin in particular was not going to be moved off that hill until something moved him. So they walked. Separately, mostly, the way men do when they have been through a thing together and need to be apart for a while without being far.

Thane walked the village.

He told himself he was looking for the waystation, and he was. He found it — the long dwarven foundation under three of the human houses, the old hearth stones, the trough for watering animals cut from a single block. *A crypt with supplies,* the dead man in the tunnel had said, and Thane had thought *crypt* meant something under the ground. But that was the escapees' word, he supposed, for what Dagna had called *a graveyard with a waystation on it*. Men running in the dark do not get their nouns right. And by the time they came here, there would have been nothing left of it but stone — no food, no water, no roof. The village would already have been empty.

They had come here anyway. They had come here because it was the plan, and his father was not a man who changed a plan because it had stopped making sense.

He walked the street and read it. He found nothing. He had not expected to. Forty years of wind, and whatever had come through the village looking for what was left. If his father had cut a mark into a doorframe here, or left a sign under a stone, the stone had long since been turned over by something else.

He walked it anyway.

He came back toward the square in the late afternoon. Korroc was sitting on the edge of the old dwarven well-head with his elbows on his knees and Fiendsplitter across his lap, looking at nothing, and Thane did not go to him. Nageru was standing at the corner of the street a little way off, still as a post, the way he stood when he was not resting so much as listening. And Caleth —

Caleth had stopped.

He was in the middle of the square, by the well, and he had one hand up on his shoulder. His left shoulder. Not the coat; the shoulder itself, up behind it, his fingers spread over the blade of it as though something had struck him there.

He was not moving. His face was empty. His eyes were open, and looking at nothing, and there was nothing in them at all.

Thane had seen men look like that. On a battlefield, a heartbeat after a blow to the head. And once in a tomb under Drezen, on a priest whose heels were arguing with his knees.

"Caleth?"

Nothing. Not a flicker. Thane was moving before he had decided to, and so was Nageru, from the other side.

"My friend," Nageru said, coming up to him. "Are you all right?"

Nothing.

It was perhaps a second. Perhaps two. It was long enough for Thane to get within arm's reach and see that the half-elf was not breathing, quite — was caught in the middle of a breath, the way a man is caught in the middle of a word when somebody walks in. Long enough for something cold to start in the bottom of Thane's stomach.

Nageru reached out and laid his hand on Caleth's other shoulder.

Caleth started. All of him, hard, like a man woken by a hand on him in the dark. He looked at Nageru — *looked at* him, as if he had not known Nageru was there, as if the aasimar had appeared out of the air — and his hand was still clamped on his own shoulder, and Thane saw the knuckles of it go white and then, slowly, ease.

"Are you well?" said Nageru, gently.

Caleth did not answer at once. He took his hand off his shoulder and looked at it, at the palm, as though he expected to find something on it. Then he let it fall.

"Whatever took Aravashnial," he said, "has been watching me."

Thane went very still.

Nageru considered this, gravely, for a moment.

"Well," he said. "That is unpleasant."

It was so exactly Nageru — so mild, so precise, so wholly inadequate to the size of what had just been said and so wholly aware of it — that Thane felt a laugh try to come up out of his chest, and did not let it. Caleth's mouth moved too, the very corner of it. It was not a smile. It was the shape a smile would have made if there had been room for one.

He nodded.

"Yes," he said. "It is."

He reached into his coat — the right side, not the left — and brought out a wand, a thin dark thing Thane had seen him carry and never seen him use. He turned it over in his fingers.

"I have this," he said. "It helps to prevent detection. It probably won't help." He looked at it with something that was not quite contempt. "But it is something."

And he touched it to his own chest, and spoke a word, and something passed over him like a cloud over a field — Thane felt it rather than saw it, a sense of the half-elf going briefly *harder to look at* — and then it was done, and he put the wand away.

Korroc had come up from the well without any of them noticing, and was standing at Thane's elbow, and his face was doing a thing Thane did not like.

"*Watching* you," he said.

"Yes."

"Watching you how?"

Caleth looked at him. It was a long look, and Thane could not read it, and that in itself was information — Caleth's face was very hard to read when he wanted it to be, and he wanted it to be now.

"I don't know," he said. And then, after a moment, as if it cost him: "I am trying to find out."

Korroc held his eyes for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, once, the nod of a man who has decided to take a thing on credit.

They did not talk about it again that afternoon. But Thane walked the last of the village with the other three in his eye-line, not out of it, and he saw that Korroc did the same, and Nageru, and he did not think any of them had decided to.

---

They made camp for the night at the edge of the village, on the old waystation's foundations, with a wall of good dwarven stone at their backs.

Caleth did something before they settled. He stood in the middle of the four of them and spoke a long working with his eyes shut, and when it was done Thane felt it go into him — a strange sensation, like the first breath of morning air after a long bad night — and understood, from what the half-elf said after, that none of them would need to sleep. They would sit up through the dark and rest in their bodies, and in the morning they would be as fresh as if they had slept the night through. *It's not a substitute for sleep,* Caleth said. *It is a forgery of it. A very good one.*

Thane liked that. A night with all four of them awake, in a dead village in the Worldwound, a day's ride from anywhere, was the best kind of night he could think of. He took a place with his back to the wall and his knives where his hands knew them, and settled down to watch the dark.

Caleth dozed anyway.

Thane saw him go. The half-elf had sat down with his back to the old stone and his coat wrapped round him and his hand, as always, on the left side of it, and sometime in the black middle of the night his head went down on his chest and stayed there. *Five days of not sleeping,* Thane thought, *and a spell that tells your body you have. The body only believes so much.* He thought about waking him and decided not to. Let him have it. Of all of them, he was the one who needed it.

He was wrong.

He knew it the moment it started, because it started in Caleth's breathing. A hitch. Then another. Then the breath going fast and shallow and wrong, and the half-elf's hand closing on his own shoulder through the coat, harder and harder, the knuckles standing out in the light of the fire, and his head turning, side to side, slowly, the way a man's does who is looking for something in the dark and cannot find it —

And then he screamed.

Thane had heard a lot of screaming. On a battlefield it all sounds the same after a while. This did not sound like that. It was not a sound of hurt; or not only. It was the sound of a man who has looked down and seen something he cannot bear to see, and it came up out of Caleth from somewhere very deep and went out across the dead village and came back off the walls, and went on, and went on.

Nageru was there first. Thane saw him go and did not see him cross the space — he was at the fire and then he was on his knees beside Caleth with a hand on his shoulder, the other shoulder, the right one, the way he had done that afternoon.

Thane was there second. Korroc third.

Caleth's eyes were open. They had come open in the middle of the scream and the scream had stopped and he was sitting bolt upright against the wall with his teeth clenched and his whole body rigid, and his hand was locked over his left shoulder so hard that Thane could see it shaking.

"Caleth," Korroc said. "Lad."

Caleth did not answer. He was breathing — in, and in, and in, too fast, through his teeth — and his face was white, and slick, and his eyes were fixed on nothing. And Thane, kneeling at his feet, watched his face and counted. He did not know why he counted. It was something to do with his mind while his body had nothing.

He got to something past fifty before Caleth's shoulders came down. Not all at once. By degrees. Like a weight being lowered off a man an inch at a time.

At last the half-elf shut his eyes, and opened them, and looked at Nageru, who had not moved and had not taken his hand away.

He shook his head.

"Same thing," he said.

His voice was rough as a rasp.

Thane rocked back on his heels. He looked at Caleth, at the hand still on the shoulder, at the face that was trying, now, to be a face again and not managing it. *Same thing.* Same as what? The afternoon, in the square. And the night before, on the riverbank, when he had woken up with his hand clamped on that same shoulder and told them all that Aravashnial was trying to send him a message. And how many other nights that none of them had seen?

He turned his head to his cousin. He kept his voice low; Dwarven, out of habit, though Caleth would understand it perfectly well, and Thane knew it, and did not care.

"He's breaking."

Korroc snorted.

It was not agreement and it was not denial. It was a sound Thane had known all his life, the sound his cousin made when somebody said a true thing he did not intend to let stand. Then he turned, and put his whole attention on Caleth, square, the way he put it on a wall he was about to work on.

"He's still watching?" he said. "Whatever this is. It's still watching you."

Caleth looked at him.

There was a long moment then, by the fire, in which Thane watched the half-elf decide something. He had seen it before, on other men. On himself, in a mirror, once or twice. It was the face of a man standing in front of a door he has kept shut a very long time, with his hand on the latch, finding that the hand has made up its mind before the man has.

"It has something to do with my birthmark," Caleth said.

Korroc frowned. "Your *birthmark?*"

Caleth did not answer him. He reached up, and undid the clasp of his coat, and shrugged it off his shoulders. Then the crusader's tabard over his head. Then, after one moment — one moment of nothing at all, in which his fingers rested on the hem of his shirt and did not move — he pulled the shirt up and off, and turned in the firelight, and showed them his back.

Thane looked.

It was on the left shoulder blade. Exactly where the hand had been. All afternoon, all evening, all the ride north — *always the left side, always the same place.* Not on the coat. Under it.

A mark. A spiral, curling out from a center, three turns or four, a hand's breadth across. It was not ink. It was not a scar. It was a *paleness* — a place where the skin was lighter than the skin around it, as though somebody had drawn the shape there with a lighter color than the rest of him, so long ago that it had grown along with him. The edges were clean. The curve was perfect. It was very beautiful, in the way a thing is beautiful when it has been made exactly right, and Thane did not like it at all.

Nobody spoke.

"I have had this birthmark all my life," Caleth said. He was speaking to the dark in front of him, with his back to them, and his voice was very steady and very quiet. "I have been told it is the mark of the Seeker's Spiral."

*I have been told.* Thane heard the shape of it. Not *it is*. Not *my people call it*. *I have been told*, the way you say a thing you got secondhand and do not entirely trust, from somebody you have not decided to name.

He let it go by. He noted it, and let it go by, because a man with his shirt off in front of a fire showing three friends a thing he has hidden for a year has paid enough for one night, and you do not go through his pockets as well.

"I don't know what that means," Caleth said. "But it seems as if Vang is finding me through it."

*Vang.* He had said that name once before, in the command room in Drezen, in a voice like struck flint, and walked out of the room. Thane had not heard him say it since. He heard it now, and saw the muscles in the half-elf's back go tight across his ribs as he said it, and loosen again.

Korroc leaned in.

He did it the way he looked at anything — close, slow, frowning, a craftsman looking at another craftsman's work for the flaw. He looked at the mark for a long time. He did not touch it. Thane saw him want to, and saw him not.

Thane looked too, past his cousin's shoulder. He looked at it the way he would have looked at a lock: *what is this, who made it, has it been forced.* And it told him nothing. It was a mark on a man's back. The skin around it was ordinary skin, gooseflesh coming up on it in the cold. There was no heat coming off it that he could see. No glow. No rawness, no burn, no blistering — nothing at all to say that a moment ago a man had been screaming over it as if it were a brand.

It was a spiral on a shoulder. It was a birthmark.

Korroc sat back on his heels, and said nothing at all.

Caleth pulled his shirt back on. He did it quickly, the way a man dresses who has been cold for a long time. Then the tabard. Then the coat, and his hand went to the left side of it, inside, and stayed there. He did not turn around for a moment longer.

When he did, his face was composed again. Almost.

Nobody asked him anything else that night. Thane saw Korroc want to, three or four times, and saw him not. Nageru sat down beside the half-elf with his shoulder an inch from Caleth's shoulder, not touching, and stayed there till morning, and did not speak, and did not move.

And Thane went back to his place against the wall and watched the dark, and found that he was not watching the dark at all.

*I have been told.*

He had kept a thing himself, since Drezen. He had kept it the way you keep a knife in your boot: so that nobody would know it was there, so that nobody would ask, so that he would never once have to stand in front of anybody and take it out. He knew exactly what it had cost the half-elf to pull that shirt off. He knew almost to the coin.

He knew, too, that Caleth had only taken out *half*. He had seen the hand stop on the latch.

*None of my business,* Thane told himself, in his mother's voice, the voice for things you did not touch until you had listened and looked. *Not yet.*

He kept watching the half-elf till dawn anyway.

---

In the morning they rode south.

Korroc went up to the graves first, alone, before the horses were saddled, and Thane let him. He came back down the hill some while later with nothing in his face at all and said "Right," and climbed onto his horse with only a moderate amount of help and a fairly short speech, and that was all.

Thane did not go up. He had said what there was to say, with his hand on the stone. He found he did not want to say it again in daylight, with the horses stamping.

They followed the riverbed back the way they had come. It was the same riverbed. It looked different. Thane rode along it and did not read it any more, because there was nothing on it now that he needed to find; and he discovered, somewhere around the third hour, that he did not know what to do with his eyes when they were not looking for something.

*Two humans came back.*

He put it away. It came back out. He put it away again.

It was a little past midday when Korroc reined in.

He did not do it well. He did it the way he did everything on a horse, which was suddenly and with a great deal of unnecessary rein, so that the animal threw its head and danced sideways and Korroc said something to it in Dwarven that would have got a younger dwarf clouted. But when he had it stopped he did not go on swearing. He sat very still, with his head a little on one side.

"What," said Thane.

Korroc's face was doing a thing it had started doing in the last day and a half, which was listening to somebody nobody else could hear and not enjoying it.

"Something up ahead," he said. "On the road." His jaw worked. "The axe thinks it's a demon."

Thane looked. Far ahead, where the riverbed ran straight for a mile between low banks, there was something small and dark in the middle of the way. Moving. Not coming toward them. Going round and round, in a little circle, the way a dog goes round before it lies down.

Korroc sighed. It was the deep long-suffering sigh of a man who had been a smith for thirty years and was now, against his will, in a working relationship with a tool that had opinions.

He listened again. His lips thinned.

"It *is* a demon," he said, in the flat voice of a man reporting what he has been told rather than what he believes, "let's go."

Then, in his own voice, resigned: "It is a demon."

They got down off the horses and went forward on foot.

It was a babau. Thane knew it at forty yards and was sure of it at twenty: the long gangling limbs, the slick black hide like something that has been dipped in tar, the single horn curving back off the long skull. He had seen a room full of them in a garrison in Kenabres, and more on the walls of Drezen, and he knew what they did and how fast they did it and where they liked to put their claws.

This one was not doing any of it.

It was walking round in its little circle in the middle of the dry riverbed, round and round, and it was *talking*. Not to them; it had not seen them. To itself. A thin, wheedling, continuous stream of Abyssal, rising and falling, and every so often it stopped and lifted something up in front of its face in both clawed hands and *shouted* at it.

The four of them stopped about fifty feet off. The babau went round again, shouting.

It still had not seen them.

Thane looked at Korroc. Korroc looked at the sky. Nageru had one eyebrow raised a fraction of an inch, which on Nageru was the equivalent of anyone else throwing up both hands.

And Caleth — Caleth was looking at the thing in the babau's hands.

Thane saw him see it. He saw the half-elf's eyes narrow and go very sharp and very still, the way they went in a library, the way they went over a page with a word on it he wanted. Whatever the babau was holding, Caleth had decided that he wanted to know what it was, and when Caleth decided he wanted to know something, he was not a safe man to stand next to.

The half-elf took a step forward. He put on a face. Thane had seen the face before — in Kenabres, on a fishmonger, holding out a scrap of something to a stray cat under a cart.

"*What have you got there, little one?*" said Caleth, warmly, in Common, as one might speak to a dog.

The babau froze.

It turned. Very slowly, the way a thief turns when he hears a voice behind him in a house he thought was empty. Its eyes found the four of them. It stood there in the middle of the riverbed with the thing clutched to its chest in both claws and stared.

Caleth smiled at it.

He crouched down a little. He held out one hand, palm up. And he said, in the same bright coaxing voice, exactly as if he were calling a hound in out of the rain:

"*Come here, then.*"

Thane looked at the half-elf.

He looked at him — the Spireborn, the Knight of the Fifth Crusade, the man who had, that same night, been screaming in his sleep over a mark on his back — crouched in a riverbed in the Worldwound, making encouraging noises at a demon. A babau. A creature that had very probably eaten somebody's children.

He felt it come up out of his chest, and this time he could not stop it.

He laughed. It came out of him like a cork out of a bottle, loud, helpless, and he could not get it back in. He put a hand over his face and it did not help. Korroc turned and stared at him. Nageru turned and stared at him. The babau turned and stared at him. Thane bent over and put his hands on his knees and laughed until his eyes ran, and could not have said, for the life of him, how much of it was about the demon.

Korroc shook his head, slowly, and said something under his breath, and Thane could not tell whether it was to the axe or to the gods.

Caleth did not so much as look round. He was watching the babau.

And the babau was coming.

It came forward a step. Then another. It was talking again, faster now, in Abyssal — and this time it was talking *to* Caleth, and Caleth was answering. Thane did not have a word of the language. He did not need one. He had spent two years in the back alleys of Kenabres watching men try to sell things they should not have had to men who should not have wanted them, and he knew a desperate seller when he saw one.

It held the thing out.

It was a rod. Long as a forearm, pale — ivory, it looked like, carved all over, with something gold glinting along it. The babau held it out at arm's length toward Caleth, and the arm was *shaking*, as though the rod weighed more than the arm could hold.

It stopped, fifty feet short. It thrust the rod out toward them, and said something, pleading.

Caleth answered. He took a slow step forward.

The babau took a step back. It snatched the rod back to its chest. It muttered — to itself, now, Thane could tell, head down, eyes darting. Then a step forward again, holding it out. Then two steps back.

It was arguing with itself. Thane had seen men do it, on the edge of a deal they would regret. *Take it. No. Take it.* He watched Caleth watch it, and saw the half-elf's eyes go to the rod, every time it was held out — measuring, reading — and come back to the demon's face, and go back to the rod.

Caleth crouched down further. He said something else, quieter, with a question in it. The babau shook its long head violently, side to side, again and again, and its voice went up into a whine.

Then it seemed to forget them altogether. It turned the rod round in its claws and began to shout at it again, the same as before, shaking it, and the rod did not answer, and the babau shouted louder.

Korroc had his free hand up against the side of his own head, as if against a draft.

"*It's very loud,*" he said, to nobody, in the voice of a man at the end of a long rope.

Caleth stood up.

He looked at the babau for a moment more. Thane watched the warmth go out of his face, all at once, the way a lamp goes out when you close the shutter on it — and what was left behind was not anger. It was a decision, taken, and filed.

He sighed, and turned his head, just a little.

"Nageru?"

He pointed at the babau.

Nageru went.

Korroc flinched as if somebody had bellowed in his ear. "*No, no, it wants to do it,*" he reported through his teeth, "*it wants —* oh, *shut up*," with real feeling, and then Nageru was across the fifty feet and the babau looked up from its argument with the rod and saw him coming and did not have time to do anything about it. One blow. Downward. Like a hammer onto an anvil.

It went down in the riverbed and did not get up. Then it was not there at all — not dead, not the way a man is dead, only sent home, the way it always went with demons. Back to its own country, whole, to be somebody else's trouble. 

The axe went on complaining for some time. Thane could tell, because Korroc's face went on doing the thing.

Caleth walked forward. He knelt beside the rod lying in the dirt for a long time, with his hands on his knees, and Thane saw his lips move, and saw the small change in the air around the half-elf that meant he had looked at the thing with more than his eyes.

He went very still.

"That," he said, "is a great deal of magic."

He reached out, carefully, and picked it up.

Thane heard it from where he stood: a faint dry rattle, from inside the rod, like a seed in a gourd. And he saw the half-elf's eyes go wide. Not slowly — all at once, like a door blown open, the way they had gone wide over a letter on the parapet in Drezen, five days ago.

"Oh," Caleth said.

He looked up at them. He was holding the rod across both palms the way Korroc held a newly forged blade.

"Just from picking it up," he said, "I know what this is." He swallowed. "This is the Pauper's Thighbone."

They looked at him.

"It's an artifact," said Caleth.

---

He spent the rest of the ride to Drezen studying it.

He rode with the reins slack on the horse's neck and the rod across his knees, turning it in his hands, running a finger along the golden marks on it one after another, his lips moving. Thane rode beside him for a while and watched. It was ivory. A long rod of it, yellowed, carved all over with very fine work — and set along it, at intervals, nine runes in gold. Real gold, Thane thought, inlaid. Bright.

"Well," Thane said at last. "Go on, then."

Caleth looked up. For a moment his eyes did not quite find him; they were somewhere else.

"It is a reliquary," he said. "There is a bone in it. A thighbone, broken. It belonged to a saint, a man called Argil — I do not know when; a long time ago. He gave everything he had. Everything, always. They say he never once in his life passed by a person in need." He turned the rod over. "The bone is sealed inside. It cannot be opened, except in the course of destroying it."

"And the runes?"

"Nine." Caleth touched them. "They feed a spell. Make it more than it would be, for nothing — no more time to cast, no more effort. Each one spent goes dull. They come back with the dawn." His thumb rested on the last. "And if the one carrying it passes up the chance to do a generous thing — deliberately, knowing — one of the runes goes dark and does not come back. Ever."

Thane looked at the rod.

"And if he gives a great deal away," Caleth said, "a new one may come."

"A rod that keeps a ledger of how generous you are."

"Yes."

"Gods help us." Thane looked at it again. "And the demon?"

"In an evil hand it does nothing." Caleth's voice was very level. "Nothing at all. It is only weight. Heavy as a stone, heavier every day, and it *cannot be put down*. Not dropped, not thrown away, not left. The only way it leaves an evil hand is if the hand dies — or if he gives it, willingly, to someone who is not evil, and they take it from him willingly."

Thane was quiet for a moment. He thought about the babau's arms, shaking. Stepping forward, and back. Holding the thing out at full stretch to four men it should have tried to kill on sight.

"That's what it was saying," he said. "*Take it.*"

"*They'll take the thing,*" said Caleth. "*They'll get rid of it. Won't you get rid of it.* It had heard somewhere that we would." His mouth did something. "*It's powerful. It can make me strong. But it doesn't make me strong. And then it bites.*"

"It wanted you to take it off him."

"Yes."

"And you didn't."

Caleth looked at the rod for a long moment.

"I wanted to see what it was first," he said. "Whether it was cursed. I could not tell, from fifty feet, with it waving the thing about. And I wanted to know where it had come from, and it would not tell me that, or its name, or anything of use." His hand closed on the ivory. "And then I had seen enough. And it was a demon."

He said it flatly, the way you report a measurement. Thane heard nothing in it at all. He looked across at the half-elf and found him looking at the gold runes along the rod, and at the last of them in particular, the one his thumb had rested on — *if the one carrying it passes up the chance to do a generous thing* — and there was something in Caleth's face for a moment then that Thane did not have a word for. It was not guilt. It was more like a man reading a letter in his own hand that he does not remember writing.

Then it was gone, and Caleth was studying the runes again.

"You'll want to be careful with that," Thane said.

"Yes," said Caleth. "I rather think I will."

---

They came in under Drezen's walls at evening, with the sun going down red behind them over the Worldwound, and there were men on the gate who knew their faces and called out. Thane did not hear what they called.

He and Korroc went to find Dagna.

They did not discuss it. They handed their horses to the first soldier who would take them and went off across the court side by side without a word, and Thane found that his feet knew where she would be — not the command room, not her quarters, but out in the citadel's broken east range where the masons were working, in the last of the light, with a mallet in her hand.

She saw them coming from across the yard. She put the mallet down.

She did not ask. She looked at them both, at their faces, one and then the other, and she wiped her hands on her apron very slowly and carefully, every finger, the way a mason does before she picks up something she does not want to mark. Then she sat down on a block of dressed stone and folded her hands in her lap and waited.

Korroc told her.

He told it the way he told anything that mattered, which was straight. The village. The cemetery. The specter at its gate. The five graves on the far side of the hill, and the two stones side by side. What the specter said. *One was injured, and succumbed to his wounds.* He had to stop there, once, and breathe, and go on. *They buried him here.*

Thane told the rest. *They then left.* And: *two humans came back, and buried more.*

Dagna sat on her stone and listened, and her face did not move at all.

Thane had watched her face on the walk back from the drakes, two days ago, when he told her that the fathers had not died in the Fourth Crusade — had watched it blow open like a door in a wind, the whole of it, for one instant, and then watched her catch the latch. She did not have to catch anything now. There was nothing to catch it against. She had braced him herself, on that walk. *If they were alive, they'd have tried to come home to us.*

They had. Both of them. Borin as far as a hill a day north of the house he had built. Thorek further, and farther, and then not at all.

She sat for a long time. The masons along the wall had stopped working and gone away, quietly, without anybody telling them to. The light went from red to gray.

At last she reached out, without looking, and took Korroc's hand in one of hers, and with the other found Thane's wrist and held it. Her grip was a mason's grip. It hurt a little.

"Well," she said. Her voice was perfectly steady. "Now we know."

Neither of them said anything.

"I won't ask you to take me," she said. "Not right away. You've only just come back, and you look like the pair of you were dragged behind those horses rather than sat on them." She was quiet a moment. "But soon."

"Aye, Ma," said Korroc.

They talked for a while longer, the three of them, on the stone in the dusk. Thane did not remember afterward much of what was said. Small things, mostly. Dagna asked what the stones were cut with and Thane told her he did not know, a knife perhaps, a chisel if they had one; and she said *a chisel, if it was Thorek, he'd not have cut his brother's name with a knife if there was a chisel to be had in all the Worldwound*; and somehow that was the thing that went in under his guard, more than any of the rest of it.

After a while he saw that Korroc had his arm round her, and that she was leaning into him, a little, against her own judgment. And he saw that there was nothing else here that needed him, and got up, and said he would see them in the morning, and went.

He did not go to his bed.

He went and found Horgus Gwerm.

The man was in his storeroom kingdom off the south court, behind his long table, in the lamplight, with his lists. He looked up when Thane came in and visibly braced for a demand. When Thane told him what he wanted, Horgus put his pen down and looked at him for a long moment with an expression Thane had never seen on him before, and did not complain once.

What Thane wanted was a place. Inside the citadel. Somewhere fit to lay two dwarves, if it came to bringing them home. Somewhere that was not a mass grave in a courtyard, or a corner of the crusade's burying-ground, but a place of their own, in the fortress they had helped build and been prisoners in and dug their way out of.

Horgus knew every room in Drezen. It was his whole profession now, knowing where everything was. He took out a plan of the citadel — his own, annotated in his fussy small hand in three colors of ink — and spread it on the table, and the two of them stood over it in the lamplight for most of an hour.

Sosiel came in partway through. He had been looking for Horgus about something else entirely, some business of altar-cloths, and he stopped in the door when he saw who was there and what was on the table, and Horgus — Horgus, of all men — said quietly, "Come in, Sosiel. Shut the door."

So he did.

They went through the places one by one. A chapel. A crypt below the old keep that had been used for something else and would need cleansing. A small walled garden on the west side, overgrown, where Horgus thought there might once have been a shrine. Thane listened to all of it and put each one in his head beside the others, and turned them over, and did not decide.

And then, when the talk had run down, and Horgus had gone to fetch more lamp-oil with a great deal of muttering, Thane looked at Sosiel across the plan of the citadel.

"In the tunnel," he said. "You spoke with the dead man for us."

"I did."

"Could you do it again?"

Sosiel looked at him.

"With my father," Thane said.

The priest did not answer at once. He was a man who took his time over things that deserved it, and he took it now. Thane watched him think. He had a good face for it — open, unhurried, a face that had decided not to let a war hollow it.

"I want to honor my father," Thane said. "Wherever he ends up. Here, or there. But I'd also like —" He stopped. Started again. "I'd also like to talk with him."

He had not said it aloud before, to anyone but Korroc. Korroc had looked at him for a long time and said *aye, I thought you might,* and nothing else.

Sosiel's eyes were very steady.

"It's highly unusual," he said gently. "You know that?"

Thane nodded.

"Yes," he said. "I understand."

Sosiel looked at him a while longer. He did not say no. Thane noticed that the way he noticed a door left unbarred. He did not say yes either. He said, at last, that they would talk about it again, later, when there had been time — and then Horgus came back with his oil, complaining about the price of it, and the moment was over.

Thane walked back across the court alone in the dark.

He did not know why he wanted it. That was the truth. He did not know what he would ask. *Where did you go, after?* — but the dead man in the tunnel had been a stranger, and the questions had been easy. *Who were the two humans? Did they know who you were? Did you ask them to bring you there?* He could ask those. They were a good inquisitor's questions. He was good at those.

But under them was another one, that he would never ask in front of a priest, and could not have put into words to himself, and kept trying to.

*Did you know we were looking?*

---

The next day, Dagna wanted to see the graves.

She did not want to wait for soon. She had lain awake all night, she said, and found that soon was not a thing she had the patience for after all, and she would thank them not to make anything of it.

Nobody made anything of it.

Caleth folded them there. All five of them, Dagna included, in the courtyard in the early morning with a ring of curious soldiers watching from a safe distance; Thane felt the world go away under him in that small cool half-second of nowhere, and come back, and they were standing among the dwarven stones on the hill above Sesker's Gully with the dead trees at the bottom of the slope.

Dagna staggered. Korroc caught her. She shook him off and stood on her own, and said something about wizards that Caleth pretended very hard not to have heard.

Then she saw the stones.

Caleth and Nageru did not come any further. They stopped where the proper rows ended, the way they had the day before, and stood there together, and Thane did not even see them decide to do it.

Dagna walked down to the five graves between her son and her nephew.

She stood at the foot of them for a long time. Then she got down on her knees in the grass, slowly — she was not young, and the hill was steep — and she put out her hand and touched the stone with *BORIN* on it, and traced the runes with one square scarred fingertip, every stroke. And then she did the same with the one beside it.

Thane watched her trace the slipped *R*. She stopped there, the way he had.

She did not cry. Thane had not thought she would. She knelt between the graves with one hand on each stone, the way she had held both their hands on the block in the yard, and she did not say anything at all for a very long time, and Thane stood behind her and found himself thinking about his mother.

*Your mother should be the one writing this letter.* Dagna had put that at the end of a page, in Nerosyan, in her small upright hand, and he had folded it into his shirt. He thought it now. *She should be the one here.* She had loved Drezen. She had always believed they would go back. She had fought beside Thorek in the crusade and come home when he did not, and lived the rest of her life on *disappeared*, and died on it.

She had never known about the tunnel. Or the stones. Or the slipped *R*.

He let that go through him, and did not stop it, because there was nobody here to see but family.

The sun climbed. A wind came up the hill off the dead country and moved the grass on the graves.

At last Dagna stood up.

She did it slowly, one hand on her knee. She brushed the earth off her skirts. She looked down at the two stones a moment more, and then she turned around and looked at the two of them, and her face was the face Thane remembered from his boyhood, telling two young dwarves exactly how things were going to be.

"We know where they are," she said. "And they are not going anywhere."

Thane had known it was coming. He had known since the first grave. He found he was not ready anyway.

"They should stay here," said Dagna. "Here. In this ground. With all these dwarves" — and she lifted one hand and swept it out over the hill, over the long rows of square gray stones running away up and over the brow, more of them than anyone could count — "who died fighting for Khar-Zadûn."

She said the name the way she had said it in the tunnel. The name of a relative.

"Yes," she said, "we built Drezen. All four of us. And you've taken it back, the pair of you, and I've never been so proud of anything in my life and never will be again." Her voice did not shake. "But Drezen was a place we lived. Khar-Zadûn was what they *dreamed*. Getting it back. That was the whole of it, for both of them, since before either of you was born." She looked at the graves. "This is the right place for them. Among their own. Among their friends."

She looked at Thane then. Directly. She knew. Of course she knew; she had known him since before he could walk.

Thane looked at the stones.

He thought about a crypt below Drezen's old keep, that would need cleansing. A walled garden on the west side. Three colors of ink on a plan in Horgus's lamplight. And a priest's open face across a table, not saying no.

He thought about his father, who had dreamed of a city in the sky, and had built another city on the ground, and had been chained in it, and dug his way out of it, and walked away from it up a dry riverbed with his brother on his arm. Who had never once, as far as Thane knew, wanted to go *back* to Drezen. Only up. Only home.

*This is an appropriate place.*

It was. He could see that it was. He hated that he could see it.

"Aye," he said.

It came out rough, and he let it.

Dagna looked at him a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, and reached up and put her hand on his face, briefly, the way she had when he was small — a dry square palm against his cheek, and gone.

They stayed a while longer, the three of them. Then they walked back up between the rows to where Caleth and Nageru were waiting, and Dagna took Caleth's arm without asking, as though he had been brought along for exactly that purpose, and in a moment the world folded and they were standing in the courtyard in Drezen with the soldiers staring.

Thane went and found something to do with his hands.

---

The next few days were quiet.

Thane did not trust them. Quiet in Drezen had never meant anything good; it meant something somewhere was taking its time. He walked the walls. He went down twice to the tunnel mouth at the eastern bridge, where the engineers were shoring the old water-way so it could be used, and stood looking into it for a while each time. He spent an afternoon at the Purity Forge with Korroc and Joran Vhane, who did not speak, and worked a blade-blank he did not need, because the hammer was something to do.

He went to see Sosiel once. They talked for some time. Nothing was decided.

Caleth scried every morning.

Thane knew because he had taken to being up early, and the half-elf's door was across the west court from his, and every morning at the same hour there was a lamp behind it. Afterward Caleth would come out, and cross the yard, and his face would tell Thane what had happened as plainly as a sign on a post. *Shut. Again.* Every morning, the same. Caleth never said so. Nobody asked.

The week ran out.

Thane had counted the days. He was a man who counted. Seven days from the quasit on the parapet: *abandon Drezen within the week, or he will come and take more.* Irabeth had doubled the watch for the last two of them, and then tripled it. On the seventh night Thane sat up on the north wall until the stars went gray, with his knives in his lap and his back to a merlon, watching the dark.

Nothing came.

Not that night, and not the next. Nothing came over the walls, or out of the Worldwound, or down out of the sky. The sun came up on the eighth day exactly as it had on the seventh, and nothing in Drezen was taken.

He did not trust that either.

It was the fourth morning after the graves. They were in the command room — the four of them, Irabeth, Aron — around the long table, going over the plans for the eastern bridge and the shoring of the tunnel. Something ordinary. Something with numbers in it. Thane had almost started to let it be ordinary.

A soldier came in. He did not knock; he had been running. He came to attention badly, chest heaving.

"Commander. Your presence is needed on the walls." He swallowed. "A lone figure has been spotted. To the west."

Irabeth frowned at him. "What do you mean?"

"Ma'am, it — it just appears to be someone walking toward the citadel." The soldier glanced at the four of them and back at her. "They've just come over the horizon. So he's still a long way off."

*One.* Thane was already moving. They all were.

---

The west wall of Drezen looked out on a country that had been farmland once and was something else now — a long rolling plain of dun earth and scrub and old stone walls, running away to a horizon that never sat quite level. Thane came up onto the wallwalk and found the sentries already crowded at the parapet, pointing, and somebody pressed a spyglass into his hand before he had asked for it. Korroc took another.

He put it to his eye and found the horizon. Found the place the sentries were pointing. Brought it up.

A figure.

One. Small, at this distance. Humanoid. Walking toward them across the open ground, alone.

Except that it was not walking. Not the way a man walks who has somewhere to be. It went forward a few steps and swayed. Went forward again. Lurched — a long sideways stagger, like a drunk, or like a man walking in his sleep — and recovered, and went on.

And fell.

Thane watched it go down. Full length, on its face, the way you fall when your legs have simply stopped agreeing to hold you. It lay there. One heartbeat. Two. Thane found that he was not breathing.

It got up.

Slowly. In stages. Hands and knees first, and a long pause on hands and knees, head hanging. Then one foot under it. Then up, swaying. And then forward again.

Toward Drezen.

He could not see a face. It was too far, and the figure had something over its head — a hood, or a cowl, or a cloak pulled up. He could not see anything but that it was a person, and that it was walking toward the citadel, and that it was very nearly done.

Beside him, Korroc lowered his spyglass.

He did not say anything at first. Thane looked at him. His cousin's face had gone very still, the way it went before a fight, and then something else had come into it under the stillness.

Korroc turned and looked past Thane — at Caleth.

"I think," he said, "we need to be out there."

Caleth did not answer.

Thane looked at him too, and saw that he did not need to. The half-elf was standing at the parapet with his hands on the stone and his eyes on that small far figure, and his face had gone blank and intent in the way that meant every part of him was working on one thing. His lips were moving, very slightly. He was not praying. He was *measuring*. Distance. Angle. The lay of the ground. Where exactly the fold would put them down.

He had been measuring since before Korroc spoke.

"Everyone get close," Caleth said.

They got close. Thane felt Nageru's shoulder against his, and Korroc's bulk at his back, and the half-elf began to speak — quickly, quietly, the long working, his hands moving — and the air started to do that thing it did, the faint pressure behind the ears —

And from further along the wall, a lookout cried out.

"*Commander! On the horizon!*"

Thane saw it, in the last half-second before the world went.

Behind the figure. Coming over the rise, out of the west, where the walking man had come from. Three shapes. Big. *Very* big. Low to the ground and wide, and too many legs —

The world folded.

---

It came back full of wind and the smell of dust and dead grass, and Thane was standing forty feet in front of the walking figure.

It had fallen again. It was lying on its side in the dirt, curled, hood over its face, one hand out flat on the ground as though it had been trying to push itself up and had forgotten partway through. It was alive. Thane could see it breathe.

And behind it, coming on across the open ground, the three of them.

They were spiders.

They were not spiders. That was the first thing and the worst. They had the shape of spiders, eight legs and a fat low body and a cluster of eyes — but each one was the size of a bull elephant, and the legs did not *move* like legs, they moved like the arms of a mill, every joint going exactly as far as it was meant to go and no further. The bodies were some dark stuff that was not flesh and was not stone. And the eyes were *lit* — each one shining with a hard, cold, steady light, like a lantern-glass with something burning behind it.

They had an odd look. Thane could not have named it. Something about them that was wrong in a way a demon was not wrong; a demon was at least *alive*.

He did not wait to name it.

He shut his eyes. One word, to her, not loud — and the blessing came down over the four of them like a hand laid flat against a door.

He opened his eyes in time to see the nearest spider look at him.

All of its eyes, at once. They brightened. And a ray came off one of them — a straight line of hard light, crossing the forty feet between them faster than an arrow —

Thane was not there. He had seen the eyes brighten and had dropped, flat, the way you drop when somebody shouts *down* in a tunnel, and the ray went over him so close he felt it lift the hair on the back of his neck.

Another spider. Another eye, another ray, this one blue-white, crackling — and it went past Thane and found Caleth, and Thane heard the half-elf cry out as the lightning went into him and *through* him, every muscle in his body locking at once.

Caleth stayed on his feet. Barely. He shook his head, hard, as though to clear water out of his ears, and looked up at the things through narrowed eyes, and Thane saw him *read* them, in the space of a breath.

"Those are constructed," Caleth said. "Not flesh."

*Made.* Thane looked at them again and understood the odd look. Somebody had made these. Built them, the way Korroc built a hinge.

On the ground, the hooded figure had fallen again. It was trying to rise. It could not.

And Korroc, beside Thane, said in a voice like a hammer coming down:

"We can't see who that is." His shield came up. "We need to make sure it's not Aravashnial."

Thane's head turned.

He had not let himself think it. On the wall, with the spyglass, watching that figure fall and get up and fall — he had not once let himself think it, because it was the kind of thought that got men killed. But Korroc had said it out loud. And Thane looked at Caleth, and saw the half-elf's face, and saw that Caleth had thought it on the wall before any of them, and had not let himself say it either.

Caleth nodded. Once. A grim, closed look came over his face, and Thane had seen that look on him before, but only once — in a cavern near the Molten Scar, with a dragon in it.

The half-elf drew himself in. Thane felt it rather than saw it: that golden deep thing in him, pulled up. He spoke, and something went out over the four of them, cool, a ward — Thane felt it settle on his skin like a second shirt, and knew what it was for. *Fire.* Then Caleth set his feet, and his face went still with concentration, and the air around him *bent*.

Thane had seen him do that against the drakes. He had not understood it then. He understood it a little better now: a space around the half-elf, thirty feet across, where things that came in did not arrive as what they had been. Cold came in as fire. And on the four of them now, the fire would meet the ward.

He had built them a house in the middle of an open field, out of nothing, in the time it took to breathe twice. Thane was a man who appreciated a good door. He appreciated this one.

The nearest spider had reached the fallen figure.

It did not attack it. It reached for it — one great leg coming down, the claw at the end of it opening, closing on the hooded shape — *taking* it —

The figure rolled. Away, and over, at the last instant, out from under the claw. Not fast. Not well. But it rolled, and the claw closed on earth, and the spider lunged again, and the figure rolled again.

*It wants him back,* Thane thought. *It's not here to kill him. It came to fetch him.*

Behind him, Korroc reached out and laid his gauntlet flat on Nageru's chest, and spoke over it in Dwarven, low. Thane felt the working go out between them, the bond his cousin had been laying on people for a year getting suddenly heavier, thicker — *half*.

And Nageru was gone.

He did not run. He took a step — and he was at the first spider, as if he had folded the world himself, and both his fists went into the side of it, one-two, and Thane heard the sound they made on whatever it was made of, a sound like a hammer on a bell.

Thane went in after him. He did not run to the side, or circle, or look for an angle, because there was no angle on a thing that size that was any better than any other. He charged. Straight across the open ground, low, knives out, and at the last stride he went in under the nearest of the great legs and put his mother's blade into the joint of it, where the leg met the body — the seam, the place where two pieces of something had been fitted together by somebody who knew their work, and had still had to leave a gap.

The blade went in. He felt it bite. Something inside the joint ground against the steel.

A ray shot past him at Korroc.

It was cold. Thane felt it go by — a breath of dead winter — and saw it strike his cousin square on the shield and the shoulder, and he flinched, because cold was the thing that got through Korroc, it was the one thing the stone in him could not stop —

And the ray *changed color*.

He saw it happen. In the last few feet before it reached Korroc, crossing into the space around Caleth, the white of it went red. Cold went in; fire came out. And the fire met the ward Caleth had laid, and broke on it like a wave on a wall, and was gone.

Korroc stood there with frost-smoke rising off his shield and not a mark on him. He looked down at himself. Then he looked over at Caleth, and Thane saw his cousin's face, and would have laughed if there had been time.

The spider Nageru was on turned and struck back.

It struck *fast*, for a thing that size. The legs came down on him — one, two, three — and he went between them, under, around, all three missing. But it had more legs than he had room, and a fourth caught him, and a bite came down out of that cluster of lit eyes, mandibles like shears — and closed. Nageru grunted. Blood. And Korroc, thirty feet away, jolted, and grunted the same grunt at the same instant.

And the spider closed a leg round Nageru and *lifted*, and held him.

Thane saw Caleth's face change. He saw the half-elf turn — not to Nageru, but to the figure on the ground, the one the other spider was still reaching for — and reach out and put his hand on Korroc's shoulder.

They were gone. Both of them. That cool small tug in the air, and two empty places in the grass where they had been standing, and then they were *there* — forty feet off, beside the fallen figure, Korroc's shield already coming up between it and the claws.

The figure rolled again, away from the spider's leg, and as it rolled the hood came back.

Just for a moment. Half a heartbeat. A face, gray with dust and dark with bruising, turned up to the sky.

Caleth's voice went across the field.

"*Aravashnial!*"

Thane had never heard Caleth make a sound like that. Not in a year. Not in the underdark, not in the Blackwing, not over the broken staff on Irabeth's table. It was not loud, exactly. It was *torn*, the way cloth is torn — a name pulled out of him from somewhere so far down that it came up with pieces of other things still attached to it.

And Caleth went to his knees beside the elf in the dirt, and put both hands on him, and Thane saw that golden deep thing in the half-elf pulled up out of him again, all at once, without a thought for how much of it he had left — and poured down through his hands. A paladin's hands. Thane saw the worst of the bruises on that gray face go pale and shrink.

Korroc, beside them, flung out his arm, and a wave of warmth went out of him over the whole field. Over all of them. Over the elf on the ground. Thane felt it go into him like a swallow of hot broth.

*Aravashnial.*

He did not have time to think about it. He put it away — put it in the place, the way you set aside a wound until the fighting's done — and turned back to his spider.

Nageru was still in the claws of his. He had stopped struggling. He had gone still, the stillness Thane had learned to dread — the stillness of a thing gathering itself — and his hands, pinned half against his chest, began to *burn*.

Gold. Pale, steady, not flickering. The light Thane had seen on him in the fane, and on the dragon. It came up over his fists, and ran up his forearms, and he drew both hands back as far as the claws would let him and brought them down together on the spider's body.

It did not dent. It *shattered*.

It came apart under his fists like a dropped pot, cracks running out across the whole dark body of it from the place he had struck, faster than Thane's eye could follow — and then there was a sound, deep inside it, like a forge-bellows drawing in —

*Down*, Thane thought. *Get down get down get —*

It exploded.

Thane went flat. The blast went over him in a roar of heat and sharp flying pieces, and he felt things go past his head like thrown stones, and heard them *strike* — and in the middle of it, out of the corner of his eye, saw a dome of light spring up out of nothing over Caleth and Korroc and the elf on the ground. Clear, shining, perfect, a bowl of glass turned upside down over the three of them. The blast hit it and broke around it like a river around a rock.

And Nageru — Nageru, who had been at the center of it, in the thing's claws, *on* it — simply was not where the blast went. He had dropped out of the falling claws and rolled under the fire as it came, and came up out of the roll on the far side, twenty feet off, covered in soot, entirely unhurt, looking slightly annoyed.

The dome went out.

Caleth was kneeling under where it had been, one hand still raised. He had reacted before the thing blew. Before Thane had. Before any of them. *Of course he had,* Thane thought. *He'd have run that future before we came through the fold.*

Something hit Thane from behind.

He had been looking the wrong way. He knew it as it happened, with the absolute clarity that comes too late to be any use. The second spider had come up on him while he watched the dome, and it did not reach for him — it *struck*. A bite, the shear-mandibles closing on his shoulder through the chain. Then a claw. Then another. Then two more, raking, one after another, four claws down his back and side like a man being raked with a harrow. Then the last claw closed on him and *lifted*.

He was off the ground. He was in the air, in its grip, with blood running into his eyes.

*No.*

*Not again. Not twice in a week.*

He twisted. The way he had twisted out of the drake's talons — the way his mother had taught him to twist out of a grown man's grip when he was seven years old and smaller than everything — every joint in his body going at once in a direction the hand holding him did not expect. The claw was strong. It was not clever. He went out of it like an eel and dropped, and landed on his feet in the dirt in a crouch with both knives up, bleeding from six places, and grinned at it, because there was nothing else to do with his face.

To his left, the third spider had reached Nageru again. It bit him. A claw opened his side. It reached for him with another — and Nageru *bent*, just a little, and the claw closed on air where he had been.

He came up out of it shaking his head.

"*Not again,*" Thane heard him say, very distinctly.

Caleth was on his feet. He had a rod in one hand. Thane saw him close his fingers around it and draw on the deep place again — *again*, how much did he have left — and turn, and fling out his other hand.

What came out of it was winter.

A great cone of white, bitter, killing cold, roaring out across the field in a fan — and it went *around* them. Around Thane, around Nageru, around Korroc and the elf on the ground. Thane felt it go past his face like a wall of ice close enough to touch, and none of it touched him. The rod, he thought. Something in the rod, threading the spell around his friends like a needle around a knot.

And then it crossed the edge of Caleth's bent air, and everywhere it reached a spider it was not cold any more.

It was fire.

Both of the remaining things went up in it. The flames ran over their dark bodies, in at the joints, in at the eyes, and they reared and shuddered and their lit eyes guttered.

Korroc had gone down on one knee beside the elf. He had his eyes shut and one hand flat on Aravashnial's chest, and Thane saw the working go out of him — that thread, the one he laid on people, going out to the elf and settling and *holding*. Whatever happened to Aravashnial now, Korroc would carry some of it.

Nageru's fists lit gold again.

He hit the spider that had just tried to grab him. Three times. He did not hurry. Thane watched the third blow land and the spider simply *stop* — every leg locking at once, every eye going dark — and it fell over sideways onto the ground like a dropped table, and lay there, inert, a thing made and then unmade. It did not explode.

That left one.

It was the one that had bitten Thane. It was coming for him again.

He was ready for it. He thought he was ready for it. He had his knives up and his feet under him and his eyes on its eyes, watching for them to brighten, and they did not brighten. It did not use the eyes. It used the legs. It came in at him with every one of them, all at once, a cage of claws coming down from above and both sides —

The first one hit him hard enough that he felt his ribs go.

The second hit him harder.

He was aware of being on the ground. He was aware of blood, a great deal of it, hitting the dirt under his face in a sound like rain. He was aware of the sky, which was the wrong color, and of a claw closing round him, and of being lifted again, and thinking, with a calm that was very far away, *well, that's three times.*

Then he was not aware of anything.

---

The first thing he knew was warmth.

It came into him from outside. Not gently. It hit him in the chest the way a thrown stone hits, and went *in*, and spread, and he felt his ribs stop being broken. Felt his lungs remember what they were for. Felt the darkness go thin.

He opened his eyes.

A face. Very close to his own. Gray with dust, and bruised dark down one side, and swollen at the eye. A long elven face, gaunt, with the bones too sharp under the skin. Its eyes were half closed.

Aravashnial.

Thane was lying on his side in the dirt, beside him. Close enough to touch. Close enough to see that the elf's lips were cracked and split, and that the side of his face that was not bruised was burned red from days of sun.

For a moment he did not know where he was. Then he did.

He did not know how he had gotten here. The last thing he had was a claw, and a sky. *Caleth.* It had to have been Caleth — he could feel it, the faint wrongness behind the ears that a fold left in you. Caleth had come and gotten him out of that claw, somehow, and folded him back here, and put him down beside the place where the elf had been lying.

And twenty feet away Korroc was standing with one arm still flung out from the throw, and his face —

Thane had seen that face on his cousin once before. A week ago, from a drake's claws, looking down.

*Three times*, he thought, at Korroc. *That's three.* But he did not have the breath to say it.

Out on the field, Nageru crossed fifteen feet of open ground in a single step and drove his fist into the last spider, and it collapsed. Straight down, all its legs going out from under it at once, like a tent when the pole is pulled. It did not get up. Its eyes went dark.

Then there was quiet.

Korroc sent a wave of healing out over them. Then another. Thane felt them both, the warmth going through him and into him, and felt the last of his wounds draw closed, and lay there in the dirt and let them.

Beside him, Aravashnial sat up.

He did it slowly. Very slowly. Thane could see it cost him. He got one elbow under himself and then a hand, and pushed, and Korroc was there at once with a gauntlet behind his back to steady him, and he came up to sitting and stayed there, swaying a little, and looked around at them.

He was not in good shape. Thane read him the way he read anything, and what he read was a man who had been at the edge of himself for a long time and had gone over it some days ago and kept going anyway, on nothing. His feet were bare, or as good as. They were black with mud and grime to the ankle and cut beneath it. His clothes were in rags — what had once been a good robe, Thane thought, torn and stiff with dirt and old blood. His face was the worst of it. Even with Caleth's healing in it, it was a map of what had been done to him: the bruising, the swelling down one side, a cut over the eyebrow badly knit.

And around his throat, a collar.

Black metal. Close-fitting. And set in the front of it, at the hollow of the throat, a single faceted stone, green as bad water.

Thane looked at that collar and felt his own throat go tight.

He had heard about it. He had not *seen* it — that had been Caleth, over a kettle of water, in a command room, before any of this. But he had heard Caleth tell it to Irabeth after, in a flat steady voice that went on being flat and steady the whole way through: *a black collar. A green crystal. Needles.* And had watched the half-elf's hands, while he said it, and seen that they were not steady at all.

Caleth knelt in front of the elf.

He looked at the collar for a long moment. Then he reached out, and touched it.

He jerked his hand back as if he had put it on a stove.

It was nothing — a flinch, a reflex. But Thane saw his face as he did it, and understood that it had not been heat. Caleth had touched the thing and *felt* what it was, and his body had decided before his mind had that it wanted no part of it.

"Anti-magic," Caleth said.

He frowned at it. He was holding his hand against his chest.

"Thane." He did not look round. "Can you get this off him?"

Thane got up.

He did it carefully, because his body was still deciding whether it had forgiven him, and he came round and knelt on Aravashnial's other side. The elf's eyes moved to him. They were half closed and very tired, and Thane could not tell how much of what was in front of them they were actually seeing.

He looked at the collar.

*Listen first.*

There was nothing to listen to. It was not a door, and there was no bolt to speak. He leaned in close, close enough to smell the man, sweat and dirt and old blood and something under it, medicinal and bitter, and he looked.

*Look second.*

He found the needles first. He had known they would be there and he looked for them anyway, the way you look for the trap you already know about, because knowing about a trap is not the same as knowing *where*. They were on the inside of the band — he could see them where the metal stood a hair off the skin at the back: fine points, a ring of them, going into the elf's neck. The skin around each one was red and angry and crusted. He did not let himself think about how long they had been in.

He found the seam. A hairline, at the side, under the jaw, where the band met itself. There was no keyhole. Of course there was no keyhole. Whoever had made this had not meant for it ever to come off, and certainly not to be opened by the man wearing it. But they had had to put it *on*, and so there was a seam, and where there was a seam there was a catch, and where there was a catch there was a way.

*Touch third.*

He took out his picks.

It took a while. He did not hurry. He worked with his head on one side and his breath held and his whole attention in the ends of his fingers, the way his mother had taught him, feeling for the place where the catch lived inside the metal. He had to go in at an angle that put the pick a hair's breadth from the elf's skin, and every time the steel shifted he felt Aravashnial go rigid under his hands, and he did not stop, and he did not slip.

Nobody spoke. He could feel the three of them standing round him and not speaking. Korroc's hand was on the elf's shoulder. Caleth was kneeling at his other side, very still, and Thane did not look at his face.

*There.*

Something in the metal shifted, deep in, and caught. He felt it the way he felt every lock that had ever given way to him — not a sound, a *settling*, a thing that had been held suddenly being allowed to let go.

*Click.*

The collar opened. He eased it, with both hands, back off the needles. Slowly. One by one he felt them come out, and the elf made a sound in his throat, and then it was off and it was in Thane's hands, warm from the man's skin, and the ring of small red wounds on Aravashnial's neck began very slowly to bleed.

Korroc's hand went over them at once. Warmth.

Aravashnial shut his eyes.

He drew a breath. It was a long one. It went all the way down and stayed there, and when it came out again some line in his shoulders that had been drawn tight for a very long time went slack. As though something that had been holding him up from inside, or holding him down, had been taken away, and he did not yet know which.

Caleth held out his hand.

Thane put the collar in it.

The half-elf did not flinch this time. He held it in his palm and looked at it — the black band, the green stone — and his face did not show anything at all. Thane had never seen it show so little.

"The wearer cannot cast," Caleth said. "At all. Nothing." He turned it over. "But anyone outside it can. It does nothing to the one who holds it." His fingers closed. "We'll keep that. For later."

Thane did not ask what *later* meant. He did not think he wanted to know.

Aravashnial's eyes had opened again. They moved over the four of them without seeming to settle on any one. His lips moved. It took him two tries to get the words out, and when they came they were a whisper, cracked and dry as the riverbed.

"Been walking," he said. "For days."

That was all.

He lay back. Slowly; Korroc took his weight and let him down into the grass. And his eyes closed, and his breath went long and even, and Thane watched him go — not into anything dark, not the way a dying man goes, but simply *out*, like a lamp turned down at the end of a long night by somebody who has decided, at last, that he can stop watching the door.

Korroc kept his hand on the elf's chest. The thread between them hummed.

Caleth knelt there, very still.

Then he reached out, and laid one hand on Aravashnial's shoulder, gently, and Thane felt the world fold around all five of them —

---

— and they were standing outside the citadel infirmary, in the west court of Drezen, with a dozen soldiers scattering out of the way in alarm.

Somebody shouted. Somebody else ran. Thane and Korroc got the elf up between them, an arm over each of their shoulders, his bare feet trailing, and carried him in through the infirmary door, and Thane had time to notice that he weighed almost nothing.

Sosiel was already there.

He was at the far end of the long room, bent over a cot, a soldier with a bandaged arm — and he looked up as they came through the door, and his face did something Thane had never seen it do. All the openness went out of it in one stroke, and what was underneath was fast and hard and very competent. He was across the room before they had got the elf to a cot.

"Here," he said. "Here. Put him down. *Gently.* What —"

And then he saw who it was, and stopped talking.

Irabeth came through the door close behind them, still half-armored, at something faster than a walk and slower than a run, with Aron at her shoulder. She must have come straight off the wall. She stopped at the foot of the cot and looked down at the man lying on it.

Thane watched the commander of Drezen's face, and saw her see him, and saw her not believe it, and then believe it.

"*Gods,*" she said. Very quietly. "Oh — gods."

Aron said nothing at all. He stood beside her with his hand over his mouth, and Thane remembered that it had been Aron who stood at Irabeth's table, the day they came back from the west, and told them how it had happened, beside the two halves of a staff.

Irabeth turned and looked at the four of them. Her eyes went from one to the next and stopped on Caleth, and held there a moment, and moved on.

"How?" she said.

"We don't know," said Thane. It was the truth, and it was all of it. "He walked. From the west. Days, he said. There were three — *things* — following him. Made things. We put them down." He held up an empty hand. "That's all we have. He said four words and went out."

Irabeth looked at him a long moment, and then nodded, once, and did not ask anything else. She went and stood at the head of the cot, and stayed there, with her arms folded, and did not leave.

Korroc did not leave either.

He had taken a stool at the bedside and set his shield against the wall and taken off his gauntlets, and he sat there with his bare hands on the elf. One on the chest. One, after a while, on the brow. He did not hurry. Sosiel worked around him, and the two of them, without a word passing between them, divided the work: the priest with his salves and his cloths and his quiet prayers, the dwarf with his hands and the thread and whatever it was that came down out of the Father's forge and through him into a broken man. They washed the worst of the dirt off the feet. They cleaned the ring of small wounds at the throat. Sosiel cut the ruined robe away and Korroc lifted the elf so he could, as easily as if Aravashnial had been a child.

Thane stood at the end of the cot, beside Irabeth, and watched, because there was nothing for his hands to do.

It took a long time. The light in the high windows went from white to gold. And at the end of it the elf was clean, and covered, and his breathing had gone from the long ragged pull of a man at the edge to the slow soft rhythm of a man asleep.

Only asleep.

Korroc sat back on the stool at last, and put both hands on his knees, and let his head hang for a moment. Then he lifted it and looked at Sosiel, and Sosiel nodded, and Korroc nodded back.

*He'll do*, the nod said. *He'll do.*

---

Thane went outside.

He sat on the step of the infirmary in the evening light with his back to the doorframe, and took out his knives, and wiped them.

Mother's blade. Father's. The punch dagger. The one from his wrist. Both faces of each, the full length, the way he always did. There was black ichor on two of them, and a kind of dark grit on the others from the joints of the spiders, and he wiped all of it off and then went on wiping, slowly, long after there was anything left on the steel.

*No Stonevein has ever sheathed a wet blade.*

His father had taught him that. Thorek Stonevein, who had dreamed of a city in the sky and dug his way out of a prison on the ground and buried his brother on a hill and walked on alone, with a tool that was not meant for stone in his pack, and died somewhere Thane would never know. And been carried home by two strangers.

Somebody had picked his father up off the ground and carried him. A long way, maybe. To a place they had decided he would want to be.

Thane sat on the step and thought about the weight of a grown dwarf in armor across two men's shoulders, and about how far you would have to want to do a thing to carry that weight a day, or a week, through this country. He thought about a man with bare black feet who weighed almost nothing.

*Today we carried somebody home,* he thought.

He did not know what to do with it. He put it in the place, with the other thing, the two strangers, and found that they fit there together, side by side, like two stones on a hill.

He sheathed the knives.

Through the door at his back, the infirmary was quiet. He could hear Sosiel moving about at the far end of the room, the clink of a basin. He could hear his cousin's breathing, slow and heavy, the breathing of a dwarf who had spent everything he had and would sit up all night anyway. And under it, very faint, the soft even breath of the man in the cot.

He could not hear Caleth at all.

He turned his head and looked back in through the door.

The half-elf had not taken the stool. He had not taken anything. He was sitting on the floor at the head of the cot, in the shadow beside it, with his back to the wall and his knees drawn up and the black collar lying in his open hand on one knee. He was not looking at the collar. He was looking at the elf's face, in the last of the window-light, and he had been looking at it, Thane realized, for the whole of the long afternoon. Every time Thane had glanced over. Every single time.

His other hand was inside his coat. On the left side. Over his heart.

Thane looked at his face.

He had seen it before, he thought. He could not place where. Not on Caleth — he had never seen Caleth look like that. Somewhere else. Somewhere recent.

And then he had it, and it went through him cold.

He had seen it on a hill above Sesker's Gully, six days ago. On his cousin. Standing at the foot of a grave with his face wet all the way down into his beard, looking at a stone with his father's name on it.

It was the same face. Exactly the same. Turned the other way around.

*It's not grief,* Thane thought. *It's what grief would look like, if you'd been standing at the grave and the stone got up.*

He looked at the half-elf for a long moment, sitting in the dark with a dead man's collar on his knee and his hand over his heart. A student, watching his teacher sleep.

*No*, said the part of Thane that read floors. *No, it isn't.*

He did not know what it was. He knew what it wasn't. And he knew, the way you know a lock has a second tumbler when the first one sets too easily, that he had just watched a man take out half of something by a fire in Sesker's Gully, six nights ago, with his shirt in his hand.

And had not, by a long way, taken out all of it.

Thane turned back to the evening, and the court, and the long shadows on the stones. He did not go in.

*Not yet*, he thought.

He sat on the step until it was dark.
