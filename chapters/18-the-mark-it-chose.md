# The Mark It Chose

*Chapter Eighteen — The Armor of the Pious*

---

Korroc Stonevein had been a smith for thirty years and a paladin for most of them, and in all that time he had never once found a good word to say about a room built to be looked at.

This one had been built to be looked at very hard.

He stood on a five-foot lip of stone that ran the whole circle of a pit, and forty feet of empty air stood on his left hand, and rising out of the far side of the pit in the southeast was a woman with six arms and no legs at all. Serpent from the waist down, coiled and rising. Six arms flung wide over the ledges with a weapon in every fist — a katana, a shotel, a falcata, a khopesh, and a kusarigama with its chain carved mid-swing so that it seemed to be still moving. Whoever had cut her had been good. That was the part that sat wrong in him. The undercuts were clean, the drapery of the coils flowed, the links of that chain had been hollowed one from another out of solid rock by somebody with a steady hand and the whole of a year to spend.

Good work in service of a bad thing. He had been finding that all week, room after room, and it had not gotten easier.

"Aponavicius," Thane said from up the ledge, and shook his head.

"Aye. That's her."

The whispering went on under all of it.

It had been running since the prison rows, and it had not stopped when the ritualist died, and it had not stopped when the great crystal came off its chain, and it had not stopped since. Low. Continuous. Pitched just beneath speech, the way a fly is pitched just above hearing. Korroc had spent the first hour trying to make words out of it and had given that up as a bad trade, and had spent the second hour simply carrying it, the way a man carries a stone in his boot when there is nowhere to sit down.

The trouble was that it did not stay outside a man.

He watched Thane work down the ledge ahead of him, and watched his cousin put a hand up to his ear for the third time in ten minutes, and press, and take it away, and it did him no good at all.

"Those damn whispers."

"I know it."

"It's not a sound." Thane said it flat, and did not look back. "That's what's wrong with it. A sound comes from somewhere."

They dropped down off the ledge to the floor level and started across, and Korroc set his boots on the flagstones and felt the citadel his family had drawn on paper seventy years ago sitting under his heels and being wrong.

That was the other thing. He had been feeling it since the stair.

Above them, all the way up through the map rooms and the barracks and the wrecked chapel of the Father, the stone was Stonevein stone. Cut square. Cut honest. The joints ran true and the load ran down through the walls the way his people set it running, and even filthy and defiled and hung with rot it had been *his*, and he had been able to put his palm on it and have it put its palm back.

Down here it did not answer him. Down here somebody else had done the cutting, and the stone knew it, and so did he.

Thane was four paces ahead with his daggers still sheathed and his head turning slowly, the way he did — and then Thane was not walking. Thane was going sideways, hard, both boots leaving the floor, one arm windmilling.

He went out over the lip of the pit.

Korroc did not think. There was nothing in it to think about. Something in his blood older than his oath moved his weight for him, and his gauntlet closed on a fistful of chain shirt and shoulder-strap, and the pit yawned under his cousin's heels for the length of one breath, and then Korroc hauled backward with the whole barrel of his chest behind it and put Thane down on the flagstones on his knees.

Both daggers came out in the same motion. Thane came up in a crouch with his back to the pit and his eyes going everywhere at once.

"What the hell was that?"

"I was about to ask you the same."

Nothing. No shape, no shadow, no sound. Caleth had Radiance up and lit and was turning a slow circle with the blade's white throwing his shadow around the walls, and Nageru had gone absolutely still in the way that meant he was reading the room with something other than his eyes, and the room gave the four of them precisely nothing.

Then Thane's nose wrinkled.

"There it is."

"What?" Caleth said.

Thane pointed with the tip of his mother's blade at a place on the wall that looked to Korroc like every other place on the wall.

"Trap. Telekinesis, I'd say. Meant to put a man over the edge."

Korroc looked at the drop and then back at the wall and had a thought he did not much care for, which was that the thing had *chosen.* Out of four crusaders walking that ledge it had reached out and put its hand on the one who was walking nearest the lip because he was the one reading the floor. A trap that punished the man doing his job.

*That's not a trap,* he thought. *That's a joke with a hinge in it.*

He was getting tired of this fortress's sense of humor.

Something else had been bothering him since they came down, and he walked to the edge to have it out. He put his hands on his knees and looked over.

Ten feet. Flagstone floor, dry, a little rubble, nothing in it.

The stone in his veins said otherwise.

That was the only way he had ever been able to put it, and he had long since stopped trying to explain it to anybody who was not a dwarf. A wall has a weight, and the weight has a shape, and when he stood near stone he *knew* that shape the way a man knows there is someone standing behind him. It was not sight. It was not a spell. It was the same thing that had let him find a good seam in a hillside when he was nineteen years old, and his father had put a hand on his shoulder and said *aye, that's it, that's the thing, and don't let anyone tell you you're guessing.*

The shape under this pit went down a long way past its floor.

He grunted.

"What?" Caleth had come up beside him.

"Something's not right with that floor."

The half-elf looked. Korroc watched him look — and watched the looking change, watched the moment Caleth stopped using his eyes and started using the other thing, the sight that had named a false Iomedae in the great court above and had not been enough to save him from her.

"Illusion," Caleth said. "It isn't a ten-foot pit. It's forty."

"Huh." Korroc straightened. "Then that trap wasn't meant to bruise him."

"No."

They both looked at Thane, who had heard all of it and did not appear to have any observations he wished to share.

"Thane." Caleth's voice was mild. "Do you need to do anything about that trap?"

Thane crossed and looked at his patch of wall for a while.

"It's spent." He put the daggers away. "Nothing left in it. One shove was all it had, and it used it."

"On you."

"Aye. On me." He walked past Korroc toward the double doors on the far wall without any change of expression whatever. "Let's find the door it was guarding."

---

The doors were oak-bound and iron-strapped and half again as tall as a dwarf had any use for. Thane went over them the way he always did — the flat of the palm, the ear to the seam, the long pause with his eyes shut — and then set his shoulder and pushed them wide.

Beyond them there was nothing.

Not darkness. Korroc had spent half his life under a mountain and he knew darkness the way he knew his own hands, and darkness had a *bottom* to it; stand in it long enough and shapes swim up, the grey ghost of an edge, the place where the floor stops being floor. This had no bottom. It was black the way a closed eye is black. The corridor's light came to the threshold of that room and simply stopped, as though the doorway were the lip of a well and the light had gone in and fallen.

Thane muttered a word and flicked his hand, and a spark of light went out over the threshold and was swallowed whole. Not dimmed. *Eaten.* The room did not brighten by so much as a shade.

"Right," Thane said.

Caleth stepped up beside him, pulled out a wand and considered the black for a moment with his head tipped, and then reached over and laid two fingers on the head of Korroc's warhammer.

He clutched the wand, a motion.

The adamantine lit like a coal coming out of the fire — brighter, whiter, the full clean blaze of a summer noon — and Korroc felt the heat of it on his beard that was not heat at all. It was the most light he had ever carried in one hand.

The blackness went out of the room.

And most of the light went with it, because what came back was not daylight. It was a lamp. A good lamp, an honest lamp, the light of a working shop at dusk — but the spell in his fist was throwing sun and the room was handing back tallow, and Korroc looked down at his own burning hammer and understood that the dark in here was not the absence of anything. It was a substance. Something had *made* it, the way a man makes a wall, and Caleth had not banished it. He had only pushed a hole through it the width of his spell. Luckily that width was enough to see the room.

"Saint's mercy," Caleth said quietly.

"Aye."

Korroc walked in, because that was the arrangement, and had been since Kenabres.

The room came to him a piece at a time, sliding out of the black at the edge of the hammer-light. Alcoves — three in the north wall, three in the south — and in each alcove a pedestal, and on each pedestal a crystal the size of a man's head. Jagged. Unfaceted. Purple as a bad bruise. Six of them. They did not glow, exactly. They *held* light the way a deep pool holds the sky, and gave none of it back.

He had seen that color once before. In a vision that came out of a shattering wardstone, in the arms of a witch who had helped tear the world's wound open.

Ahead, where the light had not yet reached, the room went on.

He kept walking, and the far wall came out of the dark to meet him, and it had a seventh alcove and a seventh pedestal and a seventh crystal standing on it. In front of that, a slab of marble, low and broad and clean — the sort of thing you set a body on, or a crown.

On the slab, crumpled, heaped, thrown down like a rag on a bench, there was a piece of red cloth.

Two statues flanked the slab in alcoves of their own. The northern one was a goat-headed thing with wings folded down its back. The southern one was an insectoid horror with a scythe. Korroc had walked a whole hallway of murals to get here and needed neither of them introduced.

He stopped ten feet short and stood with his lit hammer and looked at a heap of red cloth and felt exactly nothing about it, because he had learned better on the floor above.

Behind him, back at the first alcove by the doors, Caleth had stopped at the nearest crystal.

"Evil," he said.

Korroc heard the certainty in it, and the way it came out under the breath, the way a man names a smell.

Then he heard the half-elf go quiet.

"Huh," Caleth said, in an entirely different voice.

"What."

"It's fake."

Thane's head came around. "What?"

"The crystal. It's a fake." Caleth had crouched at the pedestal with Radiance's white on the thing, and there was something in his posture Korroc had come to recognize over three hundred miles of road — the particular offense of a man who has been lied to badly, and cheaply. "It's carved. Cut glass and a dye, or something near it. Somebody made this to *look* like the real thing and didn't even trouble to do it well." He straightened. "It's shoddy."

"And the evil?"

"That's real." Caleth's mouth went thin. "The evil is real. The stone is not."

Korroc turned that over and did not care for the shape of it.

He was going to say so.

He did not get to.

Something reached into him.

There was no warning and no direction and no sound, and it did not touch his body at all. It went past his plate and past his skin and past the meat and bone of him as though none of those things were in the way, and it closed on what was underneath — on the part of him that had knelt in an underdark shrine and felt something answer, on the part his father had put a hand on when he was nineteen — and it *pulled.*

Korroc went down onto one knee with a sound he did not recognize as his own.

His vision whited at the edges. There was a wrenching, a real one, the specific sick wrongness of a joint being taken the way a joint does not go — except it was not a joint. He got his shield-arm braced against the floor and his teeth locked, and he held, and somewhere down in him the old stone held with him. Seventy generations of it. Ancestor-weight sunk in his blood like ballast in a hull.

Then it let go. All at once, like a hand opening.

He was on one knee, breathing like a bellows.

"Korroc—"

"That went deep." He got the words out. "That went *deep.* It tried to take my soul."

Black smoke was coming up out of the floor in front of him.

It came the way blood comes up through water, unrolling, and it gathered and thickened and put on a shape, and the shape had wings. Not a body with wings on it. A wing-shaped hole in the world, and through the hole, fire. Fire in the eye-pits. Fire in the nose-slits. Fire pouring from the mouth and burning down through the smoke of its chest in slow veins, like a forge seen through a shutter. Its arms ended in claws as long as daggers, and the claws were not made of anything.

Behind him, Caleth said one word, and the word was a name.

"Eustoyriax."

---

Thane's voice went up over all of it in old Dwarven, four words, and the blessing came down on the four of them like a hand laid flat on Korroc's back.

Then Nageru was simply *behind the thing.*

Korroc never saw him cross. He had been standing thirty feet back at the doors, and then he was on the demon's blind side with his weight already through his hips, and the kick came around and up and buried itself in the smoke of its ribs — and it *landed*, Korroc heard it land, a sound like a door slamming underwater — and the follow-up punch went through the demon and out the other side and found nothing at all.

Nageru's face did not change. He simply took note.

The demon turned inside its own smoke without moving its feet, and raked him twice. Korroc saw the aasimar's shoulder open, saw the frost come off the wound in a white breath, and Nageru stepped back one pace and set himself and made no sound whatsoever.

"Cold," Korroc said, mostly to himself. "Of course it's cold."

Two more shadows peeled off the walls.

That was the only word for it. They came out of the flat black of the room the way a stain comes through a shirt — and one of them came out *through* the nearest crystal. Through the cheap carved fake, out of the pedestal, out of the alcove, and up into a man-shape with nothing inside it.

*So much for shoddy,* Korroc thought grimly. *The gem's a lie. The door in it isn't.*

One went for Caleth. Radiance came up white and went into it twice, hard, and the shadow reeled.

The other came for Korroc.

He was still on one knee. He got the shield up and said the word that put the Father's hand between him and the world — felt the air go stiff and cold and thick an inch off his skin — and the shadow's claws came down on the enameled hammer-and-anvil and skidded off it shrieking.

"Ha," said Korroc, and got his boots under him.

Thane came around Korroc's shoulder, inside the shadow's guard while it was still committed to the shield, which was the whole of Thane's craft in one movement. Mother's blade went in and father's knife went in behind it, and the shadow-stuff parted around them like smoke around a stick, and then, grudgingly, *tore.*

Across the room, Nageru hit the demon twice more, and it was gone.

It came back behind Korroc.

He felt it arrive — not heard it, felt it, the way you feel a breeze at your back — and he was already turning when the claws came down, and the turn was not enough. They went into his side. The gold-and-mithral held. The plate did not part. And the cold came through it anyway, straight through good metal and good padding and into the muscle wall of him, and Korroc's whole left side seized.

He had never been able to explain this one to anybody either. Fire he could take. Fire was an old friend having a bad night. But cold went into him like a wedge into a green log and split him, and always had, and every ward anyone had ever set against it slid off him and found nothing to hold.

He heard himself grunt. He hated that.

The shadow on Caleth swung and missed, and Radiance answered twice and did not miss, and Korroc used the half-second for the one thing that mattered.

He moved his feet.

Two steps, no more — enough to put the demon on his right, stepping away from the shadow, instead of one on each flank, because there is no shield in the world wide enough for a man being worked from both sides, and he had learned that at a barricade in Kenabres with a Vescavor spear in his armpit.

Then he reached down.

Not with his hand. With the older thing. Down through the flagstones and the fill and the bedrock beneath, down to where the mountain stopped being anything a man had cut and started being the bones of the world, and he took hold of it and let it come up into his legs — and the floor of Citadel Drezen leaned on him, and he stood inside that leaning, and he was no longer a dwarf standing on stone. He was a piece of the stone that happened to be standing up.

He swung.

The demon flowed backward out of the arc without moving anything Korroc could see, and the hammer went through empty smoke, and the thing laughed at him.

It laughed with a mouthful of fire.

"Aye," Korroc said. "Enjoy it."

Behind him the shadow reached for Thane and Thane was simply not there any more; he had drifted a half-step and put the demon between himself and the room, which was the sort of thing Thane did without appearing to decide it. His daggers went at the demon, and the demon flowed out of those too.

Then Nageru's hands caught fire.

Not red fire. Not the golden-white forge-fire that lived in Korroc's own chest and came out of him twice in a lifetime. This was a hard clean white — a *lawful* white, the light of a thing being measured and found short — and it ran up the aasimar's forearms and pooled in his fists, and he crossed the chamber in a blur and put a punch through the demon's face and a kick through its middle, and both of them bit.

The demon screamed and lunged at him.

It did not claw. It opened that burning mouth wide and *drew in*, and the air in the whole room went toward it, and Korroc felt his own soul lean — felt it, the same deep sick tug as at the doorway, weaker at the distance but there — and he saw Nageru take the full weight of it standing three feet away.

Nageru did not move.

He looked into the fire in that mouth and shook his head, once, the way a man declines a second cup.

"Not today."

The demon blinked out, suddenly gone, and then stood in front of the doors at the back of the room.

The shadow on Caleth came in hard while its master was busy — and Korroc, turning, got to watch the half-elf do the thing he had been quietly getting better at for a hundred miles. Caleth did not block. He slid, and let the claws come past his ribs by a finger's width, and caught the shadow's arm on Radiance's flat and turned it aside, and the whole cost of the exchange to him was a half-step of ground. Then the blade came off the parry in one unbroken motion and went through the thing's chest, and the shadow came apart into nothing and was gone.

Korroc had a demon off his back for the first time in a minute. He used it.

He took one step to the first shadow, the one still worrying at his shield, and brought the adamantine down on it two-handed. Adamantine does not care what a thing is made of. The shadow folded around the hammerhead and shrieked. It struck back and found nothing but enameled steel again — and then Thane was there, and two strikes later there was nothing on that side of the room but four crusaders and a demon at the door.

Nageru crossed the chamber again.

Korroc had watched the aasimar fight for a year and it still did something to the back of his neck. There was no run-up. The man was on one side of a room and then he was on the other with his fist already landing, and the only thing that changed in his face over the whole distance was nothing at all.

Punch. Kick. Both home.

The demon slashed at him, and the white fire flared off Nageru's skin like a struck spark and turned the claws aside entire.

It screamed — a real scream this time, frustration in it, the sound of a workman whose tool has broken — and it was gone, back across the room, hanging in the air beside the seventh pedestal.

"Caleth!" Korroc shouted.

"On it—"

The half-elf's hand came up with another wand in it and five darts of light tore out of the wand and went across the chamber in a curve and hammered into the smoke one after another, and the demon jerked with each.

And Korroc, watching it hang there beside a crystal it had *chosen* to hang beside, made a decision about what sort of fight this was going to be.

He put his hand out and reached for a working he almost never had cause for — a small, mean, exact piece of the Father's craft, the one that is not about hurting a thing at all. Green light sprang off his gauntlet in a flat ray and crossed forty feet and broke over the demon and ran down through the smoke of it like oil over water, and every place it touched, it set, and clung.

"I've anchored it!" he bellowed. "It's not going anywhere now! It's stuck in this room with us!"

Nageru's head came around.

And Nageru — who did not smile in fights, who did not smile much at all — grinned at him.

---

After that it was work.

Thane came across the floor low and fast. Nageru arrived from nowhere and the kick sailed high over a duck Korroc would not have believed that thing could make, and the punch came in behind it and landed under the ribs.

The demon turned on him and hit twice, and the second one was a killer. Korroc saw it going in — saw the gap open in Nageru's guard the way you see a flaw open in a weld, the claw dropping into a line that would have taken the aasimar from collarbone to hip — and saw Nageru *see it too*, a full half-second before it arrived.

And Nageru reached into the gold.

Korroc knew what that felt like from the inside, because the same fire had been sitting in his own chest since a wardstone came apart in front of him. He watched the aasimar spend a piece of it in an eyeblink; watched the claw come in and land and *skid*; a wound instead of a ruin, blood instead of a corpse. Nageru grunted and stayed standing.

Caleth's darts came again.

Korroc closed. He came up on Nageru's left shoulder and put the burning adamantine down on the demon two-handed, and this time there was nowhere for the thing to flow, because he had nailed its road shut — and the hammer went through the smoke and found something in the middle of it that was not smoke, and cracked it.

Thane came in on the far side, opposite him. The two of them boxing it the way they had boxed things since they were young dwarves and it was a barrel between them. The daggers went out and the demon slid off them by a hair.

And the slide put its back to Nageru.

Three strikes. Korroc counted them, because they came that clean: elbow, heel, fist, each one landing in the wreckage the last had made.

The demon still stood.

*Torag,* Korroc thought, with real weariness. *What is holding this thing up.*

It turned to Thane.

Afterward Korroc would go over the next two seconds a hundred times and find nothing in them he could have done differently, and it would not help in the slightest.

The demon did not strike. It came apart — all at once, the whole burning shape unrolling into black smoke in a single breath — and the smoke did not disperse. It went *forward.* It rolled over Thane Stonevein like a wave over a stone, and wrapped him, and closed, and Korroc saw his cousin's arms come up too late, and saw the smoke thin — and understood with a lurch that it was thinning because it was going *in.*

Into the mouth. Into the nose. Into the ears, into the corners of the eyes. Drawn in like water down a crack.

And then there was no smoke at all, and Thane was standing there.

He straightened up. He rolled his shoulders, the way a man does in a new coat.

And he smiled.

Korroc had known that face for fifty-one years. He had seen it grinning over a bad ale and a good joke and a finished piece of work; he had seen it split and bleeding at a barricade; he had seen it go flat and terrible over a name written in a dead man's ledger. He knew every way that face could move.

It could not move like that.

The whole of it was wrong — the muscles doing something almost right and landing a half-inch off, like a mask cut from a portrait by a man who had only had the portrait described to him. It was the exact wrongness of the goat-headed idol upstairs. Torag's hammer and anvil with the proportions gone greedy.

And in that same instant Korroc felt the thread go tight.

*The bond.* The one that had run from him to Thane since the wardstone came apart — the thread that took a measure of his cousin's hurt out of his cousin's body and put it into his own, the reason Thane could walk into rooms he had no business walking into. It ran from the middle of Korroc's chest to a place four feet away that was not Thane any more, and something on the other end of it had just noticed it was there.

And was interested.

Korroc dropped it.

He had done that once before, on a channel bank in the Ahari with a chimera's ice in his lungs, and it had felt like putting down a friend's hand.

This felt like cutting one off.

"Korroc—" Caleth's voice, and then the half-elf's hands moving, and a word of holding thrown across the room like a rope — and it broke on Thane and slid off him and did nothing at all.

Thane's smile widened.

Korroc reached in and tore out the rest. Every thread, every working, every quiet thing he had laid on his cousin over a year of walking into the dark — all of it at once, hauled back into himself, and the emptiness where they had been rang in him like an anvil struck in a cold shop.

And then Thane came at him with both knives.

It was fast. It was very fast, and it came high-low the way Thane came high-low, and it very nearly worked, because whatever was driving the body had the body's whole craft and none of its restraint.

But it did not have fifty-one years.

Korroc turned the first on the boss of his shield without looking at it, because he knew where that hand went. He had watched that hand go there over a workbench, over a card table, over the top of a shield in a hundred idle bouts in the yard behind a forge in Kenabres. He caught the second on a vambrace of gold-and-mithral and felt the point skitter down the metal and away.

He did not swing back.

"Nageru!" he roared. "It's not him!"

"I know it, brother."

The aasimar's hands came up and the white light gathered — but not into a fist. It went out from him in a spreading ring and settled over Thane's shoulders like a coat.

"Fight it, Thane." Nageru's voice was as level as a table. "We know you are in there."

The thing wearing Thane laughed.

That was worse than the smile. It was Thane's laugh, exactly Thane's laugh, the dry one from the back of the throat — and it was being *used* — and Korroc felt a rage come up in him so pure and so hot that for a second he could not see.

Then Thane made a small gesture with two fingers and went out like a snuffed candle.

Gone. Invisible. Somewhere in a black room with a lit hammer at one end of it and three of his friends between him and the door.

"*Where*—"

"There." Caleth's voice cut through it, and he was not looking for Thane at all. He had Radiance up and pointed at the far end of the room, past the marble slab, at the seventh alcove and the seventh pedestal and the crystal standing on it, and his voice had gone hard and precise in the way it did when the man had stopped hoping and started calculating. "Korroc. His *soul* is in that crystal. That's what's holding him. The crystal has to be destroyed."

Korroc looked at the crystal.

It was purple and jagged and the size of a man's head, and it was forty feet away, and Caleth Aranor had once looked at a woman in shining armor with a sunburst on her breast and called her Iomedae for one whole heartbeat before he knew better.

*And what if you're wrong, lad.*

He did not say it. There was no time to say it and no version of saying it that did not cost more than it bought — and besides. Besides. He had watched this half-elf run futures in his head at a war council and at a bridge and in a room with a false goddess in it, and had watched him be wrong exactly once, and had watched what that once had done to him.

*Knowing wasn't enough.* Korroc did not have Caleth's phrase for it. He knew the shape of the wound.

"Then we break it," Korroc said.

Caleth was already gone — the small cold fold of the air, the half-second of nowhere — and standing at the far alcove with Radiance coming down on the crystal in a two-handed arc.

Korroc turned to the empty room, put his hand up, and asked the Father, plainly, for one small thing.

The working went out of him and settled over the whole chamber like flour thrown through a beam of light — and there, in the middle of the floor, outlined in a fine clinging dust that had never been dust, stood Thane Stonevein with a knife in each hand.

"Got you."

And then Korroc did the thing he had been putting off, which was to move.

He let go of the stone.

It came out of his legs all at once, the way a load comes off a spring, and the weariness arrived behind it like a wall falling on him — a full day's labor dropped into his knees between one step and the next, the old and honest price of having stood like a mountain for four minutes. His vision greyed. His hand went out to nothing.

So he reached down into himself instead, into the well the Father keeps full and never explains, and pulled a handful of gold up through his own chest and let it go into his own bones.

The grey went away. He walked.

Thane stabbed at him as he went past — the flat, automatic, textbook strike of a man punishing an enemy for turning his back — and Korroc did not even lift the shield. He walked, and let the point find gold-plated mithral over the ribs, and heard it skid, and kept walking.

*You'd not forgive yourself for that one, cousin. When you're back.*

"I am sorry, friend," Nageru said behind him.

Korroc did not turn around. He heard it, though — the double crack of a strike delivered with the wrist held short and the follow-through cut off, the specific sound of a man who could kill with his hands choosing, twice, in a half-second, not to. He heard the small clatter of two very good daggers hitting flagstone, and the heavier sound of a dwarf's knees going down.

He reached the alcove.

Caleth had cracks in it already, white lines running through the purple where Radiance had hit it. The half-elf stepped back without being asked, because he had seen what was coming up the room behind him.

Korroc set his feet.

He had spent thirty years learning to hit metal so that it moved the way he wanted it to move. Most of that is not strength. Most of it is knowing where a thing wants to come apart, and putting the hammer exactly there and no place else, and it is the nearest thing to prayer that a smith does with his arms.

The first blow rang.

It rang like a struck bell — one great pure note that went up the forty feet of that black ceiling and came back down — and the cracks went out from the hammerhead across the whole face of the stone and around it and through it, every one of them finding a fellow, the way frost finds the flaws in a bad casting.

Korroc raised the hammer over his head with both hands.

*Torag. If this is wrong, it's on me. Not on the lad.*

He brought it down.

The crystal did not break. It *let go.* It came apart into a thousand pieces at once with a concussion that knocked dust off the alcove and blew Korroc's beard back, and purple light went up out of the wreck in a column with smoke boiling in it, furious —

— and behind him, thirty feet back, something was torn out of his cousin.

Korroc turned in time to see it. The black smoke came out of Thane's mouth and eyes and ears all at once — not flowing now but *expelled*, the way a body expels a poison — and it hit the flagstones and rolled and dragged itself back up into the shape of a winged thing with fire in its face. The fire was guttering. The shape could not hold its own edges. And for the first time all day the demon Eustoyriax did not know where it was.

Thane blinked. He looked around the room like a man waking in a strange bed.

And Nageru was already in the air.

He came down on the demon with both fists, and the white fire on him had gone gold, and there was nothing measured about it at all. The blow landed. The demon screamed, and the scream went up into something that was not a voice, and the smoke blew apart across the floor of that room and thinned and was gone.

Silence.

The hammer in Korroc's hand was still throwing daylight into a dark that ate it.

Nobody moved for a moment. Then everybody moved at once.

Korroc was walking before he knew he had started. He threw a wave of gold out behind him as he went — it rolled over Caleth and over Nageru and over the wreck of the room, and he heard Nageru's breath change as the frost-torn shoulder closed — and he had the wand out of his belt in his other hand by the time he reached his cousin.

Thane was standing. Just standing, in the middle of the floor, with his hands empty and his daggers on the stone at his feet.

Korroc put the wand on him and gave him the whole of what it had.

"You're all right," he said. "You're all right, cousin."

Thane nodded.

He did not say anything. He bent and picked up his mother's blade, and got the rag out of his belt, and wiped it.

Korroc stood and watched him do it.

There was nothing on that blade. Korroc knew it for a fact, because he had turned it himself not a minute ago — caught it on the boss of his shield, and its brother on a vambrace of gold-and-mithral, and neither of them had touched anything but good metal since the demon walked into his cousin's mouth. There was no blood. There was not so much as a mark.

Thane wiped it down its whole length, both faces, the way he had been taught before he could shave.

*No Stonevein has ever sheathed a wet blade.*

Their grandfathers' rule, and their fathers' after that, and Korroc had heard it in Thorek's voice across a table and in his own father's voice across a forge, and he had watched his cousin keep it after every kill on three hundred miles of road. Blood or none. Specters that bled nothing. An arrow that had skipped clean off a mimic's hide. It made no odds. The hands wanted the ritual and the ritual got done, and the getting of it done was how you knew Thane Stonevein was still in there.

He put it away. He picked up his father's knife and wiped that too, the same, all of it, and put it away.

And Korroc watched his cousin's hands do all of that steadily and correctly and understood what he was being shown — that they were his hands again, that the last thing they had done under somebody else's direction was try to kill him, and that this was the answer to it. Not words. Not yet. A rag and two clean blades and the oldest small law their family had.

The steadiness was the entire point of it. It had cost something.

Korroc put a hand on Thane's shoulder.

And reached into the empty ringing place in his own chest where a thread had been, and found the other end of it, and tied it back.

He felt it go taut and settle. Thane's weariness came into him at once — a grey, familiar weight — and Korroc took it gladly and did not mention it.

"There," he said. "That's better."

Thane let out a breath.

"Aye," he said. "That's better."

---

At the front of the room, Caleth said, "Korroc."

He was standing at the marble slab.

The heap of red cloth was still lying on it, crumpled and unremarkable, and Caleth had not touched it. He had one hand held out over it, six inches off, the way a man holds a hand over a stove to judge whether it is worth putting bread in.

"What is it?"

"I don't know." The half-elf's voice had gone very quiet. "That's what's strange. I *don't know.* I've looked at it three ways and I get nothing at all. Just cloth."

"Like the one upstairs."

"No." Caleth shook his head slowly. "Not like the one upstairs. Upstairs I looked at it and saw a banner with no more magic in it than a bedsheet, and it *told me the truth* — it was a bedsheet. This tells me nothing. There is a difference between an empty room and a locked door."

He looked at Korroc.

"I'm going to pick it up."

Korroc thought about a hall of murals thirty feet high, and a room built to make crusaders despair, and a black banner hanging in an iron frame.

"Aye," he said. "Go on."

Caleth reached down and took hold of the red cloth.

The room detonated.

Not with fire. Korroc had been inside an explosion of the other kind — a wardstone coming apart over his head with a golden fist inside it — and this was that same thing again and it was nothing like it, because the wardstone's light had come up out of the ground, and this came *down.*

It went out of the cloth in Caleth's hands like water out of a broken dam, white and gold and entirely silent for the first half-second, and Korroc's whole body rang with it. The stone in his veins lit. The scar tissue and the old aches and the cold still wedged in his left side went out like blown lamps. He felt the Father's hand somewhere in the enormous middle of it — not reaching for him, not even aware of him. Simply present, the way a mountain is present to a man walking past its foot.

Caleth cried out. He did not let go.

And in the six alcoves, the six cheap fakes shattered.

All at once. North wall and south wall together, blown to purple grit on their pedestals as though a hand had gone down the room closing them one after another. And the black substance of the room went with them — Korroc saw it *shred*, saw the dark come apart into rags and the rags come apart into nothing — and then it was a stone room forty feet on a side with daylight standing in it from a hammer, and every corner of it visible for the first time.

The light died back into the cloth.

Caleth stood there breathing hard with it in both hands, and it was not crumpled any more. It was a banner. Red — deep red, unfaded after seventy years in a demon's trophy room — and worked across it in gold thread that had lost nothing at all was a sword standing before a sunburst.

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

Then Thane spoke, and his voice was odd.

"Do you hear that?"

Korroc frowned. "Hear what?"

"That," Thane said. "That's what I'm asking."

And Korroc stood still in the middle of a broken room and listened, and heard his own breath, and Nageru's, and the small tick of cooling stone.

The whispering had stopped.

It had been running under everything for hours — under the prison rows and the interrogation cells and the forge and the ritual chamber and the hall of murdered gods — and it had not stopped for the ritualist's death or for the great crystal's fall, and it had stopped now.

Korroc turned all the way around once.

"Was it the crystals?" he said. "The six?"

"Maybe," said Caleth.

"Was it the banner?"

"Maybe."

Thane said nothing at all, which was its own answer, and Korroc looked at his cousin's face and saw a man refusing very deliberately to take the first thing offered him twice in one day.

"I'll not pretend I understand it," Korroc said at last. "I'll take it. But I'll not pretend I understand it."

"No." Caleth folded the Sword of Valor once, carefully, over his forearm. "Let's not do that again."

---

They went back the way they had come, and it was strange how short it was.

That was always the way of it. A road in is measured in doors and a road out is measured in steps, and the passages that had taken them the better part of a day took them a quarter of an hour going the other direction, past the marilith and her six weapons, down the ledge, back through the hall of murdered gods where a black banner still hung in its frame and nobody spared it a look.

Joran Vhane was sitting on the steps below the forge, waiting.

He had not gone. He had been left in a ritual chamber with a dead tiefling and an open road and no one watching him, and he had got up and walked to the steps and sat down on them, and that was where he was.

He was looking at his hands.

Korroc had seen him do that twice already and had run out of things to say about it both times.

"Come on then," he said. "We're going up."

Joran got to his feet with an old dwarf's care, all knees, and led them to the cage.

It was a great iron box on a chain, and it ran on a track across the top of that pit of impossible heat with the black-and-red forge squatting down at the bottom of it, and the last time they had crossed it Korroc had spent the whole trip with his hands on the bars and his teeth set, because there is a difference between heat and heat with an intention in it and this was the second kind.

He noticed, this time, that Caleth and Thane had both drifted to stand at Joran's shoulder.

Not close enough to crowd. Just close enough to watch his hands on the levers — one on each side, the wizard and the doorkeeper, neither of them looking at the other, both of them watching the same four movements and counting.

Joran noticed too. Korroc saw him notice. He worked the controls slowly and did not make it hard for them, and did not say anything about it either, and Korroc found that he did not know what to make of that and filed it beside all the rest.

The cage went across. The doors opened. They stepped out into a corridor of good Stonevein stone that put its palm back against Korroc's palm, and up ahead somewhere there was a fortress with a war going on in it.

"Where now?" Caleth said.

Joran Vhane looked at the banner folded over the half-elf's arm.

"The tall tower," he said. "If you want them all to see it. There's nothing higher in Drezen."

---

They came out onto the top of the tallest tower in Citadel Drezen at the middle of the afternoon, into cold air and grey light and a wind that had come three hundred miles off the Worldwound and smelled of it.

The whole of it was under them.

Korroc went to the parapet and put both gauntlets on the stone and looked out, and there it was: the citadel walls, the courtyards, the gatehouse; and past the gates, the Ahari cutting its channel; and all along the near bank and up the switchback roads and through the streets of a broken city, a thousand people trying to kill each other.

He could see the crusade. He could see the line of it — the shield wall grinding uphill toward the gate under a sleet of things thrown down at it, the banners of the Fifth going forward a yard at a time. He could see where Irabeth was, or where she must be, because there was a place at the front of the wedge where the fighting was worst and the line bent forward instead of back.

And he could see the other thing, which was that it was not going to be enough today.

Not a rout. Not a disaster. Just the long grinding arithmetic of a shield wall against a gate, and the arithmetic said tomorrow, or the day after, and it said a number of dead to go with it, and Korroc Stonevein had been doing that arithmetic since the Gray Garrison and had never once liked the answer.

Caleth walked out to the edge of the tower with the banner over his arm.

He did not make a speech. Korroc would remember that afterward, and be glad of it. The half-elf simply stepped up onto the ledge with the wind pulling at him, and took the two corners of the thing in his hands, and let it fall.

Seventy years of red came down the side of the tower.

The gold sunburst took the grey light and threw it back, and the sword before it stood up dark and clean against the red, and the wind got under the whole of it and *lifted*, and the Sword of Valor snapped out over Citadel Drezen for the first time since the fortress fell.

The sound that came up off the field was not a cheer to start with.

It was a break in the noise. A hole in it — men and women all stopping at once — and then it came, and it came like weather, rolling up the switchbacks and off the walls and around the courtyards, and it did not stop.

Korroc's throat closed.

He was aware of Nageru beside him, absolutely still. He was aware of Thane on the other side, gripping the parapet with both hands and saying nothing.

*Da,* he thought, and did not finish the thought, because there was no end of it that he could have said out loud.

Below, the demons began to come apart.

Korroc watched it happen and could not have told anyone precisely what he was watching. It was not fear, exactly. It was more as though something the enemy had been leaning on had been taken away from under them — and he saw, very clearly, a great brimorak-shaped thing at the gatehouse throw its arms up and twist and try to fold itself out of the world the way they did.

And fail.

It was still there. It stood on the gatehouse road with its arms up and nowhere to go, and the crusade's front rank walked into it.

All along the line it was the same. The roads out had shut. The cultists broke first — they always did — and then the demons broke, which they mostly did not, and the shield wall stopped grinding and started *running*, and the paladins of Iomedae went up that road in a wedge with their shields lit like lamps.

The gates went in a quarter of an hour later.

Korroc watched the Fifth Crusade come through the gates of Citadel Drezen, and he stood on a tower with his hands on his family's stonework.

And Radiance, slung across the half-elf's back, began to glow brighter.

Korroc turned and saw it. It came up slow — and then it flared, one hard clean pulse of light that Korroc felt on his face like sun coming out from behind a cloud, and it did not go all the way down again afterward.

Yaniel's sword had come home to Drezen with the banner.

It seemed to have an opinion about that.

---

They found Irabeth Tirabade standing in the middle of the main courtyard, and she was covered in blood, and only some of it was hers.

She had her helm under one arm and her sword still in her fist and she had not gotten around to putting either down. Around her, the courtyard was doing the thing courtyards do after: the wounded being carried in one direction and the dead in another, somebody shouting about water, somebody else counting a company off out loud and not liking the number, a knot of crusaders at the far wall staring at a wall as though they had never seen a wall before.

She turned as the four of them came out of the tower door, and her face did the whole thing at once — relief, and then a hard soldier's assessment of all four of them for wounds, and then something that got in under the assessment and went to her eyes.

Then she looked at Korroc, and grinned.

"That mark looks good on you, Korroc." She nodded at his chest. "You sure you're not converting?"

Korroc grunted.

"Ach." He scoffed at her, which took some effort with his eyes stinging the way they were. "The Father would have words."

And then he heard himself chuckle, and shook his head, and let it go.

Irabeth's eyes moved past him.

"And who is this?"

Joran Vhane had come out of the tower behind them and stopped a few paces short of the group, hands empty at his sides, in a courtyard full of crusaders who had spent the day being killed by his brother's garrison. He did not try to be smaller than he was. Korroc gave him that much.

"That," Korroc said, "is Joran Vhane."

The name went through the nearest twenty feet of courtyard like a dropped pot. Heads came around. Somebody's hand went to a hilt.

"Aye," Korroc said, loud enough. "That name. He's Staunton's brother. And he's the reason the banner's up there and not down a hole under this rock, and I'd have that heard before anyone gets clever."

He told her the whole of it standing right there.

He did not shorten it and he did not tidy it. He told her about a dwarf who had knelt in a map room while the others were still deciding whether to swing; about a brother's journal and what was in it; about a man left alone with his brother's body and a clear road out, who had instead come down a stair after four people who had promised to kill him, to tell them they were going the wrong way. He told her about the forge and the cage and the corridors below.

Nageru put in the pieces Korroc left out, and did it the way Nageru did — quietly, at the exact moments Korroc had gone past something, saying *he also said this* and *he offered that*, and once, flatly: "He was not made to come."

The two of them went back and forth over it for a while. Irabeth listened to all of it with her helm under her arm.

When they were done she was quiet a moment.

"He'll need to be taken into custody," she said.

"Aye." Korroc nodded. "That'd be wise." He let the pause sit. "But know that I'll speak on his behalf when the time comes."

Irabeth looked at him.

"I know you will," she said.

She lifted two fingers and a pair of crusaders came over, and it was done with no roughness in it, and Joran Vhane went where he was put without a word. Korroc watched him go across the courtyard.

He did not look back. He was looking at his hands.

---

The four of them found a corner.

That was all it was: a stretch of wall out of the wind where an outbuilding met the curtain, with the flagstones swept more or less clear by the day's traffic. Korroc got his back against the stone and slid down it and put his hammer across his knees and thought, with the last coherent thought available to him, that he ought to see about his armor straps.

Nageru was asleep before Korroc finished having the thought. Sitting perfectly upright, hands on his knees, gone.

Caleth lasted perhaps a minute longer.

Korroc closed his eyes.

He was most of the way down when he heard Thane get up.

He opened them again. His cousin was standing over him, looking out across the courtyard at nothing in particular, with his thumbs in his belt.

"Sit down, lad."

"No."

Korroc waited.

"Idrian Vosner," Thane said.

And Korroc, who had been thirty seconds from sleep, said one very old Dwarven word and got his hand on the wall and started levering himself back up.

They had left the man in a vampire's bedchamber four levels down, behind a secret door that no living soul on this rock knew about except the four of them — with a waterskin and his own dagger and a promise. He could not walk. He had told them to go on. Korroc had felt the shape of that promise sitting in him all day, underneath the demon and the crystal and the banner, the way a man feels a debt he has not paid.

"Wake the others?"

"No." Thane's face did not move. "Two's enough. And they've earned it."

"So've you."

"Aye. Well."

So they went back down.

It took an hour and a half. They got a stretcher off the crusade's surgeons and two volunteers who did not ask why, and they went down through a fortress that was still, in places, not entirely taken, and Korroc walked it in gold-and-mithral plate with his hammer in his fist and his eyes half-shut and his legs going by themselves.

The door was where they had left it. Thane worked the catch and it swung, and there was a man in the dark inside with a dagger in a shaking hand, and Korroc had to say his own name twice before Idrian Vosner would put it down.

He was alive.

He wept when they came in, which was not something Korroc intended to repeat to anyone, ever.

They got him onto the stretcher and out of the room and past the wreck of the coffin, and Korroc laid hands on him going up the first flight of stairs and again on the third, and by the time they came out into the courtyard the man had a little color and was trying to talk. Korroc told him to save it.

Then he sat back down against his wall.

He was asleep before his shoulders touched it.

Some time later somebody was nudging his boot with theirs.

"Hey." Irabeth, from a long way off. "Dinner's on. Get some food in you."

Korroc opened his eyes onto torchlight and the smell of onions and the sound of several hundred exhausted people eating, and he woke Caleth by putting a hand on his ankle, and the four of them went and ate an enormous quantity of very plain food sitting on the steps of a building whose purpose none of them knew.

Nobody talked much.

Afterward, somebody with a list showed them to a room with four cots in it and a door that closed, and Korroc took his armor off for the first time in a week, and set it against the wall, and slept eleven hours.

---

He woke in the grey before dawn, the way he always did.

For a while he lay and looked at the ceiling of a room in Citadel Drezen and let that be true. Then he got up, and washed, and dressed, and put the gold-and-mithral on, piece by piece, in the dark.

It still went on the way it had gone on the first time, in a demon's treasury with Thane telling him it was probably cursed. Every buckle true. The breastplate finding the barrel of his chest as though a master had measured him for it and then gone away and made it. He had worn borrowed harness before and he had worn his own, and this was neither. This was the third thing, the one he had no word for, and he had stopped asking about it out loud because he could hear how it sounded.

He went out through a fortress that was mostly asleep, and across the officer's court, and into the chapel of Torag.

Somebody had been at it.

That was the first thing he saw. Caleth had knelt in that wreck four days ago with a cantrip and a rag and cleaned the filth off the Father's carved feet until the stone stood clear, and since then somebody else — several somebodies, by the look — had carried on. The benches had been dragged back into rows. The worst of the muck was off the floor. The slashed tapestry that had once shown the raising of Drezen had been taken down off the wall and folded, which was the right thing to do with it, because you do not leave a ruined thing hanging where it can be looked at every day.

The defilement was still obvious. It would be obvious for a year. But somebody had made a start, and had not been asked to.

Korroc found a place on the floor in front of the statue and knelt down and prayed.

He did it the way he always did, which was out loud and not particularly formally, because Torag was the Father and you did not address the Father in a courtly voice. He thanked him for the banner. He thanked him for his cousin's soul, which he had personally smashed a crystal to get back and was still not entirely calm about. He asked, and did not expect an answer, for something to do about a dwarf whose hands had stopped working.

Then he stayed on his knees a while with nothing in particular going through him, which was, he had long thought, the actual point.

When he stood up there was a paladin of Iomedae watching him.

Young. Human. Bandage around one forearm, a sunburst on the surcoat, and the stiff careful posture of someone who has been standing there for a while wondering whether to speak.

Korroc squinted at him.

"Something?"

"Your armor, sir." A pause. "Rennick, sir. Second company."

"What of it?"

The young man pointed at Korroc's chest.

"Wasn't that Iomedae's mark? Yesterday, in the courtyard." He looked apologetic and did not stop. "I noticed it particularly. I remember thinking — begging your pardon — I remember thinking it didn't match. Torag on the shield and Herself on the breast. Sir."

Korroc looked down.

Etched deep in the gold over his heart, where a sunburst had been since the day he took the suit off a statue in a demon's treasury, were a hammer and an anvil.

The Father's own. Cut clean into the metal — not scratched over the old mark, not filed and re-struck, no ghost of a sunburst underneath. The gold simply had the hammer and anvil in it now, the way it had had the sunburst in it before, as though it had always been that and always would be and there had never been another question.

He stood there in a cold chapel and looked at his own chest.

And it came up out of him.

It came the way a name comes when you have been groping for it all morning and then stop trying — all at once, whole, with the whole room around it: a bench, and a low ceiling, and the smell of quench-oil and coal smoke. The shop where he had finished his apprenticeship, the summer he was twenty-two, and the old smith who ran it turning a piece of scrap over and over while he talked, because he could never talk without something in his hands.

*There's harness a man makes for a man, lad, and there's harness a man makes for a god, and then there's the third sort. Armor of the pious, they call it. Very old craft. You'd not make one and neither will I. Thing about it is it doesn't belong to a god at all — it belongs to whoever's inside it. Put a Sarenite in it and it goes Sarenrae's. Put a Shelynite in it and it puts up the bird. It reads the man and it takes his mark and it don't ask his leave first.* And then, with the scrap going over and over: *Which is a nice thought or a terrible one, depending how honest you are.*

Korroc had been twenty-two and had thought about girls all through the rest of it.

*Armor of the pious.*

It sat down into him like a stone into water. All of it — every piece of the past four days that had not fit, the thing he had been reaching for at the edge of his own mind since the treasury and could not lay hands on. The suit had not been made for him. Nobody had measured him. Nobody had known. There was no lost smith, no ancestor, no long chain of intention reaching seventy years down to put a dwarf in the right harness at the right hour.

It fit him because it had *decided to.*

He had stood in a demon's treasury and said *it feels right,* and Thane had told him it was probably cursed, and the truth was smaller and stranger than either: it had been reading him the entire time. Through a fight in a chapel and a fight in a map room and a fight in the dark with a thing that ate light. Sitting on his shoulders. Taking his measure.

And this morning, in the wreck of the Father's house, with his knees on the floor, it had made up its mind and said so.

Korroc put his gauntlet flat over the hammer and anvil on his own chest.

He did not weep. He had wept over a shield in a war camp when Iomedae changed the sigil on it, and he was not going to make a habit of it in front of a stranger. But he stood in that chapel with his hand over his heart for a good while, and there was a great deal going on behind his beard.

Because it was not the same as the shield.

The shield had been a gift. Iomedae had reached down and put Torag's mark on a borrowed thing so that a dwarf could carry it without a borrower's guilt, and Korroc had knelt in the mud and thanked both of them and meant every word. That was a goddess being courteous to a smith.

This was different. Nobody had given him anything. This suit had spent a full day looking at him with whatever it had instead of eyes, and had come to a conclusion, and the conclusion was *Torag's.* It had not been persuaded. It had not been asked. Nobody had hammered it.

*You can't make a thing be what it is,* he thought. *You can only get out of the way of it and let it say so.*

He turned that over, and did not like where it took him, and made himself go there anyway.

Because there was a dwarf in a cell across this courtyard with a Dark Smith's book in his belongings and a bone-claw wound in his back, and Korroc had spent four days trying to hammer him.

Offering. Arguing. Setting terms with a death in them. *This is your chance. Torag is waiting for you.* All of it honest, all of it meant — and all of it Korroc Stonevein putting a piece of stubborn iron on the anvil and going at it, because that was what he was and it was the only tool he had ever been any good with.

And the man had walked down a stair on his own that nobody made him walk down. And Korroc had not made that happen. He had not even been in the room.

"Sir?" said the young paladin, sounding alarmed. "Are you well?"

"Aye." Korroc took his hand off his chest. "Aye, lad. Better than I was."

He looked up at Torag's ruined statue for a moment, and at all the work still to be done on that floor and those walls, and then back at the young man with a sunburst on his surcoat standing in a dwarven god's chapel at dawn out of what appeared to be simple politeness.

"Well, Rennick." Korroc nodded at the filth still crusting the base of the north wall. "How are you with a bucket?"

---

Later that morning, they took the officer's court and turned it into a war room.

Irabeth had a table brought in and a map of Drezen put on it and stones set on the corners, and by the middle of the morning there were fifteen people around it and Korroc understood that he was one of them, which he had still not entirely got used to.

They gave the report. All four of them, in turn, and it took a long while, because there was a great deal of it — the roof and the watchtowers, the great court and the false Iomedae, the map room and Staunton Vhane, the nabasu barracks, the cells, the prison rows, the vampire, the ritual chamber, the hall of murdered gods, the black banner, the dark that ate light.

Irabeth wrote almost none of it down. She had a clerk for that. She listened with her arms folded and her jaw set, and asked perhaps six questions in an hour, and every one of them was the question Korroc would have asked.

Thane laid the ledger on the table when they got to Staunton's journal, and did not read from it, and let Caleth do the summarizing.

"Four elixirs," Thane said, when Caleth was done. "Vials of the crystal-stuff. Staunton took one of five. Four are unaccounted for."

"Not in the citadel?"

"Not that we've found." Thane's voice was perfectly even, which was how Korroc knew how much it was costing him. "We've been through the treasury, the map room, his quarters, the forge levels, and half the cells. Four vials of something that turned a traitor into a thing that nearly killed the four of us, and I can't tell you where they are."

Irabeth looked at the map for a while.

"Then that's a standing order," she said. "Every room. Every crate. Anything in glass gets brought to me and nobody drinks anything they find in this fortress, and I want that said out loud to every company by noon."

She straightened up.

"What else."

"The whispering," Korroc said.

"You said it stopped."

"It stopped." He put both fists on the edge of the table. "I'll not tell you it's finished, because I don't know what it was, and neither does he" — a thumb at Caleth — "and neither does he, and he's the one who'd know." A thumb at Thane. "It ran for hours under those levels and it didn't come from anywhere and it wasn't the ritual, and then it went away when the banner came up. That's everything I have. I'd not have you writing *resolved* next to it."

Irabeth held his eye a moment.

"Noted, and not resolved," she said, and the clerk wrote something down.

Then she said: "Tell me about the forge again."

Korroc had been waiting for that, and dreading it, and had not worked out why until she said it.

Caleth told her. He did it well, and fairly, and Korroc noted that he laid out the thing's nature without any argument attached — a forge of black and red metal at the bottom of a pit of heat that was not heat, which took holy things and made them unholy, and had been doing it for seventy years to every relic the crusade lost.

Irabeth's chin came up.

"We need to see if we can turn that."

"Turn it," Thane said.

"A forge of that power, Thane. If it can be cleansed, it's the most valuable thing in this fortress and it isn't close." She tapped the map twice. "We're going to be standing on the edge of the Worldwound for years. Do you know what it would be worth to have a place — one place — where the things this war ruins can be *unruined*?"

Korroc watched his cousin's face and saw the whole argument arrive there, and saw Thane look, once, across the table at Caleth.

Caleth looked back and said nothing at all.

Four days ago, in a room with Staunton Vhane cooling on the flagstones, the two of them had been six sentences into that fight when a tiefling's voice had come into Joran Vhane's head and ended it. *It will not lead to good things,* Thane had said. *It needs to be destroyed.* And Caleth had said *not if we can use it,* and neither of them had given a single inch, and then there had been a ritual to stop.

They had not gone back to it. Not once, in two days, through two fights and a vampire and a banner. Korroc had noticed that and had assumed the two of them were letting it cool.

He understood, watching them now, that they had both simply been waiting to see who would get to the argument first with somebody who outranked the other.

And Irabeth Tirabade had walked into it from outside and taken it away from both of them without ever knowing it was there.

Thane's jaw worked once.

"You're the commander," he said.

Which was not agreement, and everybody at that table who knew him heard it.

---

Aravashnial went down to look at the forge that afternoon, and Korroc went with him, and so did the other three.

The elf stood at the rail of that pit for a long time without saying anything at all. The heat came up off it the way it always did — the wrong heat, heat with an intention in it, heat that seemed to be *paying attention* — and Aravashnial leaned into it with his hands folded behind his back and his eyes wide open, and the zeal that had been on him since the Queen's cleric gave him back his sight was all over his face.

"Extraordinary," he said at last.

"That's a word for it," said Thane.

"Oh, it is a monstrous thing, I don't dispute it for a moment." Aravashnial did not look away from the pit. "It is also the single most sophisticated piece of applied transmutative theory I have ever stood in front of, and I have stood in front of a great deal. Do you see the channels? Not the obvious ones — *there*, under the lip. That's a cycle. That's a closed cycle. Something in this is not being consumed."

They spent three hours on it and learned a great deal and understood perhaps a third of it, and Korroc, who could tell within a hand's breadth where any furnace he had ever met wanted its air, could not tell where this one wanted anything.

Finally Aravashnial straightened up.

"We need to talk to Joran."

So they did.

Irabeth allowed it, on conditions, and they sat down with Joran Vhane in a cell with the door open and a crusader outside it, and asked him about the forge, and got the first genuinely surprising thing of the day.

He told them.

He told them for four hours. He told them the sequence and the timings and the way the heat ran and the thing about the channels that Aravashnial had guessed at and had guessed wrong. He told them what it did to good steel and what it did to bad; what it could not do; the three occasions it had failed and why he thought it had. He told it the way a craftsman tells another craftsman about a difficult tool, leaning forward, using his hands, getting irritated when he was interrupted, and about two hours in Korroc realized with a small unpleasant shock that this was the most alive he had ever seen the man.

*He's not confessing,* Korroc thought. *He's talking shop. Nobody's asked him about his work in seventy years except to tell him to do more of it.*

"There's a book," Joran said, at the end of it.

"What book?"

"About the forge. How it was made. How it's used. All of its secrets, so far as anyone wrote them down." He shrugged. "It's in Abyssal."

Which meant Aravashnial, or Caleth, or Joran, and no one else on that rock — which meant the three of them sitting at a table together for days, pouring over a book none of them would like touching.

Korroc sat in on a good deal of it. He was not much use. He read no Abyssal at all and the arguments were not about smithing, or not about smithing as any dwarf understood it — they were about *whether a bound thing can be unbound by the same road it was bound by, or only by a different one,* which the three of them went at for the better part of a whole afternoon while Korroc sat in the corner and sharpened things.

They were vicious about it, in the polite way scholars are. Caleth would say something reasonable, and Aravashnial would take it apart like a man taking apart a clock, and Joran Vhane — a prisoner, in a borrowed chair, with a guard at the door — would put two fingers on the page and say *no,* flatly, and be right.

It was, Korroc thought, the strangest room he had ever sat in.

On the fourth afternoon of it, they had a plan.

Aravashnial laid it out at the war table, and Korroc listened to all of it, and near the end of it he understood what was about to be said and set his jaw before it was.

"The cleansing wants a power source," the elf said. "A great one, sustained, over days rather than hours. And the source has to be — I will use an imprecise word — *articulate.* It has to be able to answer the thing's structure point for point, because the corruption in it is not a stain. It is a *design.* We are not washing a wall. We are unbuilding an argument."

"So," said Irabeth. "What do you need?"

"Two possibilities." Aravashnial held up a finger. "Consecration. The full rite, sustained by every cleric and paladin we can put in that pit for as long as they can stand it. It is the traditional answer and it is not a bad one." The second finger. "Or arcane. A structured working, layered, running continuously off my own reserves and Caleth's and every scroll and staff we can strip out of this fortress."

"And which is better?"

"The arcane," said Aravashnial, without a flicker. "It is not close."

Korroc heard himself say, "Why?"

He had not planned to. It came out with more in it than he intended, and every head at that table came around, and he had the sudden hot awareness of exactly how he looked: a dwarf in gold-and-mithral with Torag's hammer on his chest, asking why the holy way was the wrong way.

Aravashnial answered him seriously, which Korroc was grateful for afterward.

"Because holiness will *drive the evil out*," the elf said, "and it will do it beautifully, and it will leave the design intact. Consecration fights the thing that is living in the house. It does not rebuild the house. Whereas this—" he tapped the abyssal book — "this is a machine that was built wrong on purpose, in a language, by someone who knew what they were doing. It has to be argued with in its own grammar. You do not shout a bad weld into a good one, Korroc Stonevein. You cut it out and you lay it in again correctly."

And that, of course, was the sentence that ended it, because it was true and it was said in Korroc's own tongue.

He sat with it for a moment anyway.

The honest shape of his objection was not theological, and he knew it, and it shamed him a little to look at directly. It was this: that the forge at the bottom of that pit was, when everything else was stripped off it, *a forge.* That when this war was over — if such a word ever applied — the two people most likely to be standing in front of it with their sleeves rolled up were himself and his cousin. That he had spent a season in a lean-to behind the Defender's Heart drawing dents out of soldiers' plate with Thane at the other anvil, and had been happier than he had been in years, and had thought about that lean-to more often than he would admit while walking three hundred miles.

He wanted the Father's hands on it. He wanted, when he finally stood at that anvil, to be standing in a place that Torag had made clean.

And it would work less well.

Korroc Stonevein sat at a war table in a citadel his father had drawn on paper, and weighed a thing he wanted very much against a thing that was better, and did the arithmetic honestly, because he had been raised by a man who would have clouted him for doing it any other way.

"Right," he said. "Do it your way."

Aravashnial blinked. "I had rather expected—"

"Aye, well." Korroc pushed back from the table and stood. "I want it *redeemed.* I don't get to want it redeemed and also want it redeemed by me." He picked up his hammer. "If I'd wanted that I could have said so out loud and heard how it sounded. Go on. Tell me where to stand and what to hold."

Down the table, Thane snorted quietly into his cup, and did not look up, and Korroc knew that particular snort for what it was and took it as it was meant.

---

It took four days.

The pit was no place for a man in plate. Korroc went down into it twice on the first day, once carrying a case of scroll-tubes and once carrying Aravashnial's water, and came up both times with the sweat running off him and the wrong heat sitting on his skin like a hand, and after that they found him things to do at the top of the stair.

He hauled. He fetched. He held. He stood at the rail for hours at a stretch with nothing whatever to do, watching an elf and a half-elf and a dwarf in a prisoner's coat build something in the air out of chalk-marks and syllables, and understood perhaps one movement in twenty.

On the second day the pulsing stopped.

He felt it go before anyone below noticed. He was standing at the rail with a coil of rope over his shoulder and the wrongness of the place going through him the way it had gone through him for a week — the low chaotic *push* of it, evil coming off the metal down there the way heat comes off a stove — and then it thinned, and guttered, and was not there.

The heat stayed. The heat was only heat.

Korroc put both hands on the rail and let out a breath he seemed to have been holding since the cage.

"It's off," he called down. "The foulness. It's off it."

"That's the *surface*," Aravashnial called back, not turning around. "That's the least of it. Don't celebrate, fetch me the green case."

Korroc laughed out loud — genuinely, the first time in days — and went and fetched the green case.

The other two days were worse than the first two, because they were slower and nothing visible happened. Caleth went grey around the eyes. Aravashnial did not sleep at all on the third night and had to be shouted at. Joran Vhane worked with a guard three paces behind him the whole time and did not once look at the door.

And on the fourth evening it was finished.

Korroc came down the stair when they called him, and stood at the bottom of a pit that had tried to cook him for a week, and looked at the thing.

It was still black and red. That had not changed and, Aravashnial said, would not; the metal was the metal. But the shapes cut into it were not doing what they had been doing. The whole apparatus stood there in its cradle like a tool waiting on a bench — inert, patient, dumb — and when Korroc put his bare palm flat on the flank of it, which he did without asking anyone's permission, all it did was be warm.

He stood there with his hand on it for a long moment.

"Well?" said Caleth, hoarse.

"It's a forge," Korroc said.

He took his hand off.

"That's all. It's a forge."

Aravashnial said something then that Korroc did not entirely catch, and had to have repeated, and when he heard it he grunted.

"The *Purity* Forge?"

"It seemed apt." The elf was swaying gently with exhaustion and appeared very pleased with himself. "One should name a thing when one has changed what it is. Otherwise people go on calling it by the old name, and the old name is a kind of gravity."

Korroc thought about a hammer and an anvil cut into gold over his heart.

"Aye," he said slowly. "I'll not argue with that."

---

There were four days between the banner and the forge, and Korroc filled a great many of the hours in them with a bucket.

There was nothing else useful to do. The scholars did not need him for long stretches. So in the empty afternoons and in the evenings he went across the officer's court to Torag's chapel and worked on it, and it was slow and filthy and satisfying, and it took his whole mind, which was the point.

Some of it was scrubbing. Some of it was worse than scrubbing; there were things worked into the floor of that chapel that had to be chiseled out rather than washed, and one of them took him most of a day and he did not think about anything at all the entire time.

The paladins of Iomedae came and went. Rennick turned up on the second evening with two friends and a barrel of clean water and did not make anything of it.

And on the third evening, coming out into the court with the light going and his forearms black to the elbow, Korroc stopped.

Across the courtyard, the doors of the other chapel stood open — the shrine of Iomedae on the far side, which had been fouled the same way and worse, and which he had walked past a dozen times without going in because a man cannot clean two houses.

There was lantern-light in it. There were perhaps a dozen crusaders on their knees on the floor, and buckets, and a low murmur of somebody praying while they worked, and out through the doors and across the flagstones came the smell of lye and wet stone.

Korroc stood in the middle of a courtyard in a citadel that had been in demon hands for seventy years, with his own god's filth under his nails, and looked at that for a while.

Nobody had organized it. That was the thing. He had not asked and Irabeth had not ordered it, and as far as he could tell nobody had said a word about it to anybody. Two shrines on two sides of one court, and two sets of people on their knees in them at the same hour, each cleaning their own.

He thought about a courteous goddess putting a hammer and anvil on a borrowed shield.

He thought about a young man with a sunburst on his surcoat asking, politely, whether he was well.

*Aye,* he thought. *All right. That'll do.*

He went and got another bucket.

---

On the second morning, the paladins began going room to room.

It was slow, unglamorous, necessary work — a fortress that size does not empty just because its gates have fallen, and there were cultists in the drains and demons in the cisterns and one whole company of somebody's household troops holed up behind a barricade in a granary who had to be talked out and then, when talking failed, not talked out. It went on for the better part of a week.

Korroc went down with them on the second morning, and he went down for one reason.

He had not forgotten. Neither had Thane; Thane had raised it on the walk to the stair, in the flat voice of a man reading out a debt he had entered in his own book. Down in the prison rows, four levels below the courtyard, there were six cages.

They had walked past them on the way in. They had been on a timetable and a ritual had been starting and there had been nothing to do about it in that hour, and Korroc had told himself the word *later* and had known exactly what kind of word it was.

The six were still there.

They had been men. That was the part that had to be got past first and could not be got past at all — because they were still wearing what they had died in, and what they had died in was crusader kit. Not fresh. Twenty years old, some of it, thirty; a surcoat gone to grey rag with a sunburst you could only find if you knew where a sunburst went. Boots that somebody had once resoled carefully. One of them had a wedding band still on.

They came at the bars when the light arrived. All six. There was nothing left in any of them that knew what a bar was for.

Nageru went down with them.

He looked at the cages for a while, and then he said, "This will take some time," and began.

He did all six the way he had done the thing in the coffin chamber: in order, completely, and without hurrying. Stake first, driven true and centered. Then the axe — the adamantine one Caleth had thrown him in a treasury and he had never explained keeping — taken up two-handed for the head, one stroke each. Then the holy water, poured slow over the head and then over the body, all of it, until it stopped steaming and went on not steaming for a count of thirty.

Nobody hurried him. Two of the crusaders who had come down with them stood at the door of that row for the whole of it with their helms off.

Korroc knelt at each cage after Nageru had finished, and gave last rites over what was left, in old Dwarven and then in Common. He had no idea what god any of the six had followed. He said the words anyway, because a man is owed the words, and because the Father does not check a name at the door of a workshop that has burned down.

When they came back up into the light, Thane stopped at the top of the stair and looked back down it once.

"Later," he said, to nobody. "Aye."

They moved the prisoners into the citadel's own cells on the fifth day, out of the makeshift pens in the camp below, and it was Thane who noticed.

He came and found Korroc in the chapel, which was unusual by itself.

"Nurah's not in the prison."

Korroc sat back on his heels. "What?"

"They've moved everyone up from the camp. I went down and counted." Thane's face was doing nothing at all. "She's not there."

They went to Irabeth together.

She heard the question out, and then nodded once, and set down what she was holding.

"She tried to escape," she said. "Four nights ago. Sometime in the small hours — she got herself out of the irons, and out of the tent, and she'd got far enough to start a teleport." Irabeth's mouth pulled. "Aravashnial was close. He does that thing where he takes a teleport and turns it back on itself. She came down about twenty feet from where she'd started, in the middle of a camp full of people who knew exactly what she'd done."

She let out a breath.

"They called on her to surrender. She chose to fight. She was killed."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Korroc looked at his cousin.

Thane Stonevein stood with his thumbs in his belt and thought about it, and Korroc watched him think about it — the halfling who had put shadowblood in a prisoner cage as a distraction, who had slit a tent and gone for an old man's throat in the dark, whom Thane had caught by dropping water on empty air and had taken acid across the face for; whom Thane had led with both pommels because *you cannot question the dead.*

"She made her choice," Thane said.

And that was all he said, and he looked satisfied, and Korroc found that he could not fault him for it and did not much like the taste of it either.

---

Once the forge was clean, they let Joran Vhane work.

It was Korroc's request, and Irabeth granted it with terms attached, and the terms were the obvious ones: a guard on the stair, no unsupervised hours, and either Korroc or Thane in the room at all times.

Thane volunteered for that duty on the first morning before Korroc could ask him to.

"You don't have to," Korroc said.

"I know it."

"Thane—"

"He's a Vhane, he's been down there seventy years, and he knows that forge better than the elf does." His cousin was checking the edge of his father's knife with his thumb, unhurried, not looking up. "Somebody who isn't fond of him should be standing there. That's all. You do your bit and I'll do mine."

Which was, Korroc reflected, the closest thing to a blessing he was going to get.

So they went down, the three of them, into the pit.

And on that first morning Korroc did a thing he had been thinking about since a chapel at dawn.

There was a rack of tools by the cradle. Old ones, good ones, hung the way a careful man hangs tools. Korroc took a cross-peen off the rack — a fine piece, a little light for his own hand, worn to a shine at the grip by decades of somebody's fingers — and turned around and held it out, haft first, to Joran Vhane.

Joran looked at it.

He did not take it.

"I know," Korroc said. "The other thing's gone. Your hands don't do what they did."

The old dwarf's eyes came up, and there was something in them that was not anger and was not hope and had no name Korroc could find.

"They don't."

"Aye." Korroc did not lower the hammer. "This isn't that. This is a hammer. This does what it does whether the Dark Smith's looking or not, and whether the Father's looking or not, and it'll do the same in your hand as in mine."

He waited.

"I'm not going to tell you what you are," Korroc said. "I've been at that four days and I've had a think about it and I was wrong to. I can't hammer a man into a shape. Nobody can. That's not what we do — a smith doesn't *make* the iron into steel, lad, he gets the heat right and the time right and then he waits, and if there's steel in it, it comes."

Down the pit, Thane had stopped pretending not to listen.

"So." Korroc pushed the haft another inch. "Take the hammer. Do the work. That's all I'm asking today, and it's the whole of what I'm asking."

The silence went on for a long time.

Then Joran Vhane reached out and took the cross-peen out of Korroc's hand, and turned it over once, and looked at the worn place on the grip where somebody's fingers had been for years.

He did not say thank you. He did not say anything.

He walked to the cradle and set his feet, and looked at the work, and began.

Korroc Stonevein leaned back against the warm flank of the Purity Forge and folded his arms and watched him.

Overhead, four levels up and past a hall of murdered gods, there was still a black banner hanging in an iron frame that nobody had got around to taking down. Somewhere in this fortress or out of it there were four vials of a thing made out of powdered Nahyndrian crystal, and no one in the Fifth Crusade could say where. Across the courtyard two shrines were being scrubbed by two sets of hands. And on a table in the war room lay a ledger in a traitor's writing with every relic in it and every name in it but two.

The hammer came down.

It rang — clean, correct, the note a good tool makes when it lands where it was aimed — and the sound went up out of the pit and away into the stone of Citadel Drezen, and the stone took it, and kept it.
