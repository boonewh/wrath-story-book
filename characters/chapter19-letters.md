Drezen.

The news reached Nerosyan with the reports from the south. Drezen has been retaken. The citadel is in crusader hands. The Queen's banners fly over the walls again.

You have heard my stories about Drezen all your life, but I don't think I ever managed to make you understand what that city was to us.
It was home.
Not a fortress. Not a battlefield. Not the ruin you grew up hearing about.
Home.
Thane's mother and I knew those streets long before either of you existed. We knew which baker opened first in the morning, which tavern watered its ale, which streets flooded when it rained, and which forge would still be burning long after every other shop had closed.
Then your fathers came.
Drezen became their second home.
They helped build the citadel you are standing in now. Somewhere in those walls is stone they shaped and set with their own hands.
That was where we met them.
That was where four lives became tangled together.
Sometimes I wonder whether you and Thane realize that if there had never been a Drezen, there might never have been either of you.
We built lives there.
Then we lost them.
When Drezen fell, there was no time for proper goodbyes. We took what we could carry and left everything else behind. For years afterward I told myself we had evacuated.
Eventually I stopped using that word.
You evacuate when you expect to return.
Seventy-five years is a very long time to be away from home.
Of the four of us, I am the only one left to see this day.
I wish they were here.
I wish Thane's mother could know that her son is walking those streets.
I wish your fathers could see the citadel standing again.
Especially now that their sons helped take it back.
They would have been insufferably proud of you both.
As am I.
There are already stories reaching Nerosyan about the battle. Each telling adds more demons and fewer crusaders, so I have stopped trying to decide what is true.
But your name reached me.
So did Thane's.
Knowing the two of you, I am afraid to ask how much of those stories I should believe.
There is something I want you to do for me.
When you have time, leave the citadel and walk through Drezen.
Don't go looking for anything important.
Just walk.
Look at the streets. Listen for forge hammers. Find somewhere that the old stone still stands and put your hand against it.
Tell Drezen that Dagna remembers her.
Tell her some of us survived.
Tell her the others were never forgotten.
Tell her that the sons of the men who helped build her walls have finally come home.
And then you and Thane make certain you survive long enough for me to see you again.
There has been enough Stonevein blood given to that war.
I will not willingly give it yours.
I love you, my son.
I am proud of you.
And someday, when the Queen no longer has need of me here, I intend to come south.
After seventy-five years, I think it is time I went home.
Mother

Brother Nageru,
Word has reached the Sunken Fist that Drezen has been reclaimed.
Reports are still incomplete, and those that have reached us disagree on nearly every detail. On one matter, however, they appear consistent.
You were there.
Master Borin and I knew when you left us that your path would eventually take you into the Worldwound. I do not believe either of us expected it would carry you so quickly to Drezen.
You have accomplished something worthy of pride.
Remember that pride in an accomplishment is not the same as pride in oneself.
There is always another step.
That brings me to the reason for this letter.
There was once a fane dedicated to Irori in the lands near Drezen.
It was never a large temple. The records describe a small and isolated place, maintained by brothers and sisters devoted primarily to contemplation, discipline, and the pursuit of perfection.
When Drezen fell, all contact with the fane ceased.
No member of the order returned.
No reliable account of what happened there ever reached us.
For seventy-five years, its fate has remained unknown.
Until now, there was little reason to seek an answer. The region was firmly in the hands of the Abyss, and risking lives merely to confirm the destruction of an abandoned temple would have served no purpose.
Drezen is now held by the crusade.
And one of Irori's own stands within its walls.
So I have a task for you, Brother Nageru.
When your duties allow it, find the fane.
I do not know what you will discover.
Seventy-five years is a long time.
Perhaps nothing remains.
If it was destroyed, learn what you can of its fate.
If it was defiled, determine whether it can be cleansed.
If the remains of our brothers and sisters are still there, give them whatever rites and dignity circumstances allow.
And if something of the fane has survived, determine what should be done with it.
There is one matter I hesitate to include.
Among the oldest records concerning the fane is a passage that does not appear in later accounts. I have read it many times since word arrived that you were at Drezen.
It suggests the fane was established not merely as a place of worship or contemplation.
Those who established it were waiting for something.
Or perhaps someone.
The surviving text is unclear.
Do not allow those words to shape what you expect to find.
Expectation is a poor substitute for observation.
Go there with an open mind.
See what remains.
Understand it.
Then decide what must be done.
You have spent years preparing yourself to make such decisions without a teacher standing beside you.
It appears the time has come to discover whether we taught you well.
Walk with discipline, Brother Nageru.
Sister Lyra
Order of Irori
